A B S T R A C T
Outdoor comfort and climate adaptation studies usually focus on thermal-only investigations, typically relying 
on straightforward heat stress metrics. Human-centered validation frameworks to confirm whether built envi­
ronment interventions reduce actual physiological stress versus predicted discomfort from population-averaged 
models still lack. To fill this gap, this proof-of-concept addresses three questions: (1) Do multi-domain envi­
ronmental models explain physiological variance that thermal indices alone cannot capture? (2) Do individuals 
exhibit heterogeneous resilience to identical landscapes? (3) How can individual physiological data aggregate 
into neighborhood-scale resilience zones? Using synchronized wearable sensors (heart rate, electrodermal ac­
tivity) and multi-domain environmental monitoring (Universal Thermal Climate Index, Particulate Matter, CO₂, 
Acoustic Spectrum), we tested eight participants through contrasting Roman urban typologies such as exposed 
piazzas, tree-canopied riverbanks, enclosed gardens. Thermal comfort indices alone failed to predict stress, yet 
multi-domain models integrating thermal, air quality, and acoustic parameters explained up to 43% of physio­
logical variance. Individuals with similar characteristics (age 28–38) and lifestyle exhibited markedly different 
physiological responses to identical microclimates, suggesting that the city is not felt the same way by everyone, 
and that population-averaged vulnerability models may not reliably predict individual-level stress outcomes. We 
operationalized three resilience metrics: Toughness, Recovery Capacity, Adaptation Duration, derived through 
generalized logistic growth modeling capturing nonlinear threshold dynamics in physiological stress responses. 
K-means clustering identified five statistically distinct adaptation zones corresponding to landscape typologies, 
demonstrating that individual wearable data enables neighborhood-scale resilience mapping. This framework 
establishes a preliminary methodological infrastructure for integrating physiological sensing with urban digital 
twins, enabling validation of whether landscape configurations reduce stress for inhabitants rather than relying 
on demographic prediction alone.

Recent advances in wearable sensor integration with urban IoT

students' skin conductance during math tasks under varying classroom 
microclimates (temperature, CO2, PM). Higher CO2 and temperatures 
correlated with increased EDA features like Conductance Level (CL), 
indicating stress; machine learning confirmed temperature and air 
quality's role in physiological well-being and thermal comfort [12].

scenario-level comparisons rather than quantifying individual-specific 
resilience profiles across multiple environmental domains. Second, 
although emerging frameworks integrate multi-domain urban features 
including visual, acoustic, and microclimatic variables to predict 
neighborhood-scale comfort indices using explainable AI [19]), these 
approaches aggregate population-level perceptual data rather than 
measuring individual physiological stress responses that determine 
actual health risk. Third, smartwatch-based interventions have demon­
strated behavioral nudging potential for thermal and acoustic comfort

2. Methodology
This proof-of-concept framework examines individual physiological 
resilience to multi-domain environmental stressors through synchro­
nized wearable sensing and hyperlocal monitoring during structured 
urban transect walks. Data collected during these walks were analyzed 
through four stages (Fig. 1): (1) correlation analysis quantifying re­
lationships between physiological indicators (heart rate, EDA) and 
environmental parameters (thermal, acoustic, air quality domains) to 
establish baseline associations and inter-individual variability; (2) 
generalized logistic growth modeling fitted to physiological time-series 
under single-domain and multi-domain (thermal + acoustic + air 
quality) configurations to compare explanatory power; (3) trans­
formation of model parameters into three resilience metrics: Toughness 
(resistance to stressors), Recovery Capacity (speed of baseline return), 
and Adaptation Duration (stabilization period), operationalizing 
Schweiker's resilience framework [26]; and (4) K-means clustering on 
temporally-weighted, recovery-coupled environmental features to

across distinct urban microclimates.
The one-hour lasting comfort walk was performed in Rome, Italy, 
with a hot-summer Mediterranean climate (Csa) according to the 
K¨oppen climate classification [33] between July 1st and 2nd of 2024. 
The " comfort walk" protocol [34,35] instructed participants to maintain 
their natural, self-selected walking pace typical of urban pedestrian 
movement, without running or prolonged standing. All participants 
completed the one-hour route covering approximately 2.5 km under 
researcher supervision to ensure comparable pacing. The starting and 
ending points of the transect are represented in Fig. 2. Point B, located in 
St. Peter’s Square, Vatican City, is an open, highly exposed environment 
with minimal vegetation, with LST = 43.9 ◦C and UAQI=135.37, while 
Point A, situated at Ponte Cestio across the Tiber River, features a denser 
and taller tree canopy, offering greater thermal buffering with LST=37.9 
◦C and UAQI=126.40 on average during the previous week before the 
experimental campaign. In the midpoint of the route participants 
crossed a garden fenced with brick walls of 4 m and dense vegetation 
(Fig. 2, Point C) and a ‘transitional space’ (Fig. 2, Point D) which in the 
case of this experiment was a ‘loggia: a room or gallery with one or more 
open sides, especially one that forms part of a house and has one side open to 
the garden’ [36] to account for similar architectural typologies that are 
often present in the case study area.
The study participants comprised university students and academic 
personnel aged 28–38 years, representing diverse international back­
grounds. This sample was selected to ensure a relatively homogeneous 
sample, excluding individuals from recognized vulnerable populations 
[37]. The narrow age range (28–38) and similar educational/occupa­
tional backgrounds minimize confounding from major demographic 
factors, though residual variability from unmeasured covariates (sleep 
quality, caffeine intake, recent stress exposure, menstrual cycle phase) 
remains a limitation of within-subject models.
Each session followed four phases. First, pre-walk preparation (~15 
min) included informed consent, wristband equipment, and walking 
instructions. Second, stationary assessment periods (5–10 min) occurred 
at Points A, B, C, D where participants paused within 5 m of the envi­
ronmental backpack, acclimatized for 15 min, then completed ques­
tionnaires via their smartphones. Third, walk segments consisted of 
natural-pace walking with the researcher carrying the environmental 
backpack maintaining ≤2 m proximity and continuous monitoring. 
Fourth, session completion involved final questionnaires. Bidirectional 
design enabled one pair to walk A→C→D→B while another walked 
B→C→D→A simultaneously. The two-day experimental period was 
designed as a proof-of-concept validation study to test whether multi- 
domain models predict physiological responses better than thermal 
indices alone, and whether physically similar individuals exhibit het­
erogeneous resilience profiles. The short timeframe enabled controlled 
comparison across contrasting urban typologies under stable meteoro­
logical conditions, minimizing confounding variables for initial frame­
work validation.
2.1.2. Environmental measures
The wearable monitoring system was designed and developed at the 
University of Perugia as a backpack to ensure ergonomic comfort while 
allowing comprehensive environmental data collection on a hyperlocal 
microclimate scale [31]. The backpacks housed a modular array of 
sensors, including a BME280 for air temperature (accuracy: ±1 ◦C), 
relative humidity (±3 %), and atmospheric pressure (±0.25 %), a CV7-V 
ultrasonic anemometer for wind velocity and direction, and an 
upward-looking (sp-510-SS ) pyranometer for global solar radiation (±5 
%). It also featured a TDS0037 NDIR CO2 sensor and a PMS5003 
laser-scattering particulate matter (PM) sensor for PM1.0, PM2.5, and 
PM10. A NEO-M8 GPS unit was integrated to provide high-resolution 
spatial mapping of the collected data. The sensors were protected with 
custom 3D-printed ventilated enclosures, except for those requiring 
direct exposure, ensuring accurate measurements. The control unit, 
powered by a rechargeable battery with over two hours of autonomy, 
wirelessly transmitted real-time data of 5 s frequency to a user-friendly 
dashboard accessible via Wi-Fi, making the system ideal for monitoring 
dynamic urban environments. In addition to the above, two micro­
phones were used to account for basic acoustic recordings such as 
relative sound level and frequency. Zoom H1n compact stereo recorder 
featuring a unidirectional condenser microphone was used in a 90◦XY 
stereo format, ensuring directional sound capture with reduced side 
noise.
2.1.3. Subjective measures
Questionnaires administered at four urban settings (Points A, B, C, D) 
captured subjective comfort across thermal, air quality, and acoustic 
domains (Supplementary Materials, Table S1) Given limited sample size, 
subjective data served as secondary validation for physiological obser­
vations rather than primary analytical endpoints. Perceived adaptive 
capacity was assessed through standardized inquiry: "In case you would 
experience exhaustion from extreme climate conditions, how accessible 
would you find suitable environments for thermal relief?" (5-point Likert 
scale: not at all (0) to fully accessible (+5)). This metric quantified 
participants' perception of available thermal refuge options, tested for 
correlation with measured physiological stress responses, and provided 
insight into psychological modulation of physiological adaptation 
(perceived control).
2.2. Data processing
Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) calculations were per­
formed using the wearable sensor data combining air temperature, 
relative humidity, solar radiation and wind speed and processed in Py­
thon via the ‘pythermalcomfort’ library [38] with a metabolic rate of 1.3 
met (light walking activity) and clothing insulation of 0.36 clo (standard 
for shorts and t-shirt attire) [39].
The relative sound level in dBFS was calculated using the root mean 
square (RMS) of the audio signal, which measures its effective amplitude 
[40]. This value was then converted into a logarithmic scale relative to 
the maximum possible amplitude of the digital audio. A higher RMS 
value results in a dBFS level closer to zero, indicating a louder sound, 
while lower RMS values produce more negative dBFS levels, repre­
senting quieter sounds. This approach provides a standardized way to 
assess and compare sound intensity in digital audio analysis. To enhance 
the reliability of the analysis, data preprocessing includes outlier 
removal and signal smoothing. Outliers are identified using the Inter­
quartile Range (IQR) method, where values falling below Q1 −
1.5 ×
IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR are considered extreme and excluded. This 
procedure prevents anomalous values from skewing the analysis, 
ensuring a more accurate representation of the underlying trends. Next, 
time-series data is smoothed using a low-pass Butterworth filter to 
reduce high-frequency noise, with a carefully chosen cutoff frequency 
relative to the Nyquist frequency to preserve relevant trends [41]. This 
filtering is crucial for accurate relevant peak detection and subsequent

Initial correlation analysis quantified bivariate relationships be­
tween physiological indicators (BPM, EDA) and environmental param­
eters across thermal, acoustic, and air quality domains. Pearson 
correlation assessed linear relationships for normally distributed vari­
ables, while Spearman rank correlation captured monotonic associa­
tions for non-normal distributions [43]. This exploratory analysis

generalized logistic growth modeling, consistent with similar 
individual-level studies reporting r = 0.15–0.20 as meaningful [44].

individual-level physiological data due to high inter-individual vari­

(Section 2.4) and clustering analysis (Section 2.5) represent the primary 
analytical approaches where relationship strength is properly quantified 
through R² and validated through statistical testing.
2.4. Logistic growth model for resilience dynamics
By combining signal processing techniques, peak detection, and 
nonlinear modelng, this study analyzes the adaptive physiological re­
sponses of each participant to urban environmental stressors. The 
generalized logistic growth function was selected to operationalize 
Schweiker's theoretical resilience framework [26] through three key 
advantages over alternative approaches. We employed the generalized 
logistic growth function [45,46] because it captures nonlinear threshold 
dynamics in physiological adaptation while providing mechanistically 
interpretable parameters (B = sensitivity, M = threshold, ν = recovery 
modulation) that can be extended to multi-domain stressor interactions 
unlike linear models (Fig. 3). Critically, the model enables multi-domain 
extension that preserves individual parameter interpretation while 
revealing interaction effects, allowing quantification of how thermal 
stress modulates air quality sensitivity, or how acoustic load influences 
thermal adaptation capacity, through coupled environmental terms 
rather than forcing additive assumptions. This mathematical frame­
work, originally developed by Richards in 1959 to model plant growth 
rates and later widely applied in ecological modelling [46], operation­
alizes parameters that can be derived from empirical physiological time 
series, compared across individuals to quantify inter-personal resilience 
differences, and aggregated to neighborhood scales to map spatial pat­
terns in collective adaptation capacity. Specifically, with particular 
emphasis on how fluctuations in air temperature, air quality, and noise 
levels affect BPM and EDA [47,48], the human response to increases or 
decreases in environmental stress can be modeled to reveal how multi­
ple stressors simultaneously affect distinct physiological systems such as 
thermoregulation and autonomic stress response, which interact syner­
gistically rather than independently.

and physiological responses, a generalized logistic growth function as 
mentioned by Schweiker [26] is employed. This model captures the

These resilience metrics (Table 1) provide mechanistic links between 
environmental design and physiological outcomes, operationalizing 
Schweiker's [26] three-phase resilience framework. Toughness (T =
1/B) quantifies resistance to initial environmental stress, describing how 
slowly physiological responses escalate as stressors increase. Higher 
toughness values indicate individuals who maintain baseline function 
despite rising thermal stress requiring fewer immediate interventions. 
However, excessive toughness may delay recognition of accumulating 
strain, particularly in vulnerable populations who may report thermal 
conditions as acceptable despite associated health risks [51]. Recovery 
Capacity (RC = ν/B) measures the speed of return to homeostatic 
equilibrium after peak stress exposure ends. This metric directly informs 
urban cooling infrastructure design: populations with low recovery ca­
pacity require more frequent spatial access to thermal refuges, as they 
cannot efficiently restore physiological equilibrium between exposures. 
Adaptation Duration (AC = t₉₅ % - tpeak) quantifies the time required for 
physiological stabilization after stress peaks, representing what 
Schweiker describes as the "ability to cope" phase. This metric estab­
lishes minimum exposure thresholds for intervention effectiveness. If 
cool spots or green spaces provide relief periods shorter than an in­
dividual's adaptation duration, the physiological system cannot com­
plete its recovery process, leaving residual strain that accumulates 
across repeated exposures. Critically, these person-specific metrics 
enable identification of low-resilience individuals who may be missed by 
demographic vulnerability indices, while simultaneously aggregating to 
neighborhood scales for spatial mapping of collective adaptive capacity 
[52].
Single-domain models (5 parameters: A, K, B, M, ν) tested seven 
environmental predictors independently: UTCI, CO₂, average PM, 
overall sound level (dBFS), and three frequency-specific acoustic mea­
sures (low-frequency: <500 Hz representing traffic and mechanical 
noise; mid-frequency: 500–2000 Hz capturing human voices and urban 
activity; high-frequency: >2000 Hz indicating birds, wind, and high- 
pitched mechanical sounds). Multi-domain models (8 parameters: A, 
K, B₁, B₂, M₁, M₂, Q, ν) tested eleven pairwise combinations: UTCI paired 
with CO₂, PM, and each acoustic frequency band; CO₂ paired with each 
acoustic frequency band; and PM paired with each acoustic frequency 
band. Each configuration was fitted separately for BPM and EDA across 
all participants, with only models achieving R² > 0.20 reported in 
Tables 4–5.
Generalized logistic growth models were fitted as within-participant 
models to account for repeated measurements collected during each 
individual's transect walk (multiple time points per participant across 
varying environmental exposures). Models were then fitted indepen­
dently for each participant using nonlinear least squares optimization. 
This participant-specific approach enabled individual heterogeneity 
quantification as required by the theoretical framework, while the small 
sample size (n = 8) precluded reliable mixed-effects modeling across 
participants. Model performance was assessed through adjusted R² ac­

rameters) and multi-domain (8 parameters) configurations. Leave-one- 
participant-out (LOPO) validation ensured findings were not driven by 
outliers. We recalculated R² values and resilience metrics after 
systematically excluding each participant, with stability assessed 
through the range of LOPO mean predictions (<0.15 considered robust) 
[53]. Coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated for full sample and 
each LOPO iteration to confirm heterogeneity (CV>50%) persisted 
across participant subsets. R² values represent within-participant model 
fit, not population-level variance. This individual-specific approach was

with replacement, refitting K-means models, and calculating the per­
centage of iterations each observation was assigned to its modal cluster. 
Post-clustering, the resilience index, a normalized indicator of the 
adaptation metrics calculated (Section 2.4) and the average physiolog­
ical stress deriving from HR and EDA data entries were compared across 
zones using one-way ANOVA. Zones were then ranked by mean resil­
ience to represent a gradient from optimal adaptive environments to 
low-capacity, personalized stress-prone zones.

3. Results
The design of the transect route was subsequently validated using 
subjective comfort vote data. Point A was characterized as warm and 
humid based on thermal sensation metrics and was perceived as slightly 
to extremely uncomfortable in terms of thermal and air quality condi­
tions by 87.5% of participants. In contrast, Point B exhibited similar 
warm and humid conditions but was reported as thermally more un­
comfortable by 62.5% of participants. During each session, thermal 
conditions were monitored through UTCI readings, ranging from 24.84 
± 0.88 ◦C to 30.20 ± 0.67 ◦C. Physiological responses were tracked via 
heart rate (BPM: 86.93 ± 13.24 to 100.61 ± 16.64 bpm) and EDA (2.82 
± 1.04 to 47.08 ± 17.91 µS). Environmental quality parameters 
included PM measurements at three levels (PM1.0: 4.25–13.70 μg/m³, 
PM2.5: 7.02–16.69 μg/m³, PM10: 6.91–17.28 μg/m³), carbon dioxide 
levels (354.39–438.31 ppm), and solar radiation via pyranometer 
readings (0.04–1704.91 W/m²). Acoustic environment was character­
ized through sound level measurements (−59.37 to −61.36 dBFS) and 
frequency analysis across low, mid, and high ranges. Local microclimate 
conditions comprised temperature (25.46–30.04 ◦C) and relative hu­
midity (49.17–52.18 %RH) readings.
3.1. Preliminary correlation analysis
Correlation analysis was conducted for each participant individually, 
examining relationships between target variables (UTCI, BPM, and EDA) 
and environmental parameters across all domains (thermal, acoustic, air 
quality), one physiological parameter (skin temperature), and one sub­
jective measure (perceived adaptation access). The analysis demon­
strated varying degrees of association with environmental factors, 
suggesting predominantly individualized response patterns with a few 
consistent correlations observed across multiple participants (Fig. 4). 
Specifically, as expected, UTCI exhibited the most consistent relation­
ship with air temperature, with correlation coefficients ranging from r =
0.53 to r = 0.98 across all participants. However, other environmental 
parameters demonstrated notable variability in their associations. Hu­
midity exhibited inconsistent correlations, ranging from moderately 
negative (r = −0.49) to slightly positive (r = 0.22), suggesting that 
contextual factors may modulate its influence on UTCI. Particularly 
noteworthy were the divergent correlations with air quality parameters. 
While some participants UTCI showed strong positive correlations with 
PM (e.g., Participant 3: r ≈0.60–0.66), others demonstrated moderate 
negative correlations (e.g., Participant 4: r ≈−0.31 to −0.35). BPM 
correlations revealed marked individual differences in physiological 
responses to environmental stimuli.
Relative sound levels demonstrated reliably negative associations 
with BPM (r = −0.28 to −0.45). Moreover, the relationship between air 
quality metrics and heart rate varied substantially between participants, 
with both positive and negative correlations observed. EDA demon­
strated the highest degree of inter-individual variability among the 
measured physiological parameters. Temperature correlations ranged 
from moderately positive (r = 0.42) to negative (r = −0.29). Humidity 
showed relatively consistent correlations with EDA. Particulate matter 
correlations were inconsistent, indicating that air quality may not have a 
uniform influence on electrodermal activity across individuals. A 
consistent negative correlation emerges between adaptation access and 
various physiological and environmental variables. Specifically, partic­
ipants who perceived easier access to spaces for heat stress relief, 
assessed at different points of interest (A, B, C, D), tended to exhibit 
lower BPM. The strongest negative correlations are observed with BPM, 
averaging −0.44, with this effect being most pronounced in 4 out of 8 
participants (Fig. 4). This trend suggests that improved adaptation ac­
cess may help reduce physiological responses, as indicated by negative 
correlations with BPM (−0.29 to −0.44) and EDA (−0.44 to −0.20). 
These physiological changes likely reflect both direct environmental 
effects and significant psychological components, including higher 
perceived levels of control and reduced perceived heat risk when 
adaptation options such as accessible heat shelters are available [54].

3.3. Multi-domain approach
Taking the same reference participant as an example for our multi- 
domain model (Table 3), when UTCI is paired with CO₂ concentration, 
BPM response shows relatively high toughness (0.294) but zero recovery 
capacity, suggesting that while this combination resists initial changes 
in heart rate, it severely compromises the system's ability to recover. 
This pairing represents a form of multi-domain overload where the 
thermoregulatory system maintains initial stability but cannot adapt 
over time. In contrast, when UTCI interacts with average low frequency 
sounds, we observe a much lower toughness (0.098) but significantly 
higher recovery capacity (1.498), indicating greater physiological 
sensitivity but enhanced resilience over time. The combination of CO₂ 
with acoustic stressors reveals also interesting patterns. When CO₂ is 
paired with high-frequency sounds, the model predicts moderate 
toughness (0.099) and high recovery capacity (1.469), suggesting that 
this multi-domain interaction initially challenges physiological stability 
but allows for robust recovery. However, with mid-frequency sounds, 
the same CO₂ levels yield a lower recovery capacity (0.278), indicating 
frequency-specific physiological responses that would be missed in 
single-domain analyses. Particularly noteworthy is the average PM 
interaction with high-frequency sounds, which shows the highest 
toughness (0.259) and recovery capacity (3.348) values among all 
combinations. This finding indicates that this pairing creates a more 
resilient physiological response compared to other environmental 
combinations, potentially indicating an adaptive combined effect 
unique to this pairing.

PM–sound interactions produce the most resilient profile overall (T =

3.5. Multi-domain collective resilience
The following section presents the results of this multi-domain 
assessment, highlighting patterns and differences in physiological re­
sponses among all the participants aiming to summarize the resilience 
metrics collectively. Findings reveal that the combination of PM con­
centration with high frequency sounds shows the most consistent re­
lationships to BPM (present in all 8/8 participants, R² = 0.220) 
(Table 4), while the pairing of UTCI with PM produces the highest 
average R² (0.400 across 4/8 participants). These relationships 
demonstrate varying resilience patterns: the UTCI-PM domain pairing 
shows relatively low toughness (0.016) but measurable recovery ca­
pacity (0.066), while CO₂ paired with high frequency sounds shows 
more moderate toughness (0.057) and higher recovery capacity (0.369) 
with an R² of 0.323 observed in 6/8 participants. This finding suggests 
that environmental variables influence human physiological responses 
through different mechanisms, with thermal domain (UTCI) playing a 
central moderating role in the most significant relationships.
While BPM demonstrates robust relationships with multiple envi­
ronmental domains, particularly when UTCI interacts with PM (R² =
0.400) and when CO₂ interacts with sound frequencies (R² = 0.323 for 
high frequency), EDA exhibits even stronger associations (Table 5), 
especially with thermal-air quality domains. For EDA, the UTCI-PM 
pairing exhibits the highest average R² (0.434 across 5/8 participants) 
with moderate toughness (0.055) and recovery capacity (0.168). Simi­
larly, the UTCI-CO₂ combination shows meaningful correlations (R² =
0.328) with comparable resilience metrics (toughness = 0.050, recovery 
capacity = 0.145). This differential response pattern suggests a hierar­
chical physiological adaptation mechanism in outdoor environments, 
where EDA appears particularly attuned to thermal and air quality pa­
rameters, showing higher correlation strengths than cardiovascular re­
sponses for these specific domain pairs.
Notably, both physiological measures (BPM and EDA) show 
consistent relationships with acoustic stressors across all participants, 
though with varying strengths. The sound frequency pairings with CO₂ 
and PM demonstrate universal significance across all participants for 
EDA (8/8 participants for all frequency ranges), suggesting that these 
environmental combinations reliably trigger electrodermal responses 
regardless of individual differences. The PM-low frequency sound 
combination shows particularly high recovery capacity for EDA (0.894), 
indicating robust adaptation potential for this specific environmental 
pairing. This dual-measure approach reveals complementary aspects of 
human physiological adaptation to multi-domain environmental 
stressors, with cardiovascular responses (BPM) showing strongest cor­
relations to UTCI-PM interactions, while electrodermal responses (EDA) 
demonstrate stronger associations with both thermal-air quality and 
thermal-acoustic domain pairs. These findings highlight the importance 
of considering integrated multi-domain environmental quality assess­
ments aligned with recent studies supported by crowdsourced data on 
human perception.
Leave-one-participant-out (LOPO) validation confirmed robustness 
of these multi-domain relationships (Supplementary Table S2). Multi- 
domain superiority persisted across all iterations. For heart rate re­
sponses, five of seven domain pairs (71 %) showed LOPO stability <0.15 
(meaning the predictive strength remained consistent when each 
participant was systematically excluded, considered robust for this 
study), with PM-acoustic combinations demonstrating particularly 
robust findings (stability: 0.03–0.06) and CO₂-acoustic pairings showing 
consistent predictive power (stability: 0.11–0.12). EDA demonstrated 
higher between-participant variability, with UTCI-PM (stability: 0.18) 
and UTCI-CO₂ (stability: 0.19) showing moderate variability, yet both 
EDA domain pairs still substantially outperformed single-domain ther­
mal models.

4. Discussion
4.1. Multi-Domain environmental interactions override single-stressor 
predictions
Vulnerability is not a static spatial pattern: it varies across in­
dividuals, across time of day, and across sensory combinations, a fact 
that aggregate indices obscure and that only person-scale physiological 
monitoring can recover. Our findings confirm the multi-domain frame­
work's explanatory superiority established in the results. This challenges 
Attention Restoration Theory (ART) and Stress Recovery Theory (SRT), 
which traditionally emphasize visual nature exposure as the primary 
restoration mechanism. Our results demonstrate that UTCI functions as a 
moderator of other stressors rather than an independent driver, 
revealing multi-domain synergies absent from classical single-domain 
restoration frameworks. Temperature-PM interactions showed the 
strongest correlations (BPM: R²=0.40; EDA: R²=0.43), while CO₂- 
acoustic frequency pairings demonstrated universal electrodermal re­
sponses across all participants. These compound effects extend beyond 
visual-natural restoration pathways emphasized in traditional theory 
[21]. PM-acoustic pairings succeeded where UTCI alone failed, indi­
cating cross-domain interactions, not singular thermal or visual drivers, 
determine adaptive outcomes. These findings partially align with sen­
sory and cognitive load theories suggesting that urban overload from 
noise, density, and pollution does elevate stress through autonomic 
activation. But the patterns observed go beyond that: EDA responded 
more strongly to thermal-air quality interactions than cardiovascular 
measures did, suggesting that different physiological systems handle 
different environmental loads, and these loads interact synergistically 
rather than additively. Optimal resilience emerged from balanced 
multi-domain conditions (Zone 1: CO₂=426 ppm, UTCI=30.1 ◦C with 
low-to-mid frequency acoustic dominance and minimal high-frequency 
intrusion) rather than maximizing single factors like vegetation extent 
or minimizing thermal exposure alone. Tree-canopied areas with poor 
circulation showed elevated discomfort undermining recovery despite 
visual-natural qualities [56], demonstrating that greenery alone is not 
sufficient to ensure improved physiological outcomes. This contributes 
to explaining mixed evidence in existing density and greenery research 
[23]further framing that previous studies capturing single-domain ef­
fects (visual greenery, thermal relief) may have missed the compound 
thermal-air quality-acoustic interactions that actually determine physi­
ological outcomes, leading to inconsistent restoration findings across 
contexts. Hong and Jeon [7] showed that pleasantness and visual quality 
together contributed similarly to perceived environmental harmony 
(standardized path coefficients of 0.37 and 0.34 respectively), yet 
climate adaptation planning continues to optimize what sensors can 
measure remotely rather than validating what bodies experience locally. 
Our results confirm that landscape-mediated comfort emerges from in­
tegrated thermal-air quality-acoustic conditions shaped by vegetation 
placement, spatial configuration, and material selection, not tempera­
ture reduction, greenery extent, or density alone [11,57].
4.2. Individual heterogeneity
In this pilot sample, individuals with similar observable character­
istics (age 28–38, healthy adults, similar educational backgrounds) 
exhibited markedly different physiological responses to identical mi­
croclimates. Kruskal-Wallis tests suggest highly significant inter- 
individual differences in both BPM and EDA across all 18,815 observa­
tions, with large effect sizes persisting within identical CO₂ exposure 
ranges (Section 3.5, Table S3), consistent with Wang et al. and their 
review on inter-individual thermal comfort variability [55]. The same 
temperature-PM combination producing high toughness (T = 0.294) but 
zero recovery in one participant generated low toughness (T = 0.010) 
with moderate recovery (RC=0.160) in another. To check whether 
findings depend on a single day's conditions, we stratified analyses by 
session (Table S4). The two days differed slightly. Day 2 was approxi­
mately 2.7 ◦C cooler with lower PM2.5 but CO₂ exposure was compa­
rable and both days fell within the same UTCI range. Physiological 
heterogeneity was statistically significant within each day indepen­
dently, confirming the finding is not an artifact of either day's specific 
conditions. While this small-sample observation cannot validate 
whether demographic categorization fails broadly, it demonstrates that 
at least within this homogeneous group, population-averaged assump­
tions did not predict individual stress responses, supporting the rationale 
for personalized physiological monitoring in larger validation studies. 
While GIS-based modeling remains essential for spatial patterns [16,17], 
it cannot predict individual health risk without wearable physiological 
monitoring. Perceived adaptive capacity showed consistent negative 
correlations with stress (BPM: r=−0.44), indicating that both environ­
mental conditions and psychological factors (perceived control, risk 
assessment) modulate adaptation.
4.3. Temporal dynamics and spatial aggregation for design validation
Extended adaptation durations demonstrate that complex multi- 
domain environments require substantial physiological stabilization 
time, with direct implications for thermal refuge design where brief 
exposure may provide insufficient adaptive benefit. PM-low frequency 
sound combinations showed particularly high recovery capacity (EDA: 
0.894), representing frequency-specific responses missed by single- 
domain assessment yet actionable for landscape interventions. The hi­
erarchical adaptation mechanism, cardiovascular responses showing 
broad sensitivity while electrodermal activity exhibits stronger thermal- 
air quality coupling, confirms that climate-resilient design requires 
orchestrating thermal, acoustic, and air quality interventions as inte­
grated systems. Spatial clustering identified five statistically distinct 
adaptation zones (ANOVA: F₅,₁₅₈=9.42, p < 0.001) corresponding to 
measurable urban typologies. Optimal zones (resilience=0.66±0.17) 
exhibited balanced air quality (CO₂≈426 ppm), moderate acoustics 
[58], and thermal stability (UTCI≈30 ◦C), while stress-prone zones (0.41 
±0.11) showed elevated CO₂ and broadband acoustic loads. This dem­
onstrates that aggregating multidimensional urban features enables 
neighborhood-scale comfort mapping identifying where physiological 
adaptation manifests versus where demographic models merely predict 
quality of life [59].
4.4. Limitations and future directions
This study presents three primary limitations. First, the small sample 
size (eight participants) establishes proof-of-concept but constrains 
generalizability. However, the observed physiological heterogeneity 
within this homogeneous group validates individual-level monitoring as 
necessary rather than reflecting inadequate statistical power. Unmea­
sured covariates (sleep quality, caffeine, stress levels, menstrual phase) 
may contribute to observed heterogeneity alongside inherent physio­
logical differences. Future work should incorporate ecological momen­
tary assessment to partition person-specific traits from contextual 
modulators. Second, the two-day experimental period limits assessment 
of temporal stability in resilience metrics. We cannot validate whether 
observed patterns persist across diurnal cycles or seasonal acclimatiza­
tion. However, the short timeframe achieves the primary objective: 
demonstrating that multi-domain models outperform single-domain 
indices in predicting acute physiological responses. Day-stratified ana­
lyses suggested that physiological heterogeneity was statistically sig­
nificant within each session independently (Section 3.5, Table S4), 
indicating the temporal limitation of this study applies to seasonal 
generalisability rather than to the internal validity of the core findings.

Despite these limitations, this study establishes that multi-domain 
monitoring predicts physiological stress better than thermal indices

magnitude and individual stability require longitudinal verification.
5. Conclusion
Do we all feel the city the same way? This study suggests we do not 
by establishing a methodological infrastructure for outdoor environ­
mental resilience assessment by integrating individual physiological 
sensing with spatial analysis to validate whether building and landscape 
interventions successfully reduce stress for actual occupants [60]. 
Despite sample and temporal constraints detailed in Section 4.4, this 
investigation demonstrates technical feasibility and addresses three 
foundational questions: 
1. Multi-Domain Superiority (RQ1): Multi-domain models combining 
thermal, acoustic, and air quality parameters explained up to 43% of 
physiological variance, while heat stress index (UTCI) alone pro­
duced no predictive power. This demonstrates that UTCI functions as 
a moderator of other stressors rather than an independent driver, 
fundamentally challenging single-domain building performance 
frameworks.
2. Individual Heterogeneity (RQ2): In this pilot sample, individuals 
with similar characteristics showed statistically significant differ­
ences in physiological stress responses, with effect sizes large enough 
to be detectable at this sample size. While n = 8 does not allow broad 
generalisation, the results demonstrate that meaningful physiolog­
ical heterogeneity exists even within a homogeneous group, sup­
porting the need for larger studies to validate whether personalised 
monitoring improves vulnerability assessment beyond population- 
averaged models.
3. Spatial Aggregation (RQ3): K-means clustering identified five sta­
tistically distinct adaptation zones (p < 0.001) corresponding to 
measurable urban typologies, demonstrating that personalized 
wearable data can be aggregated into neighborhood-scale resilience 
maps. This enables evidence-based validation of where interventions 
improve physiological adaptation versus where demographic models 
merely predict vulnerability.
The three resilience metrics (Table 1) revealed extended physiolog­
ical stabilization periods, with direct implications for thermal refuge 
design where brief exposure may provide insufficient adaptive benefit. 
The hierarchical adaptation mechanism, for example cardiovascular 
responses showing broad sensitivity while EDA exhibits stronger 
thermal-air quality coupling, confirms that climate-resilient building 
design requires coordinating multisensory interventions as integrated 
systems. Yet current practice lacks methods to validate whether these 
strategies improve resilience for actual inhabitants versus theoretical 
models.
This framework provides a methodological pathway for closing the 
validation loop between building intervention and individual response. 
The three-resilience metrics: Toughness, Recovery Capacity, and 
Adaptation Duration, translate individual measurements into actionable 
planning parameters that complement demographic indices and GIS 
modeling, enabling empirical assessment of where population- 
calibrated design assumptions fail and which landscape configurations 
produce measurable stress reduction for actual inhabitants. By inte­
grating physiological sensing with spatial analysis, this proof-of-concept 
framework demonstrates how individual-level measurements can vali­
date whether landscape interventions produce measurable stress 
reduction at neighborhood scale. This approach addresses a critical gap 
in current practice: while building science optimizes thermal perfor­
mance and urban planning maps demographic vulnerability, neither 
validates whether implemented designs actually reduce stress for people 
inhabiting those spaces [61]. Human physiological responses provide 
the missing validation layer connecting building-scale performance to 
neighborhood-scale climate outcomes.
Future scaling of this methodology and the integration of validated 
physiological stress models with physics-informed digital twins that 
combine building energy simulation and urban microclimate modeling 
would enable pre-deployment validation: testing whether coordinated 
building innovations (material assemblages, transitional space config­
urations, cooperative energy management) aggregate to measurable 
pedestrian comfort improvements before construction begins. This re­
quires scaling from proof-of-concept validation (n = 8) to population- 
level inference, from two-day acute response to seasonal acclimatiza­
tion monitoring, and resolution of technical barriers regarding sensor 
network scalability, real-time data processing infrastructure, and pri­
vacy governance frameworks [62]. Applications of the validated 
proof-of-concept framework include green infrastructure placement 
strategies informed by measured recovery capacity, thermal refuge 
network design calibrated to adaptation duration requirements, and 
evidence-based evaluation of whether densification interventions 
maintain or improve physiological resilience. The fundamental question 
remains: will climate adaptation continue relying on predicted comfort 
from population-averaged models, or begin validating interventions 
through measured physiological outcomes of actual inhabitants expe­
riencing urban space?
