A B S T R A C T
Background: Given the increasing elderly population in the future, attention to the health of older adults is 
important. Environmental factors, including ambient temperature, sound type, and noise level, greatly affect the 
health of older adults. This study looks at how these elements influence the heart rate of elderly individuals.
Method: This study involves laboratory research with 216 elderly male participants from Ilam province in Iran 
over four months, from May to August 2024. Participants were exposed to different ambient temperature settings 
(20 ◦C, 25 ◦C, and 30 ◦C) and four noise levels (55, 65, 75, and 85 dB(A)) using sound types like music, traffic, 
and crowded noise. They were randomly divided into three equal groups of 72 by ambient temperature and then 
into subgroups of 24 based on the three sound type; finally, these subgroups were tested under four different 
noise levels, with each group consisting of 6 participants. The data from participants were analyzed using a three- 
way ANOVA model.
Results: The findings showed that ambient temperature, sound type, and noise level greatly affected older adults' 
heart rate, with strong statistical significance. There was a notable interaction between ambient temperature and 
noise level. However, other interactions did not show significant relationships with heart rate.
Conclusion: The study highlights important impacts on the health of elderly in noisy urban areas. It shows that 
long exposure to high noise and heat increases heart rate. This research emphasizes noise control and heat 
management to protect older adults and informs public health policies and urban planning.
1. Introduction
Population aging is a significant demographic challenge and a key 
driver of social change in the 21st century, with its effects expected to 
increase in the coming years (Rowland, 2012). The growing elderly 
population, especially “older adults,” is recognized globally as a critical 
issue that is placing increasing pressure on health services, social care, 
housing, and the economy (Christensen et al., 2009). Population aging is 
characterized by an increase in the number and proportion of in­
dividuals aged 60 years and older, along with a decline in the number 
and percentage of those aged 15 years and younger (Ismail et al., 2021). 
Population aging is occurring in both developed and developing coun­
tries, although it is most rapid in newly industrialized and developing 
regions (Noroozian., 2012). Many scholars argue that population aging 
has emerged as a significant demographic phenomenon that warrants 
careful consideration (Cristea et al., 2020).
The rising aging population in Iran is increasing more quickly than 
the global average. Iran has the second fastest-growing older population 
in the world, with a significant rise in the percentage of people aged over 
60, projected to reach 14. 8 % by 2030 and 29. 4 % by 2050 (Noroozian., 
2012). The aging population in Iran is growing at a rate that exceeds the 
global average. According to data from the Statistical Center of Iran, 
looking at the elderly population of Iran (65 years and older), it can be 
observed that the elderly population of Iran has increased from about 
1.6 % of the total population in 2016 to 7.8 % in 2024. Also, based on the 
center's forecasts, over 10.4 million people will be added to the popu­
lation of Iran over the 20 years from 2016 to 2036, among which the 
elderly population (65 years and older) will increase by about 5.5 
million people or equivalently 11.5 % (Statistical Centre of Iran, 2024).
To improve the health and well-being of older adults in Iran, 
collaboration between the government, private sector, civil society, and 
the elderly is essential. Establishing geriatric assessment units and 
comprehensive care programs is recommended, along with increasing 
the number of geriatric specialists. It is important to focus on the

distinctive needs of older adults and make necessary policy adjustments, 
allocate resources, and enhance healthcare services specific to this de­
mographic (Piroozi et al., 2024). Policymakers and healthcare managers 
are encouraged to invest in legislative measures, prevention strategies, 
and resource allocation that effectively respond to the needs of the 
elderly. By fostering partnerships within the community and enhancing 
the current health infrastructure, the adaptability and responsiveness of 
healthcare systems can be improved (Samouei et al., 2024). The global 
increase in life expectancy emphasizes the importance of understanding 
the factors that influence the well-being of older adults (Crimmins and 
Zhang, 2019). Age is widely recognized as a critical factor in thermal 
comfort perception (Wu et al., 2023; Jiao et al., 2023). Due to disrup­
tions in body ambient temperature regulation (Baquero and Forcada, 
2022; Inoue et al., 1991), older adults do not respond to hot and cold 
environments as effectively as younger individuals (Al-Absi et al., 2022; 
Zhu et al., 2018). As ambient temperatures rise, older adults often 
experience an increase in heart rate (Zhu et al., 2018). A variety of 
policies have been implemented to enhance the quality of life for older 
adults in urban areas, with the auditory environment being a significant 
factor affecting overall comfort (Baquero Larriva and Higueras García, 
2023).
Noise, defined as unwanted sound, poses potential risks to health and 
communication (Ismaila and Odusote., 2014). Noise-induced stress 
negatively impacts human performance (Simpson et al., 1974), and is 
associated with neurological and psychological complaints (Baquero 
Larriva and Higueras García, 2023). It is recognized as one of the four 
major pollutants that severely threaten human physical and mental 
health. Factors such as construction, traffic, and crowd noise create 
fluctuating sound pressure levels due to their random occurrence (Guan 
et al., 2020). Consequently, exposure to traffic noise can influence heart 
rate variability (Huang et al., 2013), and crowd noise is linked to 
increased heart rates (El Aarbaoui and Chaix, 2020).
Individuals over 65 years old are at a greater risk for heart disease 
due to noise exposure compared to younger individuals (Vienneau et al., 
2015). Therefore, noise serves as an environmental factor that elevates 
heart rate (Schnell et al., 2013). Heart rate is one of the most important 
indicators of cardiovascular health and holds special significance for the 
elderly. There are substantial knowledge gaps between the biological 
aging hallmarks and aging-related diseases in humans (Zhao et al., 
2024). As age increases, physiological changes occur in the cardiovas­
cular system that can have significant effects on the well-being of elderly 
individuals (Al-Tabtabai et al., 2025).
Monitoring heart rate in older adults can aid in the early diagnosis of 
issues such as arrhythmias, heart failure, or coronary artery diseases. 
Additionally, controlling heart rate and maintaining it within a normal 
range can help prevent serious complications such as heart attacks or 
strokes (Ndlovu and Chungag, 2024). This is especially important for the 
elderly, who are more at risk for these dangers. Research on trans­
portation noise as a risk factor for cardiovascular death was summarized 
in a 2021 meta-analysis (Cai et al., 2021). Learning stress management 
techniques and reducing heart rate during stressful times can contribute 
to the mental health improvement of the elderly. Understanding the 
impact of heat stress on the cardiovascular system in older adults un­
derscores the importance of developing adaptation strategies, in­
terventions and policies to protect this vulnerable population during 
extreme heat events (Liu et al., 2025). Overall, paying attention to heart 
rate and cardiovascular health in older adults not only helps to preserve 
their health but can also enhance their quality of life (Jakovljevic, 
2018). A collaborative approach involving various stakeholders, 
including researchers, is necessary to improve geriatric care. Sharing 
successful international care models can provide insights for better 
practices in Iran, specifically in Ilam province.
The present study aims to examine the influence of environmental 
factors, including ambient temperature, sound type, and noise level on 
the heart rate of elderly individuals. Given the aging population and the 
increased susceptibility of older adults to environmental factors, 
understanding how ambient temperature and noise affect their heart 
rate can lead to improved care measures. For the health and well-being 
of older adults, collaboration among the government, private sector, and 
civil society is crucial. Recommendations include establishing geriatric 
assessment units and enhancing healthcare services. Policymakers 
should invest in legislation, prevention strategies, and community 
partnerships to improve healthcare systems for the elderly.
2. Methods
2.1. Study participants
This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Islamic Azad 
University, Ilam Branch, with the identification code IR.IAU.ILAM. 
RES.1403.012. A total of 216 healthy elderly men, aged between 65 and 
75 years, with a normal body mass index, were selected for the study and 
agreed to participate. In this study, relative humidity was set at 50 %, 
and airspeed was <0.1 m per second. Three ambient temperature levels 
(20, 25 and 30 ◦C) corresponding to cool, neutral, and warm sensations 
were tested, ensuring that the conditions were stable for 10 h before the 
experiment. Additionally, four noise levels (55, 65, 75, and 85 dB 
denoted as dB(A)), reflecting typical exposure levels of noise throughout 
the day, and three types of sound (music, traffic, and crowded) were 
tested. The metabolic rate was approximately 1.0 MET, and the clothing 
insulation value was 0.6 Clo. For each participant, standard clothing 
with the same material and design was provided to ensure that the 
clothing coverage rate was consistent across individuals.
To assess the eligibility of individuals with a history of cardiovas­
cular diseases or sensory-motor and auditory disorders, we initially 
verified the qualifications of the older adults participating in this study 
through interviews and self-reporting. Participants were assured that 
their involvement in the research was entirely voluntary and were 
instructed not to include any identifying information on the question­
naire. The eligibility criteria for participants included: - Full consent to 
undergo the relevant tests - An age range of 65 to 75 years - Mental 
alertness - Cardiovascular health - Absence of auditory and sensory- 
motor disorders. Additionally, individuals were excluded from the 
study if they had issues with alertness or any auditory or sensory-motor 
disorders that could interfere with their experience in a thermal envi­
ronment. After confirming participants' eligibility, they were asked to 
stay at a designated location, free from noise, the day before the tests.
The experiment method in this study is as follows: the participants, 
who include 216 elderly men, were randomly divided into three equal 
groups of 72 people based on the environmental ambient temperature 
variable at three ambient temperature levels: 20 ◦C, 25 ◦C, and 30 ◦C. 
Then, each of the three groups mentioned above was also randomly 
divided into three equal groups of 24 people to measure the environ­
mental sound type variable (music, traffic, and crowd noise); finally, 
each of these 24-person groups was tested under four noise levels (55, 
65, 75, and 85 dB(A)). It is important to note that in this study, the heart 
rate of the participants was not measured before and after the experi­
ment because to examine the heart rate of the elderly participants, we 
first placed them at a neutral temperature of 25 ◦C for 20 min to control 
individual tensions and mental and psychological factors that might 
affect the results of the experiment, and for the elderly to reach thermal 
adaptation. In addition, their heart rate was not recorded in the pre-test 
phase and before entering the laboratory chamber to reduce the sensi­
tivity of the elderly to heart rate measurement under laboratory condi­
tions and minimize psychological effects on the experiment results. 
Further details on how the tests were conducted are provided below.

±0.3 degrees for three hours before the tests.
To assess the potential impact of noise on physiological parameters, 
participants measured their heart rate using a small desktop device 
(Omron, HEM-7320), which can measure rates from 40 to 180 beats per 
minute with an accuracy of ±5 %. Participants conducted the heart rate 
test themselves and then recorded the results on the questionnaire form. 
After a half-hour of thermal adaptation, participants were placed in one 
noise level environment for 15 min. It is crucial to present the samples to 
participants in a counterbalanced manner to avoid order effects. The 
sound was played in a randomized sequence during the experiment. 
Following this, the operating staff adjusted the noise level. The entire 
experimental process lasted 120 min, maintaining a constant thermal 
environment.
2.2.2. How to perform the test
This study investigated the combined effects of four noise levels (55, 
65, 75, and 85 dB(A)), ambient temperatures (20 ◦C, 25 ◦C, and 30 ◦C), 
and sound types (music, traffic, and crowded noise) on the heart rates of 
elderly men. A total of 216 healthy elderly men, aged 65 to 75 years, 
with a normal body mass index, were selected for the experiment. Par­
ticipants were instructed to abstain from caffeine and smoking for 24 h 
before the study. Additionally, their hearing was tested using an audi­
ometer to ensure none had hearing impairments. The 216 participants 
were initially divided into three main groups of 72 individuals each, 
with each group exposed to a specific type of sound: music, traffic, or 
crowded noise. Each of these main groups was further subdivided into 
three subgroups of 24 participants, each tested under different ambient 
temperature conditions (20 ◦C, 25 ◦C, and 30 ◦C). Within each ambient 
temperature condition, participants were exposed to four different noise 
levels (55, 65, 75, and 85 dB(A)).
To facilitate the experiments, each group of 24 was divided into two 
groups of 12, and then into smaller groups of 6. Each testing session 
involved 6 participants. Participants entered the chamber room. After a 
half-hour of thermal acclimation, they were exposed to a specific noise- 
level environment for 15 min. During this time, they completed ques­
tionnaires. Upon arrival, participants spent 5 min in room 1 changing 
into the provided standardized clothing. Next, in room 2, they waited for 
20 min in a pre-conditioning chamber maintained at 25 ◦C with 50 % 
relative humidity while wearing the experimental clothing to ensure 
uniform thermal conditions. During this time, the experiment and 
questionnaires were explained to them. Following that, in room 3, where 
the desired ambient temperature was set 10 h earlier, they are placed for 
15 min under the specified ambient temperature and noise levels. 
Finally, at the last step, which took 80 min to execute, participants 
experienced four 20-min intervals, during which they were exposed to 
different noise levels for 15 min, and 5 min were spent filling the 
questionnaires and recording individuals' heart rates. Fig. 2 shows the 
schedule of each test.
3. Results
Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26. The analysis of 
variance (ANOVA) was used in this study. The level of significance was 
fixed at P = 0.05. The mean and standard deviation of the variables for 
each group are displayed in (Table 1). The mean heart rate of elderly 
individuals varied based on ambient temperature levels, recorded at 
20 ◦C, 25 ◦C, and 30 ◦C, with values of 91.67, 69.65, and 80.63 beats per 
minute, respectively. The highest mean heart rate, averaging 91.50 
beats per minute, was observed in the crowd noise group at an ambient 
temperature of 30 ◦C and a noise level of 85 dB(A). In contrast, the 
lowest mean heart rate, recorded at 63.50 beats per minute, was asso­
ciated with the music sound type group at an ambient temperature of 
20 ◦C and a noise level of 55 dB(A). Results also showed a significant 
interaction effect between temperature and noise level on heart rate; p 
< 0.05. However, the interaction effects between temperature and 
sound type p > 0.05, sound type and noise level, and the three-way 
interaction between temperature, sound type, and noise level were not 
significant; p > 0.05. Also, Figs. 3–5 illustrate the two-way and simul­
taneous interaction effects of temperature, noise level, and sound type 
on the heart rate of elderly individuals under different temperature 
conditions, showing detectable significant effects. Of course, readers can 
refer to (Appendix A) for more information and to see the results of the 
analysis of variance of the heart rate of the elderly participants in this 
study.
One of the key assumptions in performing an analysis of variance 
(ANOVA) is that the error variance of the dependent variable is uniform 
across the study groups. In this case, Levine's statistic is 2.161. At the 1 % 
significance level, we do not have sufficient evidence to reject the 
assumption of equal variances among the groups, as p > 0.01. Conse­
quently, we can proceed with the analysis of variance to examine the 
differences between the study groups.
According to (Fig. 1), varying noise levels have different effects on 
heart rate at different ambient temperatures. Therefore, by controlling 
for the variable of sound type (specifically music), a two-way analysis of 
variance was performed only on ambient temperature and noise level. 
The results indicate significant effects for ambient temperature levels; p 
< 0.01 and sound type; p < 0.01 on heart rate. Additionally, there is a 
significant interaction effect between ambient temperature and noise 
level; p < 0.05. On the other hand, the results from Tukey's test indicate 
a significant difference in the mean heart rate among elderly individuals 
exposed to different noise levels. Specifically, there is a significant

In this study, we focused on investigating the impact of two varia­
bles—noise level and ambient temperature—on heart rate while keeping 
the “type of sound” constant. By limiting our analysis to a single type of 
sound each time, we aimed to determine whether noise level and 
ambient temperature significantly affect individuals' heart rates. The 
necessary analyses have been completed, providing detailed explana­
tions about the simultaneous interaction effects of three sound type­
s—music, traffic, and crowd noise—along with different levels of 
ambient temperature and noise levels on the heart rates of elderly in­
dividuals. This includes relevant charts to illustrate the findings.2
4. Discussion
The present study demonstrates that both ambient temperature and 
noise level significantly affect heart rate in elderly individuals. Elderly 
care is a crucial issue influenced by environmental factors such as 
ambient temperature, sound type, and noise level, garnering the atten­
tion of many researchers (Verderber et al., 2023; Kumar et al., 2023; 
Peralta et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Yan and Geng, 2024). Various 
studies have examined this topic and yielded differing conclusions. For 
instance, research conducted in China highlighted changes in heart rate 
among the elderly between 22 ◦C and 28 ◦C (Madaniyazi et al., 2016). 
Another study noted a significant increase in thermal discomfort at 30 ◦C 
when noise levels rose from 55 dB(A) to 85 dB(A); this increase in noise 
also correlated with a notable rise in heart rate (Guan et al., 2020). 
Furthermore, a study exploring the impact of noise levels on elderly 
heart rate found that heart rate increased even at relatively low noise 
levels of 65 dB(A) (Kraus et al., 2013). Research focusing on traffic noise 
has shown that higher noise levels (65.6 dB(A)) significantly affect heart 
rate in the elderly (Huang et al., 2013). The present study supports these 
findings, indicating that both noise level and ambient temperature 
markedly influence heart rate in elderly individuals. Consistent with 
previous research, our study highlights that an increase in noise level, 
particularly at 30 ◦C, leads to a rise in heart rate.
The results of this study indicate that as noise levels increase from 55 
dB(A) to 85 dB(A), heart rate also rises, particularly at higher ambient 
temperatures. For instance, at 20 ◦C, the heart rate increases from 65 
beats per minute to 70 beats per minute. At 25 ◦C, it varies between 65 
and 75 beats per minute. At 30 ◦C, it can reach between 80 and 85 beats 
per minute. Music tends to cause a lower heart rate compared to the 
other two types of sounds, even at higher noise levels (remaining below 
75 beats per minute at 85 dB(A)). In contrast, traffic and crowd noises 
lead to a significant increase in heart rate, especially at higher noise 
levels. For example, loud noises can raise the heart rate to 80 beats per 
minute at 85 dB(A). Higher ambient temperatures generally result in 
increased heart rate, with traffic and crowd noises showing more pro­
nounced effects compared to music. At 30 ◦C, traffic noise can increase 
heart rate from 85 beats per minute, while crowd noise can increase 
heart rate between 80 and 85 beats per minute. Thus, this study shows 
that high noise levels and ambient temperatures generally lead to an 
increase in heart rate in older adults. Traffic and crowd noises cause a 
significant increase in heart rate, particularly at 30 ◦C, whereas music 
tends to maintain a lower heart rate even at higher noise levels.
The findings of this study are consistent with a study by Meade et al. 
(2024a, 2024b), which explored the effects of ambient temperature on 
heart rate in older adults. This study demonstrated that higher ambient 
temperatures are linked to increased heart rate, particularly when 
ambient temperatures exceed 26 ◦C. Specifically, heart rates were found 
to rise at ambient temperatures of 31 ◦C and 36 ◦C, while no significant 
increase was noted at 26 ◦C compared to a control ambient temperature 
of 22 ◦C. These results indicate that prolonged exposure to elevated 
indoor ambient temperatures could elevate heart rates in older adults, 
raising concerns about cardiovascular health. Therefore, it is advisable 
to keep indoor ambient temperatures below 26 ◦C to help prevent heat- 
related complications and reduce mortality risk in this population. Also, 
the results of our research also align with the findings of the study by 
Meade et al. (2024a, 2024b), which indicates that exposure to high 
ambient temperatures (31 ◦C and 36 ◦C) leads to an increase in heart rate 
in older individuals.
The results of the current study are consistent with the findings of 
Ohlendorf et al. (2023), which explored the effects of musical sounds on 
the heart rates of elderly individuals. Their research indicated that the 
type of music had a minimal impact on older adults' heart rates, which 
tended to be more stable and analogized to those of younger adults. On 
average, the heart rates of older individuals were consistently lower 
than those of younger individuals across all sound conditions. Further­
more, the variability in heart rates among older adults was less than that 
observed in younger adults, suggesting that older individuals may be 
less sensitive to music and sound stimuli. Altogether, these findings 
indicate that listening to music does not significantly influence the in­
crease or decrease in heart rates among the elderly. Moreover, in 
another study conducted by Kraus et al. (2013), examining the effect of 
noise levels on the heart rate of the elderly, the results showed that even 
at relatively low noise levels (65 dB(A)), an increase in heart rate was 
observed, which is not consistent with the findings of our study. This 
discrepancy may be due to differences in the locations of the studies 
reviewed and also the duration for which the participants examined.
Previous studies have identified heart rate variations at different 
ambient temperatures and moderate noise levels (65 dB(A)), with traffic 
noise having a particularly strong effect (Huang et al., 2013; Guan et al., 
2020; Kraus et al., 2013; El Aarbaoui and Chaix, 2020; Cai et al., 2021). 
Specifically, our study shows that traffic and crowd noise are responsible 
for the greatest increase in heart rate, reaching 80 to 85 beats per minute 
at 85 dB(A), underscoring the significant role of environmental factors 
in elderly care. The present study demonstrates that both ambient 
temperature and noise level significantly affect heart rate in elderly 
individuals. Specifically, ambient temperature has a notable impact on 
heart rate, especially at 30 ◦C. Additionally, an increase in noise level 
from 55 dB(A) to 85 dB(A) corresponds with a rise in heart rate, 
particularly at 30 ◦C. Traffic and crowd noises lead to the most

5. Conclusion
This study examined the impact of ambient temperature, sound type, 
and noise level on the heart rate of 216 healthy elderly men. The ex­
periments were conducted at three ambient temperature levels (20, 25, 
and 30 ◦C) and included four noise levels (55, 65, 75, and 85 dB(A)), as 
well as three types of sound (music, traffic, and crowd noise). These 
findings suggest that not only the loudness but also the nature and un­
pleasantness of sound can be crucial in determining physiological re­
sponses. The results of this study hold significant implications for the 
health and well-being of the elderly, particularly for those living in 
urban environments with high levels of noise pollution. These findings 
indicate that prolonged exposure to high noise levels and elevated 
ambient temperatures increases heart rate, emphasizing the importance 
of implementing noise control measures and heat management strate­
gies to mitigate these risks. Overall, this research enhances our under­
standing of the complex relationships between the type of sound and 
noise level, ambient temperature, and heart rate in elderly individuals, 
highlighting the need to consider these factors in public health policies 
and urban planning.
