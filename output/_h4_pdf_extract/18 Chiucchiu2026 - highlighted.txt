A B S T R A C T
The built environment accounts for nearly one-third of global energy demand, underscoring the need to enhance 
building performance through strategies that integrate both technological solutions and human factors. Occu­
pants play a pivotal role in this process, as their comfort and well-being are shaped by complex interactions 
across multiple environmental domains. This study investigates cross-domain effects between thermal and 
acoustic environments, with a specific focus on how personality traits modulate perceptual responses. An 
experimental campaign was conducted in a controlled test room under two thermal conditions: a neutral phase 
(21 ◦C) and a mild discomfort phase (23 ◦C). In each phase, 25 participants evaluated seven soundscapes with 
distinct acoustic characteristics, ranging from natural to anthropogenic. Continuous monitoring of environmental 
parameters ensured accurate boundary conditions, while personality traits were assessed to capture inter- 
individual variability. Results show that even slight thermal variations influence acoustic perception, produc­
ing systematic negative shifts in the evaluation of the main soundscape’s descriptors across phases. Cluster 
analysis of personality traits identified two groups: one with higher emotional stability and openness, and 
another with stronger neurotic tendencies. These groups exhibited divergent perceptual patterns, with neurotic 
individuals reporting greater sensitivity to anthropic and chaotic soundscapes, and emotionally stable partici­
pants showing stronger appreciation for natural ones. Overall, the findings demonstrate that occupant perception 
cannot be understood through single-domain analyses alone. Integrating cross-domain interactions and 
personality-driven variability into adaptive comfort models and occupant-centric design strategies is essential to 
support buildings that are both energy-efficient and responsive to human diversity.
1. Introduction
Humans spend nearly 90 % of their lives indoors [1], where they are 
constantly exposed to multiple and simultaneous environmental stimuli, 
that influenced by the indoors activities, building use and uninterrupted 
exposure to environmental conditions over a 24-hour cycle. Human 
environmental perception is shaped by human senses that can be 
broadly categorized into two main groups: conscious (touch and taste) 
and unconscious (sight, hearing and smell). Unlike conscious senses, 
unconscious modalities cannot always be avoided at the onset of an 
experience, as they depend on automatic behavioural responses [2]. 
These stimuli, including thermal, acoustic, visual, and air quality envi­
ronmental domains, collectively contribute to the overall Indoor Envi­
ronmental Quality (IEQ) and strongly influence health and well-being 
[3,4], occupant satisfaction [5,6], and performance [7,8,3,4,9]. 
Improving IEQ can therefore enhance occupants’ comfort and mood 
while simultaneously reducing buildings’ energy consumption [10,11] 
and carbon footprint [12]. For instance, maintaining occupant comfort 
by running HVAC systems continuously is often energy inefficient, 
highlighting the need to balance energy efficiency with individual 
comfort through passive strategies such as natural ventilation.
Traditional comfort models, such as Fanger’s Predicted Mean Vote 
(PMV), have long guided building regulations and design standards by 
specifying fixed setpoints to be respected according to building type. 
However, these models not only deal with a single environmental 
domain at a time (such as the Fanger’s model just targeting thermal 
variables), but these also rely on average values and the “standard 
person” assumption, neglecting inter-individual variability. Moreover,

recent studies have highlighted the role of non-IEQ factors (e.g., work 
hours, contextual factor, and personality traits) on occupants’ well- 
being and performance [13–15], all variables that are typically neglec­
ted in common building design practices. Consequently, numerous 
studies report discrepancies between predicted comfort levels and oc­
cupants’ self-reported perception of IEQ [16,17]. This gap underscores 
the limitations of classical comfort models, which fail to account for 
individuals’ characteristics, such as psychological and personality fac­
tors, that are increasingly recognized as central to human perception 
[3].
In this framework, the theory of multi-domain comfort has recently 
emerged to contribute to face this complexity. By considering in­
teractions between environmental factors and leveraging technological 
advances such as IoT, big data, and machine learning [18], 
multi-domain approaches enable the development of personalized 
comfort models (PCMs) that could better reflect individual needs and 
perception in terms of perceived IEQ [8]. Nevertheless, research in this 
field remains fragmented, as reported by Schweiker et al. [19], with 
some studies reporting contradictory findings. For instance, whether 
acoustic stimuli significantly shape thermal perception. These in­
consistencies highlight the need for a deeper understanding of 
cross-domain effects and their interaction with human factors.
Beyond environmental variables, psychological and physiological 
characteristics are increasingly recognized as determinants of IEQ 
perception [20,21]. Most existing studies have examined correlations 
between objective physiological and psychological indicators and 
environmental parameters. Physiological variables (e.g., heart rate, skin 
temperature, electrodermal activity) are commonly integrated into the 
development of PCMs [22], whereas neurophysiological indicators (e.g., 
EEG), are often employed to also address the psychological dimension, 
to detect cognitive responses to environmental changes [23] and to 
assess individual’s environmental perception [24]. For instance, Pour­
ghorban et al. [25] review a wide range of studies that apply EEG 
analysis to evaluate human experience under different thermal condi­
tions. However, sensing systems required to detect brain activity pat­
terns by monitoring neurophysiological signals (i.e., EEG) are often 
invasive, limiting their practical use.
Current research predominantly examines the impact of IEQ on 
psychological restoration, mood and mental health, rather than 
exploring how individual psychological traits influence environmental 
perception. Studies have demonstrated the effects of each environ­
mental domain on psychological restoration, mood, and minimization of 
mental health problems [26–29], despite these stable individual char­
acteristics can help in explain systematic differences in how people 
perceive and evaluate their environment. Indeed, personality traits 
assessment offers a complementary perspective to neurophysiological 
monitoring, providing stable personal characteristic instead of 
moment-to-moment cognitive or psychological states. Moreover, per­
sonality traits assessment does not require invasive sensing technolo­
gies. The “Big Five” framework, based on five orthogonal dimensions (i. 
e., openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroti­
cism) [30], is widely adopted to investigate these differences. Extroverts 
and agreeable individuals report higher satisfaction in open-plan offices 
[31], whereas highly extroverted workers may also experience increased 
stress under certain IEQ conditions [32]. These findings underscore how 
personality modulates the individuals’ perception, adaptation and 
response to indoor environments.
Focusing on environmental exposure, the acoustics domain is 
particularly relevant, as sounds acts not only as a physical stimuli but 
also a potent psychological trigger. Stress-inducing auditory inputs can 
activate the autonomic nervous system, altering emotional state, 
including feelings of relaxation and stress, heart rate, and peripheral 
temperature [33,34]. Concerning indoor environments, while most 
research studies have focused on noise reduction in workplaces and 
learning environments, recent approaches emphasize perception-based 
acoustic design to enhance health, well-being, and overall quality of 
life within buildings [35]. Importantly, sound perception varies across 
individuals: the same auditory stimulus may induce relaxation in one 
person but stress in another, depending on personality profiles [36]. 
Thus, understanding these interactions may allow for more accurate 
predictions of behavioural, psychological, energy-related [37], and 
health outcomes in response to acoustic environments.
In this framework, research exploring the relationship between 
personality traits and individuals’ IEQ perception remains limited, and 
most existing studies focus primarily on understanding human behav­
iour under discomfort conditions [38]. Addressing these gaps is essential 
for developing PCMs that account for both environmental conditions 
and individual variability, ultimately supporting healthier, more adap­
tive, and energy-efficient indoor spaces. Understanding how personal 
characteristic [39], specifically personality traits, shape individual 
perception represents a crucial first step in this direction. Such insights 
could inform the development of PCMs that, in turn, could be imple­
mented by Building Management Systems (BMS) to operate adaptive 
building layers more effectively [40,41].
Based on the above considerations, the primary research question of 
this study is: Do personality-driven differences influence occupants’ 
perception of indoor conditions, particularly thermal and acoustic, 
within a multi-domain comfort framework? Accordingly, this study aims 
to investigate the cross-modal influence of acoustic and thermal comfort 
on occupants’ perception of the indoor environment, considering indi­
vidual personality traits. Specifically, it examines how different per­
sonality profiles shape occupants’ evaluation of environmental quality 
across multiple comfort domains. By identifying these perceptual pat­
terns, this research aims to provide useful insights for the development 
of personalized, occupant-centric strategies, that could be potentially 
implemented through BMS to enhance comfort, well-being, and long- 
term adaptive design. Furthermore, these findings may guide future 
research toward the creation of robust PCMs and evidence-based design 
strategies.
2. Method
An experiment procedure was designed and conducted within a test 
room to capture the interaction between thermal and acoustic domains 
and psychological traits. Subsequently, statistical analyses were con­
ducted to investigate potential cross-domain influences and variations in 
perceptual responses, with personality profiles identified through a 
clustering process based on trait assessments.

2.1. NEXT.ROOM
The NEXT.ROOM [42], a test room located in the Environmental 
Applied Physics Laboratory at the Engineering Campus of the University 
of Perugia (Italy, Cfa K¨oppen-Geiger climate classification [43]), is a 
dedicated facility for multi-domain, and highly controlled experiments. 
The thermal environment is regulated by two independent but com­
plementary systems: a radiant panel system and an HVAC system, which 
operate concurrently to ensure spatially uniform thermal conditions. 
The HVAC unit, which incorporates a Controlled Mechanical Ventilation 
(VMC) module, allows operation at multiple fan speed settings. These 
settings were systematically characterized in a previous study to map 
and quantify the background noise levels in several positions within the 
NEXT.ROOM.
In addition, NEXT.ROOM is equipped with an extensive sensing 
system, enabling continuous monitoring and acquisition of environ­
mental and physiological parameters throughout the entire duration of 
experimental sessions. The monitored variables and corresponding 
instrumentation are reported in Table 1. Since the NEXT.ROOM is not 
equipped with an integrated audio system, acoustic stimuli, during the 
experimental session, were provided through noise-cancelling head­
phones (model WH-1000XM4, by Sony).
2.2. Experimental procedure
The experimental campaign was conducted between October 2020 
and January 2021. The experimental procedure (Fig. 1) consisted of four 
consecutive phases, beginning with an acclimation period during which 
participants were introduced to the indoor environment [44] and 
equipped with the two wearable devices: a multi-sensor wristband and 
noise-cancelling headphones. In this acclimation phase, lasting at least 
20 min [45], the researcher outlined the study protocol using an infor­
mation sheet, obtained informed consent, and assisted participants in 
calibrating the noise-cancelling headphones. The EEG monitoring 
headband was used only in the earliest sessions, as the headphones 
consistently interfered with signal quality and caused considerable 
discomfort, leading to its discontinuation in subsequent sessions. To 
exclude potential auditory impairments that could bias the results, a 
hearing test was also administered. The multi-sensor wristband, 
throughout the experimental session, continuously recorded physio­
logical parameters, such as Electrodermal Activity (EDA), Heart Rate 
(HR) and Skin Temperature (ST), with a 1-minute sampling rate. The 
initial condition (C0) was carried out at a constant indoor temperature 
of 21 ◦C. Participants were invited to evaluate a total of seven different 
soundscapes and, subsequently, complete an environmental perception 
survey using a 5-point Likert scale. The questionnaire assessed perceived 
environmental quality, comfort and preference across environmental 
domains (i.e., thermal, visual, acoustic and air quality). this was fol­
lowed by a 30-minute transition phase, during which the indoor tem­
perature gradually increased to 23 ◦C, via the NEXT.ROOM mechanical 
ventilation system. During this phase, participants completed the Big 
Five personality questionnaire [46]. The final condition (C1) replicated 
the procedure of C0, with the setpoint temperature maintained at 23 ◦C. 
The experimental procedure and sequence of phases was identical for all 
participants, ensuring consistency across the experimental protocol.
Throughout the procedure, the participants remained alone in the 
NEXT.ROOM, seated beyond a N centrally positioned desk (Fig. 2). They 
were instructed to access an online platform via the provided laptop, 
which was used to administer both questionnaires and soundscapes. The 
soundscape order was randomized across phases and participants. 
However, the thermal sequence was identical for all participants, with 
phase C1 representing the higher temperature condition.
A total of seven soundscapes were submitted to the participants, each 
with distinct characteristics: (#1) Beach, (#2) Woodlands, (#3) Quiet 
street, (#4) Pedestrian zone, (#5) Park, (#6) Shopping mall and (#7) 
Busy street. Each recording had a duration of 30 s and was selected to

an individual’s personality traits, offering insights into behavioural 
tendencies and predispositions.
2.3. Participants
Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after 
they were fully briefed on the aims and potential risks of the experi­
mental protocol, which was approved by the University of Perugia’s 
Ethics Committee. Forty volunteers were initially recruited for the 
experimental campaign; however, due to the complexity pf the experi­
mental procedure and the relative data collection, 25 tests were 
considered as suitably of interest and reliable for the analysis. The final 
sample therefore consisted of 25 completed test sessions performed by 
25 volunteers, recruited at the University of Perugia, including students, 
PhDs and Post-Docs. The sample comprised 12 males and 13 females, 
with an average age of 27.4 ± 2.6 years (range: 22–33 years). In order to 
participate in experiment, individuals were required to meet specific 
criteria, including being in good health, having a Body Mass Index (BMI) 
within the normal range, and not using drugs or consuming excess 
alcohol. During the experimental session, participants were required to 
complete a low impact listening task (metabolic rate: 1.2 MET) during 
C0 and C1 phases at the provided workstation. Moreover, the partici­
pants were not asked to wear specific items but were instructed to 
maintain throughout the experimental session a clothing insulation 
equal or close to 1.0 clo, typical of the heating season, as indicated by the 
CBE Comfort Tool [47].
2.4. Data analysis
For the statistical analysis, a sample size of 25 participants was 
considered, and a significance level (α) was set at 0.05. Despite the two 
environmental conditions, the experimental procedure yielded a unique 
dataset, which had been assessed for normality using Shapiro – Wilk test. 
Most variables included in the analysis (11 out of 15), with the exception 
of the environmental parameters, exhibited non-normal distributions (p 
< 0.05).
To obtain objective comfort indicators, the Predicted Mean Vote 
(PMV) was calculated for both thermal conditions using the “comf” 
package developed by M. Schweiker [48]. This package implements 
established models and equations for human comfort assessment. PMV 
indices were computed based on temperature, relative humidity, and air 
velocity, assuming a metabolic rate of 1.2 MET, which represents a 
person performing office activities, and a clothing level of 1.0 clo, 
indicative of autumn/winter attire. The calculations were performed in 
R, following the package documentation. To allow direct comparison 
between objective (i.e., PMV index) and subjective (i.e., participants’ 
perception votes) comfort indicators, the five-point scale of the subjec­
tive data (−2 to +2) was linearly rescaled to match the seven-point PMV 
index scale (−3 to 3). This procedure preserved the relative distances 
between categories while aligning the ranges of the two measures for 
visualization purposes. Physiological signals (i.e., EDA, HR and ST) were 
also analysed to examine potential differences across the two thermal 
phases. Given the 1-minute sampling rate, the EDA signal was treated as 
tonic electrodermal activity (EDAt), reflecting slower, baseline varia­
tions in sympathetic arousal rather than rapid phasic responses [49,50]. 
For each participant, mean EDAt, HR and ST were calculated separately 
for C0 and C1 and compared to assess the activation of physiological 
regulation mechanisms associated with the thermal variation. These 
comparisons were tested for statistical significance using paired t-test, 
and effect size were quantified using Cohen’s d for paired samples, 
computed as the mean difference divided by the standard deviation of 
the paired differences.
Subsequently, given the non-normality of most variables, the 
Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were computed to explore 
potential associations between subjective perceptual data and person­
ality traits, with particular attention to each environmental domain 
perception and personality trait dimension. In addition, correlations

2.4.1. Clusterization
To identify common personality profiles, participants were grouped 
into clusters based on their personality trait assessments, aiming to 
capture potential patterns in perceptual responses. Each trait, measured 
at three levels (i.e. low, average or high) using the administered per­
sonality questionnaire, was considered equally in the clustering pro­
cedure. The K-means algorithm was applied to partition participants 
into cluster based on trait similarity. Each data point was assigned to the 
cluster whose centroid, the mean of points within that cluster, was 
nearest.
To determine the optimal number of clusters, the Silhouette score 
was computed. This metric evaluates clustering quality by considering 
cohesion within clusters and separation between clusters. The Silhouette 
score (i) ranges from −1 to 1, with values near 0 indicating points on the 
boundary between clusters. An average Silhouette across all points 
provides an overall measure of clustering quality, where higher values 
indicate more cohesive and well-separated cluster.
The validity of the silhouette-based solution was further corrobo­
rated using the Elbow method, a heuristic that examines the proportion 
of variance explained as a function of the number of clusters. The 
“elbow” point of the curve indicates the optimal cluster number.
Finally, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to 
visualize the resulting clusters in a two-dimensional representation. PCA 
reduces the dimensionality of the dataset while retaining the greatest 
variance, enabling a clear graphical representation. Component load­
ings indicate the contribution of each personality trait to the principal 
components, reflecting both the weight and direction of each variable in 
defining the reduced dimensions.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Overview of environmental data
During each test, environmental parameters from the four main 
domains were continuously monitored at 1-minute intervals. Noise 
level, illuminance and CO2 concentration remained substantially con­
stant and were analysed to provide a reliable characterization of the 
surrounding environmental conditions. Regarding the visual and 
acoustic domains, except during the evaluation of soundscapes, the 
variables were considered stable, in line with the measurements re­
ported by Vittori et al. [42] following the construction of NEXT.ROOM. 
Background noise was 39.7 dB in both phases C0 and C1, corresponding 
to a medium fan speed setting of the mechanical ventilation system, and 
increased to 58.1 dB during the transitional phase due to the higher fan 
speed required to raise the temperature. Illuminance levels were 474.2 
± 14.7 lx and 478.7 ± 5.3 lx in C0 and C1, respectively. Meanwhile, the 
CO2 concentrations were 540.2 ± 46.1 ppm and 496.0 ± 26.1 ppm in C0 
and C1, respectively.
The analysis focused on the thermal domain, characterized by tem­
perature and relative humidity. In terms of temperature, the operative 
temperature was computed as the arithmetic mean of air temperature 
and mean radiant temperature, consistent with established approaches 
for moderate environmental conditions.

3.2. Sample clusterization
A comparative analysis of the clustering metrics was performed to 
determine the appropriate number of clusters. Using the silhouette 
method, the two-cluster solution was identified as the most appropriate 
(highest coefficient = 0.26). A complementary evaluation with the 
elbow method suggested that two to four clusters could be suitable for 
the dataset, with inertia values of 85.5, 75.2 and 63.6 for 2, 3 and 4 
clusters, respectively. To minimize heterogeneity in terms of cluster size 
and gender distribution, a two-cluster solution was adopted. Conse­
quently, the sample segmentation was performed using the K-means 
machine learning algorithm, based on the five personality traits rated as 
low (−1), medium (0) or high (1). This resulted in a nearly even dis­
tribution: 15 participants in Cluster A (7 females and 8 males) and 10 in 
cluster B (6 females and 4 males).
Subsequently, using the Principal Component Analysis (PCA), the 
two-dimensional representation of the cluster was provided, as illus­
trated in Fig. 4.a. The first two Principal Components (PCs) described 
64.5 % of the variance in the original data, ensuring an adequate 
description of the total sample. PCA1 primarily reflected self-control and 
emotional stability, while PCA2 captures a dimension of extraversion 
and emotional instability, in contradistinction to conscientiousness. The 
component loadings for each personality trait on PCA1 and PCA2 are 
reported in Table 2.
The two clusters that were identified were given the designations 
Cluster A and Cluster B. More specifically, Cluster A has values of 1.33 
and 0.12 for PCA1 and PCA2, respectively. Conversely, Cluster B 
exhibited values of −1.05 and −0.10 for PCA1 and PCA2, respectively. 
This finding is further corroborated by the radar chart (Fig. 4.b), which 
demonstrates that Cluster A individuals exhibit higher levels of self- 
control, emotional stability, and openness to new experiences and so­
cial interactions. Conversely, Cluster B subjects exhibit pronounced 
tendencies towards neuroticism, emotional volatility and reduced 
conscientiousness.
3.3. Influence of personality traits on indoor environmental quality 
perception
Following the identification of two distinct clusters, the correlation 
between the five personality traits, assessed using the Big Five ques­
tionnaire, and participants’ ratings of thermal, visual, acoustic, and air 
quality sensations was examined. To capture changes over time, the 
difference between the ratings in the initial phase (C0) and those in the 
final phase (C1) was calculated, producing a delta value for each com­
fort domain. These delta values were subsequently correlated with each 
personality trait to uncover potential relationships.
The findings delineate distinct personality profiles across the two 
clusters, underscoring their relevant role in shaping environmental 
perception. As shown in Table 3, Cluster A is characterized by higher 
levels of self-control and emotional stability. Within this group, thermal 
perception was negatively associated with Agreeableness and positively 
associated with Openness. In addition, visual perception demonstrated a 
significant positive association with Neuroticism. This pattern suggests 
that, even among individuals who are generally characterized by 
emotional stability, visual perception remains meaningfully linked to 
neurotic traits, even when such traits are expressed at very low levels.
Cluster B, by contrast, is defined by higher emotional instability, 
reduced self-control, and a greater prevalence of Neuroticism. For this 
group, Neuroticism and Openness emerged as the most influential traits 
across the assessed comfort domains, with the largest deviations from 
zero. In contrast to the findings observed in Cluster A, which included a 
robust positive correlation between neurosis and visual perception, 
however, no specific results were evident in Cluster B. These findings, 
detailed in Table 4, emphasize the differential role of personality traits 
in shaping the multisensory perception of interior environments.
These correlation values are partially aligned with findings from 
previous studies by Marzaban et al. [31], Hartog et al. [51], and Kallio 
et al. [32], which identified extraversion, agreeableness, and openness 
to new experiences as the personality traits most strongly associated 
with individuals’ perceptions of their surrounding environment.

3.4.1. Thermal perception: the role of personality profiles
According to achieved changes in NEXT.ROOM thermal environ­
ment throughout the test, observed PMV was, on average, equal to 0.11 
± 0.23 and 0.32 ± 0.27 in phases C0 and C1, respectively. This 
discrepancy between the two phases, although minimal, is statistically 
significant, with a p-value lower than 0.05. Considering the physiolog­
ical response to environmental changes (i.e., +1.22 ◦C in temperature 
and −2.25 % in relative humidity from C0 to C1), the applied thermal 
variation can be considered as sufficient to stimulate physiological 
mechanisms. Previous studies have demonstrated positive correlations 
between temperature changes and fluctuations in physiological in­
dicators [52,53]. In the present study, skin temperature (ST) increased 
by 0.76 ◦C (p < 0.00, d = 1.9), whereas both tonic electrodermal activity 
(EDAt) and heart rate (HR) decreased, respectively −0.25 µS (p < 0.00, 
d = 0.9) and −3.17 bpm (p < 0.00, d = −2.0). These large effect sizes 
indicate robust physiological responses across participants. Conversely 
to the findings of Heal et al. [54], who reported a positive correlation 
between HR and temperature in children, with increase of 8.7 bpm to 
13.7 bpm per 1 ◦C raise, the present results showed a different cardio­
vascular response (i.e., decrease in bpm) under the temperature varia­
tions tested. This divergent pattern may be attributable to differences in 
participant age compared with those examined by Heal et al. [54], and, 
more importantly, to the fact that participants in the current study 
remained seated throughout the entire experimental protocol, likely 
contributing to a reduced metabolic rate.
In contrast, the analysis of participants’ subjective ratings in terms of 
thermal sensation, as illustrated in Fig. 5, reveals a different pattern 
when compared to the PMV index. In fact, the thermal sensation ratings 
remained mostly similar between the two configurations, with average 
values equal to 0.40 ± 0.79 and 0.20 ± 0.73 in C0 and C1, respectively. 
As shown in the box plot, the mean values of the two groups are almost 
identical, and the distribution is highly similar, with no statistically 
significant difference.
The subsequent division of the total sample into the two identified 
clusters revealed that the different personality profiles demonstrated 
varied responses to changes in thermal conditions. In general, cluster B 
reports a warmer sensation than cluster A, but the thermal sensation 
expressed by cluster B remains almost constant between the two phases. 
Concurrently, cluster A shows a neutral stance during the initial phase 
C0, with a perception progressing towards negative values (i.e., cold) in 
phase C1.
As demonstrated in Fig. 6, the disparate responses exhibited by the 
two clusters in phase C1 are found to be statistically significant, with a p- 
value that is less than 0.05. In line with the findings of Kallio et al. [32], 
who reported that more extroverted individuals are more sensitive to the 
environmental stimuli, Cluster A displayed a different mean thermal 
sensation compared to Cluster B.

variation in the evaluation of each soundscape descriptor was consid­

significant or not. As the Table 5 illustrates, greater variation values 
emerged for soundscapes #1 and #2, which, according to their desig­

Subsequently, the analysis focused on the soundscapes that were 
identified by the participants as being predominantly characterised by

’’Pleasant’’ and ’’Calm’’.
Here the effect of applying a temperature variation on the evaluation 
of the different soundscapes was analysed. No consideration was given 
to either contextual or personal variables. Consequently, the in­
dividuals’ personality characteristics were included in the investigation 
and, therefore, the two defined personality profiles. As demonstrated in 
the preceding section, acoustic perception is influenced by different 
personality traits in the two clusters. Indeed, subsequent to the seg­

the evaluations of the soundscapes in the two thermal phases exhibited

4. Conclusion
In light of the growing need to design interior spaces that ensure the 
occupants well-being while minimizing energy consumption, research 
on environmental comfort is increasingly shifting toward an individual- 
centred approach. Within this context, emerging studies focus on the 
development of personalised models aimed at predicting users’ per­
ceptions and satisfaction with the built environment [57]. It is well 
established that each individual exhibits diverse physiological, psycho­
logical and preferential responses, shaped by the interaction between 
environmental conditions, contextual factors, personal characteristics 
and past experiences [58]. These differences substantially influence 
both perception and behaviour, suggesting that unexpected or subopti­
mal occupant actions, often unaccounted for during the design or 
simulation phase, can lead to increased energy consumption. The find­
ings of this study may provide a basis for the formulation of strategies to 
mitigate discomfort caused by environmental factors through the 
implementation of post-occupancy strategies, such as Personal Envi­
ronmental Comfort Systems (PECS) to create more tailored and 
occupant-centric environments. Furthermore, considering the diverse 
stakeholders involved in the building sector, the insights gained here 
may inform the operation and coordination of different building adap­
tive layers. In particular, in open space offices or co-working spaces, 
understanding how personality traits influence occupants’ perception of 
IEQ can support the design of environments, enhancing both individual 
comfort and energy consumption.
In this context, the present study examined the cross-modal effects of 
two environmental domains – thermal and acoustic – and the moder­
ating role of personality characteristics on the perception of IEQ. This 
exploratory investigation constitutes a component of a more extensive 
research programme that is oriented towards the utilisation of the user 
as the pivotal focal point within the design and management processes 
of constructed environments.
To address these questions, an experiment was conducted in which 
environmental conditions were controlled, specifically through varia­
tions in temperature and exposure to soundscapes comprising natural 
and anthropogenic components. This methodology allowed the inves­
tigation of cross-domain interactions, specifically whether certain 
thermal conditions influence the perception of soundscapes, potentially 
the salience of noisier auditory stimuli. Conversely, the study also 
investigated whether certain soundscapes could attenuate the percep­
tion of temperature variations, potentially reducing the likelihood of 
occupant actions such as adjusting thermostats or opening windows.
Firstly, the analysis of environmental conditions confirmed that the 
applied temperature variation, although minimal, was sufficient to 
generate a statistically significant difference in the PMV index. More­
over, the analysis of the physiological signals (i.e., EDAt, ST, HR) re­
ported the activation of regulatory mechanisms, aimed to maintain 
equilibrium with the surrounding environment, confirming the physio­
logical response to the applied temperature variation. However, this 
temperature variation was not mirrored in the participants’ thermal 
sensation ratings, which remained constant between the two phases (i. 
e., C0 and C1). Considered together with the soundscapes evaluations, 
these results provide further evidence of the potential reciprocal influ­
ence between thermal and acoustic domains. Specifically, for the 
soundscapes under consideration (i.e., #2 – Woodlands and #7 – Busy 
street), participants reported lower ratings for the main descriptors, 
suggesting that minor thermal fluctuations can attenuate the perception 
of both pleasant and unpleasant auditory stimuli, and vice versa, that 
auditory stimuli can mitigate the perception of thermal variations.
Subsequently, two personality profiles were identified on the basis of 
the five characteristic traits assessed by the Big Five Questionnaire. 
Cluster A, marked by higher levels of self-control, emotional stability 
and openness, showed greater sensitivity to natural sounds, consistently 
rating them as calm and pleasant, while perceiving no substantial dif­
ferences in anthropogenic sounds across thermal phases. Conversely, 
Cluster B, characterized by neurotic tendencies, lower emotional sta­
bility, and reduced self-control, was more reactive to anthropogenic 
sounds, which they evaluated as annoying and chaotic. Interestingly, 
under higher temperature conditions, the Cluster B reported a partial 
reduction of negative evaluations, with corresponding increases in 
calmness and pleasantness.
Taken together, these findings highlight both the cross-domain 
interaction between thermal and acoustic comfort and the mediating 
role of personality traits in shaping individual environmental percep­
tion. Even when divided into personality-based clusters, the results 
consistently demonstrated a potential reciprocal influence between the 
two domains, though with distinct perceptual patterns across profiles.
In conclusion, individual characteristics significantly influence the 
perception of environmental domains, shaping different ways of expe­
riencing and adapting to indoor spaces. Nevertheless, the study is sub­
ject to several limitations. Firstly, the modest sample size, which 
precluded the development of probabilistic models of perception and 
comfort, based on the environmental conditions experienced. The 
modest sample size also limited the clustering analysis, resulting in a low 
silhouette score and affecting the robustness of the clustering outcome. 
Moreover, the experiment was conducted in a test room, a controlled 
setting without contextual factors, and used noise-cancelling head­
phones, which may have created an artificial scenario in the partici­
pants’ acoustic domain. Future research should extend these 
investigations to real environments, such as Living Labs, to assess cross- 
domain effects in real contexts. Furthermore, targeted use of sound­
scapes at specific frequencies, delivered via both headphones and room- 
based speaker, may hold potential for enhancing cognitive performance 
and concentration while mitigating the perceived impact of environ­
mental fluctuations. Finally, integrating self-reported environmental 
preferences of individuals [59], together with additional physiological 
measures such as EEG, could yield a more comprehensive understanding 
of cognitive and affective processes underlying multi-domain perception 
(Fig. 7).
