A B S T R A C T
The comfort of urban square environments in terms of thermal and acoustic conditions directly influences the 
quality of people’s travel experiences and their overall well-being. This study, represented by Xi’an Datang 
Everbright City, investigates the impact of thermal-acoustic environments on human comfort in cold regions of 
China. Four typical spaces within the city square were selected for meteorological measurements and subjective 
questionnaire surveys to gauge the thermal perception of active crowds. Using the Universal Thermal Climate 
Index (UTCI) as a thermal benchmark, the study compares the interaction between thermal conditions and 
acoustic environments. Results indicate that the neutral UTCI in Xi’an urban squares is 14.9 ◦C, with a neutral 
UTCI range of 10.8~19.0 ◦C and a preferred temperature of 17.1 ◦C. Positive types of sounds moderately reduce 
thermal sensation. Moderate environmental sounds alleviate thermal discomfort to some extent among re­
spondents. In environments such as birdsong and flowing water, people prefer higher temperatures, thereby 
increasing thermal tolerance. Conversely, in noise from traffic or construction, individuals prefer lower tem­
peratures, reducing their thermal tolerance. In environments with broadcasts or conversations, respondents 
exhibit slightly higher thermal tolerance compared to the average neutral temperature. Finally, based on 
meteorological characteristics at different survey points and respondent feedback, optimization strategies for 
design are proposed.
1. Introduction
With the rapid development of cities, China’s urbanization process 
has accelerated significantly, leading to substantial changes in urban 
meteorological conditions [1]. According to data from the National 
Bureau of Statistics in 2022, China’s urbanization rate has increased 
dramatically from 17.9 % in 1978 to 64.72 % in 2021 [2], and the urban 
resident population has grown from 170 million to 910 million Xi’an, for 
instance, has an urban population of 10.34 million and an urbanization 
rate of 79.59 % [3], making it a representative example of a highly ur­
banized city. While this rapid urbanization reflects the progress of 
China’s modern economic development, it also imposes considerable 
pressure on the urban ecological environment.
The urban heat island effect is one of the environmental issues 
resulting from rapid urbanization. Modern cities have seen an increasing 
number of buildings and structures that absorb significant amounts of 
solar radiation during the day and release this heat at night, causing a 
noticeable increase in temperatures in urban centers. Additionally, the 
urban heat island effect exacerbates temperature differences within the 
city. This temperature variation has widespread impacts on both resi­
dents and the environment [4]. Excessive temperatures not only cause 
health issues for residents, such as heatstroke and respiratory diffi­
culties, but also lead to increased energy consumption, particularly due 
to the higher use of air conditioning.
Therefore, mitigating the urban heat island effect and enhancing 
environmental comfort in cities have become significant topics of 
research and discussion among scholars in the field of the built envi­
ronment. For instance, Negar et al. [5] used measurements and ques­
tionnaires to explore outdoor thermal comfort dimensions on acoustic 
comfort, emphasizing plant communities in urban parks, to derive 
metrics of thermal comfort in relation to landscape quality. Li et al. [6]

Urban squares, as typical public spaces within cities, provide areas 
for residents and tourists to rest and interact. Therefore, it is essential to 
mitigate the heat island effect in these squares and enhance their ther­
mal comfort. However, in real environments, the factors affecting 
human comfort are complex, and studies focusing solely on the thermal 
environment cannot comprehensively reflect the level of comfort. 
Additionally, with advancements in technology and the continuous 
innovation of research methods, studies considering multiple factors 
under composite conditions have gradually emerged in recent years. 
Gao et al. [8] analyzed the weight factors of various influencing factors 
in the outdoor environment of urban residential areas and established an 
evaluation method for thermal and acoustic environments. Ali et al. [9] 
investigated gender differences in acoustic-thermal comfort and cogni­
tive performance in several simulated work environments with load 
levels, and showed that gender differences in acoustic-thermal comfort 
and performance were nearly significant, emphasizing the importance of 
considering gender differences when designing indoor work environ­
ments for optimal comfort and performance. Du et al. [10] studied 
Multiple effects of visual-acoustic-thermal perception on the overall 
comfort of older adults in residential outdoor environments, resulting in 
a regression equation that predicts the overall comfort of older adults. 
Ying et al. [11] used a university campus as a research site to explore the 
changing characteristics of human comfort in thermo-acoustic envi­
ronments, and concluded that both acoustic and thermal environments 
are important factors affecting subjective human comfort, but thermal 
environments are more important than acoustic environments. Yin et al. 
[12] explored the impact of background music on human comfort and 
work efficiency, concluding that changes in environmental parameters 
can affect psychological emotions and physiological parameters, 
altering levels of human comfort. Xiao et al. [13] investigated the impact 
of environmental quality on thermal perception under different visual 
and auditory conditions, demonstrating a negative correlation between 
overall comfort and environmental parameters, provides research sup­
port for urban space planning and design, and facilitates the creation of 
better outdoor environments. Wen et al. [14] simulated different levels 
of thermal and acoustic environments and office activity levels through 
climate chamber experiments, The conclusion that thermal environ­
ments have little effect on comfort at rest, but a significant impact during 
reading, guides the thermal and acoustic design of office building 
spaces. Yang et al. [15] studied the interaction of thermal and acoustic 
environments on indoor thermal comfort at Terminal 3 of Xi’an Xia­
nyang Airport and proposed optimization strategies. Zhen et al. [16] 
researched campus spaces, demonstrating that in most locations, 
thermal evaluation is primarily influenced by PET, while acoustic 
evaluation is mainly affected by LAeq. The interaction of thermal and 
acoustic environments affects people’s overall comfort, however, 
focusing on thermal environments alone does not provide a compre­
hensive picture of human comfort levels. Multifactorial comfort studies 
under composite conditions have gradually emerged in recent years. 
Zhang et al. [17] explored the effects of four dimensions of soundscape 
on acoustic comfort in open public spaces, and the results showed that 
the dominance of the sound source had a significant effect on relaxation, 
communication, spatial sense and dynamics.
Most current research focuses on the interaction of various comfort 
factors in laboratory, indoor, or semi-open spaces, The results show that 
under thermoacoustic interaction conditions, factors such as sound can 
significantly affect human sensitivity to the thermal environment; 
however, existing indoor thermal comfort studies tend to measure under 
stable thermoacoustic conditions, where the thermoacoustic parameters 
are relatively stable with respect to the site environment, and it is not 
possible to accurately integrate a wide range of parameters. Meanwhile, 
outdoor thermoacoustic interaction study sites have complex thermoa­
coustic conditions, and existing studies are less likely to quantify the 
type of acoustic environment according to the subjective perception of 
respondents. Existing studies have shown that improving the layout and 
facilities of the plaza space can increase people’s thermal-acoustic 
comfort to a certain extent [18]. This study investigated the interac­
tion between human acoustic perception and thermal perception in 
scenic plaza spaces through meteorological parameter measurements 
and questionnaire distribution, combined with statistical analysis of 
subjective polling, and used this as a basis to provide design optimiza­
tion strategies for outdoor environments, providing new ideas and di­
rections for future urban plaza design and management.
2. Methods
2.1. Study area
Xi’an is located in northwestern China (34◦16′N 108◦54′E / 34.267◦N 
108.9◦E). According to the K¨oppen climate classification, Xi’an lies at 
the junction of a cold semi-arid climate and a humid subtropical climate 
[19], characterized by a temperate continental monsoon climate with 
distinct four seasons. Summers are hot and humid, while winters are 
cold and dry. Based on annual temperature data from 2010 to 2021, the 
highest monthly average temperature (Ta) occurs in July (28.08 ◦C), 
with a maximum Ta of 39.33 ◦C. The lowest monthly average Ta is in 
January (0.62 ◦C), with a minimum Ta of −8.01 ◦C. The annual average 
relative humidity ranges from 51.28 % to 73.68 %. According to China’s 
current climate zoning regulations [20], Xi’an is classified as a cold 
region with long, cold, and dry winters, presenting significant oppor­
tunities for outdoor environment enhancement. To investigate the 
impact of thermal and acoustic environments on human comfort in

2.2. Experimental design
The comprehensive evaluation of human thermal comfort in varying 
environmental conditions is influenced by multiple meteorological pa­
rameters [21]. In this experiment conducted outdoors, all meteorolog­
ical parameters relevant to human thermal sensation were measured. 
The research period for this study lasted from September 2022 to 
September 2023, a one-year period covering the transition season, 
winter and summer. In Xi’an, temperatures are significantly lower in the 
early morning and evening compared to daytime, resulting in large 
diurnal temperature variations. Nearly all residents of Xi’an for over a 
year mitigate the impact of these temperature differences on comfort by 
adjusting clothing and outdoor activities. Therefore, the research was 
conducted between 9:00 and 17:00 to avoid these times.
Furthermore, rainy or snowy weather not only affects the heat ex­
change of ground surfaces in open squares but also influences re­
spondents’ subjective choices in voting. Consequently, this study 
selected days with suitable weather conditions such as clear skies or 
light cloud cover, conducive to outdoor activities for the survey.

2.2.2. Questionnaire survey
In this experiment, a questionnaire survey method was used to 
collect data from respondents. Randomly selected respondents at the 
measurement points were given questionnaires. While the respondents 
filled out the questionnaires, the surveyors simultaneously measured 
meteorological data to ensure the precision and timeliness of the data. 
The survey was conducted on days suitable for outdoor activities, such 
as sunny or cloudy days, avoiding uncomfortable weather conditions 
like rain, snow, and sandstorms, which could lead to abnormal meteo­
rological parameters.
The questionnaire consisted of two main parts (Specific question­
naire information can be found in the appendix). The first part collected 
basic information about the respondents, including age, gender, length 
of residence in Xi’an, and physical activity status in the past 20 min. Due 
to the differences in the perception of thermal sound, and the degree of 
thermal sound sensitivity of respondents in different age groups, in the 
questionnaire survey, in order to avoid such errors, we divided the re­
spondents according to the age interval they were in, and limited the 
number of respondents in each interval to an appropriate level. The 
metabolic activities were referenced according to the standard meta­
bolic rates outlined. The second part investigated respondents’ percep­
tions of the thermal and acoustic environment in public open spaces, 
including Thermal Sensation Vote (TSV), Thermal Comfort Vote (TCV), 
thermal preference, thermal acceptability, Acoustic Sensation Vote 
(ASV), Acoustic Comfort Vote (ACV), acoustic preference, acoustic 
acceptability, and overall comfort. At the same time, the main types of 
environmental sounds perceived by the respondents during the ques­
tionnaire filling process (insects, bird calls, flowing, traffic, machines, 
hawking, radio, music and crowds) were recorded and categorized after 
the data collection was completed.

comfort, this study used the Likert scale to reflect respondents’ per­
ceptions of the current thermal and acoustic environment. According to 
the ASHRAE definition, a 7-point Likert scale was used in the ques­
tionnaire for thermal sensation (−3, cold; −2, cool; −1, slightly cool; 0,

2.2.3. Thermal index
In this study, the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) was 
selected as the thermal index. This index was developed by the Inter­
national Society of Biometeorology (ISB) based on the Fiala multi-node 
thermoregulation model and the adaptive clothing model [23]. The 
UTCI is a comprehensive thermal comfort evaluation index widely used 
in existing research. It takes into account human metabolic processes, 
heat accumulation, and heat transfer, making it the most thoroughly 
considered and deeply calculated thermal index. The UTCI can be used 
for thermal comfort model predictions across different climate zones, 
environmental characteristics, and populations of different races. The 
calculation of this index incorporates various thermal environment pa­
rameters such as temperature and humidity and is typically performed 
using the RayMan software. Fig 2(b) illustrates the overall process of 
conducting the empirical tests and questionnaires in this study.
3. Results
3.1. Descriptive analysis
3.1.1. Respondent attributes
In this study, a total of 1153 questionnaires were collected, with 
1004 valid responses. Among these, 232 were from the LS site, 279 from 
the OS site, 206 from the PS site, and 289 from the SS site (Table 2). The 
survey period included dates with significant temperature variations, 
covering winter, summer, and transitional seasons. Table 2 presents the 
personal information of the respondents surveyed during the study. 
Among the respondents, 43.1 % were female and 56.9 % were male, 
indicating a higher proportion of males. The demographic at the Datang 
Everbright City showed a tendency towards youthfulness, with the 
majority of respondents aged 19–25 years, accounting for 42.8 % of the 
total survey population; followed by those aged 26–40 years, comprising 
34.4 % of the surveyed group. A significant portion of respondents had 
lived in Xi’an for less than 6 months (59.6 %), suggesting that many 
respondents were recent tourists to Xi’an, likely drawn by the attractions 
of Datang Everbright City.
3.1.2. Meteorological parameters
Through descriptive analysis using SPSS software, the maximum, 
minimum, mean, and standard deviation of meteorological parameters 
at four measurement points were computed (Table 3). It was observed 
that the OS site had the highest solar radiation (994.9 W/m²) and black 
globe temperature (45.7 ◦C), likely due to the absence of surrounding 
buildings or trees and the reflective surface of marble flooring absorbing 
significant heat. Consequently, this site also recorded the highest 
average wind speed (0.63 m/s) and lowest air humidity (26.2 %). In 
contrast, the LS site exhibited the lowest average solar radiation (70.89 
W/m²), while the PS and SS sites showed similar solar radiation levels 
(161.23 W/m² and 132.25 W/m², respectively). Observations indicated 
that the LS site was predominantly shaded by buildings, whereas the SS 
and PS sites were semi-open spaces enclosed on their eastern and 
western sides. Additionally, the PS site had the highest air humidity 
(58.9 %) likely due to a nearby water feature causing increased relative 
humidity through evaporation. The LS site showed significantly higher 
average temperatures and black globe temperatures compared to the 
other three locations (Ta: 32.6 ◦C, Tg: 33.7 ◦C). The SS site recorded the 
lowest average wind speed (0.53 m/s), attributed to wind obstruction by 
high walls and trees on both sides. Lastly, the PS site had the lowest 
average temperature (22.02 ◦C), possibly influenced by heat absorption 
by the water feature to some extent.
The sound level meter data indicates that there is minimal variation 
in the acoustic environment between the SS, PS, and OS measurement 
points. Analyzing the results of the questionnaire in conjunction with 
the questionnaire, we found that the negative sound within the OS space 
and the PS space maintained a high level. Among the four points, the OS 
site recorded the highest noise pressure level and maximum average

3.1.3. Thermal sensation vote and thermal comfort vote
Fig 4(a) illustrates the distribution of Thermal Sensation Votes (TSV) 
during the survey period. Overall, the majority of respondents voted as 
feeling "very hot" (TSV=3), accounting for 41.3 %. The next most 
frequent sensation was "neutral" (TSV=0), while very few respondents 
felt "very cold" (TSV=−3), comprising only 3.7 %. At the LS site, the 
highest proportion of respondents selected "very hot" (TSV=+3), 
totaling 63.7 %, significantly more than the other three locations. This 
perception of warmth at the LS site is largely due to the majority of votes 
being collected during the summer months, as indicated by the survey 
distribution dates in Table 2. The distribution of Thermal Sensation 
Votes at the OS and SS sites was similar, while the PS site generally felt 
cooler overall. This is attributed to landscape features such as water 
pools in the area, which lower local temperatures.
Fig 4(b) depicts the distribution of Thermal Comfort Votes (TCV) 
across the four spaces during the survey period. It shows that the ma­
jority of respondents (70.2 %) felt comfortable with the current thermal 
environment, giving votes ranging from "neutral" (TCV=0) to "slightly 
comfortable" (TCV=+1), "comfortable" (TCV=+2), and "very comfort­
able" (TCV=+3). Within the LS space, the number of people who felt

3.1.4. Acoustic sensation vote and acoustic comfort vote
In order to simplify the types of acoustic environments and investi­
gate their impact on human comfort, this study categorized the sounds 
into three different types based on respondents’ perceptions: positive 
sound sources (insect sounds, natural wind sounds, bird songs, and 
flowing water sounds), neutral sound sources (vendors’ calls, broadcast 
voices, music, and crowd sounds), and negative sound sources (traffic 
noise and machinery sounds).
The distribution of acoustic satisfaction across measurement points is 
illustrated in Fig 5(a), (b). The majority of respondents (73.3 %) rated 
the current acoustic environment as moderate (ASV=0) or satisfactory 
(ASV=2.3). There were significantly more respondents perceiving the 
current acoustic environment as noisy (ASV<0) compared to those 
perceiving it as quiet (ASV>0), indicating generally lower acoustic 
environment quality across the four spaces. Respondents at OS and SS 
sites rated the acoustic environment lower than other spaces, likely due 
to their proximity to open squares near roads and tree-shaded relaxation 
areas with high pedestrian traffic, leading to poorer acoustic 
environments.
The distribution of acoustic comfort across measurement points is 
depicted in Fig 5(c), (d), indicating that the majority (83.5 %) of re­
spondents felt comfortable (ACV≥0) with the current acoustic envi­
ronment. Specifically, the highest level of acoustic comfort was reported 
at the PS site, with the highest proportion (31.9 %) of respondents 
feeling comfortable (ACV≥0) in the current environment. Respondents 
at OS and SS sites rated the acoustic environment less comfortable 
(ACV<0) (16.2 % and 14.3 %, respectively) compared to PS and LS sites 
(18.1 % and 19.8 %, respectively), consistent with the findings in Fig 5
(a), (b). This suggests that the noisy acoustic environments at OS and SS 
sites contribute to a lower overall level of acoustic comfort.
Similar to the acoustic sensation vote, the proportion of votes indi­
cating comfort with the acoustic environment gradually decreases as the 
acoustic environment shifts from positive sound types to negative ones. 
When the acoustic environment includes sounds such as bird songs and 
flowing water, the proportion of respondents feeling comfortable rea­

types. Only 13.9 % of respondents find the acoustic environment un­
comfortable. This indicates that positive acoustic environments signifi­

tolerance for positive environmental sounds.
To explore the relationship between human acoustic perception and 
acoustic comfort during the survey period, we normalized all votes for 
Acoustic Comfort Votes (ACV) and calculated the Mean Acoustic Com­

graph for linear regression analysis, yielding Fig 5(e). This figure depicts 
how perceived acoustic environments influence acoustic comfort.

surroundings, with acoustic comfort decreasing as perceived noise levels 
increased. Specifically, when ACV = 0, ASV = −0.27, indicating mod­

acoustic perception and acoustic comfort across three types of acoustic 
environments. These were plotted together on a single coordinate

acoustic comfort variation (Fig 5(f)). The results show that for positive

perception voting, respondents reported lower acoustic comfort in 
negative noise environments compared to neutral noise environments, 
with positive noise environments yielding the highest acoustic comfort 
votes. These findings indicate that in negative noise conditions, changes 
in acoustic perception significantly affect acoustic comfort; furthermore, 
increasing sound pressure levels further decrease acoustic comfort 
relative to the other two acoustic environment conditions.
3.1.5. Relationship between thermal sensation and meteorological 
parameters
When individuals are in outdoor spaces, meteorological parameters 
such as air temperature and solar radiation significantly impact their 
thermal perception [24]. Variations in these parameters lead to differ­
ences in subjective evaluations of the thermal environment. To explore 
the effects of four factors on thermal sensation in different spaces, partial 
correlation analysis was conducted between Thermal Sensation Vote 
(TSV) and four key meteorological parameters, as shown in Table 4. In 
analyzing the correlation between a specific meteorological parameter 
and TSV, the other three parameters were controlled, thereby isolating 
the influence of each parameter on thermal perception. The r value 
represents the correlation coefficient between thermal sensation and the 
meteorological parameter, indicating the parameter’s influence on TSV, 
while P signifies the significance of the parameter’s correlation.
The results indicate that air temperature is the most critical factor 
affecting thermal comfort across all spaces (P ≤0.01), positively 
correlating with TSV, which aligns with subjective perceptions. Relative 
humidity and wind speed were found to be the least significant factors, 
showing weak correlations with thermal sensation and having no sig­
nificant impact at most measurement points. In OS site, solar radiation 
exhibited the strongest correlation with thermal sensation (P ≤0.001), 
followed by SS site, consistent with environmental factors. The OS site, 
being open squares with no surrounding obstructions, did not block 
solar radiation, resulting in higher G values and increased TSV. 
Conversely, in LS site, located in shaded areas and not exposed to direct 
sunlight during the survey period, the correlation was weakest (r =
0.062) and the significance was lowest (P = 0.350).

4. Discussion
4.1. Outdoor thermal indices
4.1.1. Neutral temperature
This study is conducted in Xi’an, a cold region. To compare thermal 
comfort variations under different conditions and with overall popula­
tion data, several studies from different regions were used to extract 
neutral temperatures. Xi’an’s neutral UTCI is similar to year-round 
surveys conducted in the same region [35] (Table 5), being 0.7 ◦C 
higher than Tehran [36]. This similarity indicates similar thermal per­
ceptions among residents in these areas. Xi’an’s neutral UTCI is lower 
than Athens [34] by 5.4 ◦C and Guangzhou [38] by 8.8 ◦C, but higher 
than Harbin [37] by 8.2 ◦C. These differences in NUCTI are due to cli­
matic variations: Athens has a warmer climate, Guangzhou is in a sub­
tropical region, and Harbin experiences severe cold.
4.1.2. Range of neutral temperature
The NUTCIR observed in this study is narrower compared to year- 
round NUTCIR in the same surveyed area, wider than Tehran and 
Guangzhou, and closer to Dalian. This variation may stem from the 
study’s focus on children in Xi’an [35], who exhibit stronger adaptive 
capabilities to thermal changes. In contrast, Tehran [36] experiences 
more stable climatic conditions. Residents in Xi’an are thus more 
adaptable to the diverse seasonal changes in thermal environments. The 
lower limit of NUTCIR in this study is higher than Harbin [37] by 10.3 
◦C, while the upper limit is lower than Athens [34] by 5.2 ◦C and Dalian 
[39] by 4.0 ◦C. This indicates varying levels of adaptation to thermal 
environments among residents living in different climatic zones.
4.1.3. Percentage of thermal discomfort
When MTSV = 0, the PPD in Xi’an’s campus is lower compared to 
other surveyed areas within the city (Fig 15). This suggests that during 
the study period, respondents in this area had a higher tolerance for their 
environment. Within discomfort zones (MTSV = +2, +3) or (MTSV =
−3, −2), Xi’an’s PPD is lower than ISO 7730 standards, indicating that 
the population in Xi’an is more capable of adapting to uncomfortable 
temperatures compared to the standard.
4.2. Strategies for enhancing thermal-acoustic comfort in urban open 
spaces
4.2.1. Physical environment status of open squares
This study highlights the significant influence of physical factors on 
the thermal-acoustic environment of open square spaces. Targeted 
landscaping can effectively improve outdoor micro-climates. In the LS 
site, globe radiation is significantly lower compared to other spaces. This 
area exhibits higher thermal comfort in summer but lower comfort in 
winter due to lower temperatures. The OS site, being open and unob­
structed, experiences higher solar radiation in summer and reduced 
thermal sensation in winter due to high wind speeds. Moreover, this 
space has the lowest relative humidity, exacerbating thermal discom­
fort. The PS measurement point features abundant water features that 
increase humidity, alleviating thermal discomfort, while large trees on 
the west side provide ample shaded areas, reducing solar radiation in 
summer. The SS site, enclosed by high walls on both sides, experiences

In terms of acoustic environment, the LS site, located at the end of a 
road, has the lowest average sound pressure level (SPL Mean = 58 dB),

Therefore, measures should be implemented at OS and PS sites to reduce 
sound pressure levels and improve acoustic conditions.

discomfort. [40] On this basis, Su et al. [41] analyzed the effects of the 
setting height of the spray device and the amount of spray on the human 
body in different types of environments. Therefore, for space OS and 
space SS, a spray device is added in summer to increase humidity, 
alleviate the thermal discomfort caused by solar radiation, reduce the 
temperature of the human surface through water vapor evaporation, and 
increase the overall comfort of the environment.
There are many kinds of materials for landscape sketches, and too 
high temperature may cause thermal discomfort or even scalding, which 
will affect people’s safety. Huang et al. [35] studied the influence of 
outdoor landscape and seat surface material on the thermal perception 
of the human body, and concluded that the temperature of some 
landscape sketches is higher when exposed to direct sunlight, which 
seriously affects the thermal safety of children.
To alleviate the discomfort of the heat, people who chose to rest in 
the shade voted the most. For the OS, SS and PS spaces that are exposed 
to direct sunlight in summer, it is expected to set up sunshade facilities in 
combination with landscape sketches, pay attention to public partici­
pation and interaction, and expect to achieve artistic aesthetics and 
practical application value.
Optimizing the environmental conditions of measurement point 
spaces should be based on empirical data, using layout and landscape 
composition to strategically improve thermal-acoustic environments 
and enhance overall comfort in outdoor activity spaces.

4.3. Limitations and future work
Our study has several limitations. 1) the acoustic environment pa­
rameters at measurement points may exhibit significant fluctuations, 
which are less stable compared to meteorological parameters. For future 
study we should continue to monitor the acoustic environment to 
improve the accuracy of the research. 2) this study only considered re­
spondents’ thermal and acoustic perceptions; future research should 
delve deeper into other factors such as light.
5. Conclusion
In an open square space in Xi’an, four typical spaces were selected for 
systematic study and comparison of thermal and acoustic physical en­
vironments and environmental landscape factors influencing people’s 
thermal perception evaluations outdoors, using methods such as mete­
orological measurements and questionnaires. A series of strategies for 
multidimensional optimization of the square were proposed. The con­
clusions are as follows: 
1. The UTCI of Xi’an urban squares is 14.9 ◦C, with a neutral UTCI 
range of 10.8~19.0 ◦C, preferred temperature of 17.1 ◦C, and ther­
mal comfort range of 7.8 ◦C-25.7 ◦C.
2. When thermal sensation votes are neutral (TSV=−1,0,1), re­
spondents influenced by negative sound tend to prefer lower
