A B S T R A C T  
As human beings live in an environment where various factors interact and integrate, it seems vital to examine 
the effects of these factors on environmental perceptions. Outdoor thermal and acoustic environments are the 
main factors affecting the overall comfort of urban spaces. This research uses measurements and questionnaire 
surveys to explore the effects of outdoor thermal comfort dimensions on acoustic comfort, emphasizing the plant 
community in a city park (the El Goli Park of Tabriz, Iran). In this regard, the general comfort of the psycho­
logical equivalent temperature (PET) under different conditions of the thermal-acoustic environment has been 
evaluated and predicted. The results showed that a higher thermal sensation vote (TSV) and PET result in a 
significant decrease in soundscape quality (SQ), overall impression (OI), and the percentage of reported natural 
dominant sound (%RNDS), while an inverse trend has been reported in thermal comfort vote (TCV). On the other 
hand, by increasing TSV and PET, reported loudness (RL) has recorded an upward trend. However, this process 
was completely reversed in TCV. Furthermore, the analyzes showed that PET has a lower effectiveness compared 
to TSV and TCV and, it should be stated that the three indicators of thermal comfort did not show any significant 
relationship with landscape quality (LQ) and sound pressure level (SPL). These findings are important for 
managing and improving outdoor acoustic comfort and soundscapes, and it is expected that future studies will 
consider the acquired results in various green urban environments and provide a practical framework for 
landscape architects and urban planners.   
1. Introduction 
Indiscriminate urbanization as one the phenomenon of the present 
century leads to a variety of environmental problems, including water 
shortage [1], industrial and community waster [2], land pollution [3], 
increased density of population (S. [4], noise discomfort [5], and 
climate change [6]. In this regard, exposure to noise in outdoor envi­
ronments has numerous effects on human health, such as hearing loss, 
sleep problems, cardiovascular and mental health disorders [7,8]. On 
the other hand, the direct impact of climate changes on people’s quality 
of life and the creation of a local phenomenon called the urban heat 
island (UHI) has led to thermal discomfort in urban spaces [9]. 
Urban parks are recognized as an integral component of the intricate 
urban ecosystem network, which provides various advantages to urban 
residents [10]. Open green areas in urban parks can improve mental and 
physical health by decreasing the exposure of inhabitants to air 
pollutants, noise pollution, and extreme heat as well as offering psy­
chological relaxation and stress relief [11]. Plant Covers have been re­
ported as one of the most important factors determining the quality of 
urban spaces in the acoustic comfort of urban spaces [12–15],followed 
by thermal comfort [16–18]. 
Acoustic comfort is the basic feeling of users towards the acoustic 
environment [19]. Studies show that acoustic comfort is beyond noise 
control and includes subjective interpretations of a sound in a particular 
context [20]; F [21]. The term “soundscape” has been defined as the 
“acoustic environment as perceived or experienced and/or understood 
by a person or people, in context” [22]. Typical studies dealt with 
soundscape experiences such as sound level, perceived loudness [23], 
perceived dominant sound [24], and pleasantness for sounds [25] etc. 
These experiences may be influenced by different sound sources [26, 
27], the social/demographic/behavioral characteristics [28], landscape 
features [29] and physical factors such as temperature, humidity and

On the other hand, thermal comfort can be expressed as the physi­
ological interval in which the human able to operate or tolerate the 
environment [35]. It is the condition in which the human body feels 
satisfied with the thermal environment [36] and is affected by heat 
conduction, convection, radiation, and loss of evaporative heat [37]. 
Several factors such as air temperature, humidity, air flow, solar radia­
tion, terrestrial radiation, metabolic heat, and clothing insulation are 
factors and the energy budget of a person in outdoor spaces [38,39]. In 
the evaluation of the outdoor thermal environment, environment 
microclimatic condition, physiological components (such as the meta­
bolism rate), and behavioral elements are essential for better thermal 
comfort [40]. 
It is necessary to evaluate and identify different aspects of thermal 
comfort as one of the most important environmental factors affecting 
soundscapes [14]; M [34].. To evaluate acoustic comfort conditions, 
only understanding the acoustic factors is not enough, and multiple 
environmental stimuli, including thermal comfort, visual beauty of the 
environment, and greenness can be effective in the perceived quality of 
the acoustic environment. Studies showed that thermal conditions 
significantly affected feelings of loudness or calmness of sound [41–43]. 
Temperature changes have also been shown to increase or decrease 
perceived acoustic comfort [44]. Meanwhile [45], suggested that the 
thermal environment had no effect on the measured noise levels. 
Although the stated results can have different effects in different spatial 
conditions, including the variety and extent of natural elements and 
different socio-cultural factors such as age, it is predicted that the 
acoustic environment perceived in outdoor spaces is related to the 
experienced thermal comfort conditions. 
Plants are one of the influential indexes in managing environmental 
conditions that can lead to better outdoor comfort conditions. These 
include an improved quality of visual comfort [46] and thermal comfort 
[47,48]; J [49]. In terms of acoustic comfort evaluation, previous studies 
pointed out the importance of plants in mitigating noise pollution by 
plants, tree [50–52], or by land use parameters emphasizing green 
spaces and their ratio [53] [54], or the psychological and physiological 
impacts of noise reduction [55]; F [56]. Some research also found that 
non-acoustical features including plants can increase acoustic comfort 
quality by producing natural soundscapes such as tree rustling [57–59]. 
At the same order to regulate outdoor thermal comfort, solar atten­
uation, and evaporative cooling capacity can provide relatively suitable 
thermal comfort conditions in different cities [60]. These effects are 
compared to the reduction of air and surface temperature under the tree 
canopy compared to other areas [61,62]. Shade in trees is also consid­
ered as one of the determining factors in improving thermal comfort to 
cope with the heat sensation of the day and reduce the mean radiant 
temperature (MRT). Numerous researches have analyzed the impact of 
trees as a cooling strategy on urban climate and regulation of human 
thermal comfort in Asia [63]; H. [18,36,64,65]; L. [66], Europa [67–71] 
and North America [72]; Y [73]. which points to the undeniable effect of 
urban trees. The summary of the studies on the influence of plant spaces 
on outdoor thermal comfort and acoustic comfort, are highlighted in 
Table 1. 
The review of previous research illustrated that the role of plant 
covers in improving acoustic and thermal comfort has been well estab­
lished. But few studies have examined the relationship between these 
two indicators in the outdoor environment with an emphasis on natural 
elements, among which the role of the effect of acoustic comfort on 
thermal comfort has been further analyzed [14,44,77–79] (Table 1). 
This study focuses on the combined effects of thermal comfort on the 
acoustic environment, with an emphasis on changes in the plant com­
munity of El Goli park, Tabriz city, Iran. In this regard, first, the acoustic

2. Methodology and data collection 
This section describes the study area and how the thermal comfort 
and soundscape data of the EL Goli Park were collected and then 
analyzed. 
2.1. Study area 
This study was carried out in Tabriz, Iran (38.0792◦N, 46.2887◦E) 
at an altitude of 1351 m [80]. The existence of the Guzni and Bababaghi 
mountains in the northeast and Sahand mountains range in the south 
creates considerable differences in the climate in the north and west of 
the city. According to the K¨oppen classification, Tabriz has a cold and 
dry climate [81] with a maximum temperature of 26.60 ◦C, and a 
minimum temperature of −1.77, an average annual temperature of 
12.9 ◦C (Fig. 1). 
The selected location (El-Goli Park) with an approximate area of 70 
km2 hectares is located in the southeast region of Tabriz, Iran, which is 
surrounded by main highways in the south and west, and residential 
areas in the east and north. El-Goli Park is made up of a vast garden, a 
40,000 m2 artificial lake, an octagonal mansion in the center of the lake, 
and recreational spaces such as Luna Park and cafes that have been 
considered as an excellent example of a topographic design. This park 
was listed in the national heritage of Iran in 2009. The park includes a 
great diversity of natural communities, especially plant communities. 
The sounds of plants are one of the main features of the park, which can 
be found in different plant forms, leading to different biological 
soundscapes. To determine the selected points for the evaluation of 
outdoor acoustic and thermal comfort, first the climatic, cultural, and 
social characteristics of it were considered. Then, based on the type of 
plant enclosure, the selected spaces were classified into open spaces 
(OS), semi-open spaces (SS), closed spaces (CS) and vertical spaces (VS) 
(L1to L20). L1, L2, and L17 were located at the entrance of the park, 
where there were fewer plants and sound elements, but greater traffic. 
Similarly, L3 and L12, which are located inside the park, have a lower 
plant density. The remaining 15 selected points were surrounded by 
diverse plant and landscape characteristics (Table 2 and Fig. 1). 
2.2. Objective assessment 
2.2.1. Acoustic comfort data 
Sound levels (dBA) were measured to analyze the quality of the 
acoustic environment at each observation point [28]. To achieve this, 
the A-weighting sound pressure level A (SPL) LAeq was recorded [82], 
with a sound level meter (see Appendix 1, Table 8) for a duration of 5 
min. The meter microphone was positioned 1.2 m above ground level to 
decrease the impact of acoustic reflection [83]. Acoustic data collection 
was conducted for one week in June, July, and August 2021 during three 
specified time periods: 8–10 a.m., 1–3 p.m., and 6–8 p.m. The selected 
days had good weather and were without rain and wind (wind speed 
<10 m/s). Finally, to generate the representing LAeq, the mean value 
was calculated at all 20 locations. 
2.2.2. Thermal comfort data 
Microclimate data monitoring was conducted simultaneously in the 
time intervals mentioned in the previous section. To analyze and 
determine the range of the thermal comfort index in EL Goli park in 
Tabriz, PET was considered as a thermal comfort index. In this regard, 
given the ASHRAE standard, air temperature (Ta), relative humidity 
(Rh), wind speed (Ws), and mean radiant temperature (Mrt) have been 
analyzed. To calculate MRT, it was necessary to consider global tem­
perature (TGLOBE) Eq. (1) where ε is global emissivity and D is global 
diameter [18,36,84] and was performed by the kestrel 5400 (standard 
ST-174B) while for calculating other parameters have been used a data 
logger at a height of 1.7 m has been used. Appendix 1, Table 8 illustrates 
the features of the equipment used.

2.3. Subjective assessment (questionnaire) 
Questionnaire surveys have been conducted simultaneously with the 
monitoring step, and it consists of 3 parts. The first part of the ques­
tionnaire is allocated to demographic data (that is, age and sex). In the 
second part, and given that prior research [25,86–88]; X. [24,29,66], 
three subsections were defined for acoustic comfort responses. Firstly, to 
examine the effects of perceived sound types on acoustic comfort, re­
spondents were asked to rank three types of perceived sounds, using a 
ten-point Likert scale, in which 0 was ‘unable to hear” and 10 was 
“clearly hear”. Then, their answers were grouped according to the type 
of sound: natural, anthropogenic, or mechanical. Second, the partici­
pants were asked whether they perceived a subjectively dominant sound 
in the soundscape and if so, which. Then, four indicators including 
soundscape quality, the perceived loudness, landscape quality, and the 
overall impression were determined for participants to score on a scale 
of 1–5 (1 indicating the lowest quality and 5 indicating the highest). 
Eventually, the last section of the questionnaire was related to the vis­
itors’ position, type of activity, type of clothes as well as thermal 
sensation and thermal comfort. In this section, unlike many previous 
studies (M. [89]; M. A [90–92]. that used seven-category thermal 
sensation scales, due to the better adaptation to the wide range of out­
door thermal conditions [93] and more potential for the development of 
thermal comfort indices such as PET thermal sensation patterns [94,95] 
two extreme votes were added. Therefore, this scale ranges from −4 to 
+4 (very cold (−4), cold (−3), cool (−2), slightly cool (−1), neutral (0), 
slightly warm (+1), warm (+2), hot (+3) and very hot (+4). An example 
of the questionnaire used in the park is shown in Appendix 2, Table 9. 
2.4. Characteristics of the respondents 
Participants were randomly selected in the area, and a total of 300 
valid questionnaires were collected. All participants were informed 
about the voluntary nature of the study before taking part. Considering 
that the differences in the basic physical environment might be caused 
by the time, the survey at the 20 positions was carried out from 9:00 a.m. 
to 21:00 a.m. Most visitors were sitting (45.7%) and the rest were 
walking (20.7%) or standing (33.6%). Moreover, respondents who were 
under the shading trees environment accounted for 43.26% while the 
rest of them were not in the shade 20 min before the survey. These data 
provide a comprehensive background of the respondents to ensure a 
random sampling of participants and allow potential adjustments to 
these factors in the statistical analyses. The age group that was repeated 
the most was 21–35 years (38.57%), followed by 36–50 years (28.7%). 
More men completed the survey than women (57% men, 43% women), 
and their average height and weight were 1.72 cm and 71 kg, respec­
tively, while the average height and weight of the women who partici­
pated in this survey were 1.52 cm and 57 kg, respectively. A summary of 
personal information is observed in Table 3. These details give a rich 
portrait of the respondents, allowing for possible adjustments to be 
applied in statistical analyses to account for any imbalances in the dis­
tribution of respondents’ characteristics.

models of the correlated indicators were developed. Furthermore, a one- 
way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was conducted to find the re­
lationships between thermal and acoustic comfort dimensions. Finally, 
simple linear regression was used to predict acoustic comfort based on

3. Results 
3.1. Analysis of acoustic comfort and reported indicators 
In terms of sound pressure level (SL), Fig. 2 shows the difference of 
LAeq among the selected places during summer. The existing differences 
are rooted in plant communities, user activities, and spatial conditions. 
VS with dense plants recorded the lowest sound levels. On the contrary, 
Location 1 reported a high LAeq (between 64 and 67 dB) due to the large 
gathering of birds, especially crows, as well as exercise activities. CS 
were the second quietest places and L8 reported remarkable low LAeq 
values with values between 55 and 58 dB, as it was adjacent to the 
residential parts and the protected forest covering the external noise. 
Meanwhile, SS showed a slight increase in sound levels. The high LAeq 
of place 11 reflected the impact of human activities, a place where 
morning sports, children’s play, and cafe meetings occur. Finally, OS 
recorded the highest LAeqs (L16 and 20, with LAeq values between 66 
and 72 dB), which was mainly due to exposure to external noise factors, 
including traffic and Luna Park equipment. 
In terms of the identified sound types, Fig. 3 illustrates the frequency 
of the identified sound sources at each selected location. In VS, natural 
sounds were perceived the most and heard clearly, such as the 
swooshing sounds of dense plant species with larger leaves, such as 
plane trees. In addition, participants highly mentioned the sound of 
birds and water forms (burbling water, channels, and streams). In CS, 
both anthropogenic and natural sound were dominantly perceived. 
Nature sounds including rustling of trees with small leaves, such as 
willow (L19) and pine (L7 and L9), followed by bird singing, frogs, and 
cicadas was heard. Visitors also produce various types of human sounds 
such as talking, yelling, and footsteps. Regarding SS, human sounds 
were dominant and both natural and mechanical sound were secondly 
identified. The dominance of human sounds was due to various public 
activities including talking, playing, fitness activities, and cycling. Also, 
a weak swooshing can be heard where the plant community owned 
different leaf types, such as Robinia and Morus alba pendula (L13, L14 
and L15). Bird singing was present at a lower level due to the lower 
density of plants. Lastly, OS owned the most frequent mechanical 
sounds, which were located near the Luna Park and city routs. Natural 
sounds were heard weakly, which were negligible at locations 19 and 
20. 
The Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to assess whether there were 
significant differences in acoustic comfort dimensions between the 
specified space types. Based on the results, as shown in Table 4, there 
were significant differences among the four indicators, including 
soundscape quality (SQ), overall impression (OI), percentage of reported 
natural dominant sound (%RNDS) and reported loudness (RL) (p < .05). 
However, there were no significant differences between LQ (p > .05). 
This finding could be explained by two factors: It is crucial to properly 
plan natural elements in line with other landscape aspects, such as 
architectural elements, because the sheer presence of natural elements, 
such as plants, cannot adequately boost the visual quality of the envi­
ronment. Additionally, the landscape’s vastness (the depth of field) is 
occasionally thought of as an environmental design potential that can be 
constrained by the presence of a plant community. For example, location 
3 in the category of open space was said to have a high-quality envi­
ronment because the El Goli Park mansion was located in the middle of 
the main pool and had beautiful views of the surroundings. Fig. 4 shows 
the mean values of reported indicators. It can be seen that SQ, OI and % 
RNDS showed a similar trend. 
Regarding SQ, Table 4 shows that there were significant differences

4. Discussion 
Outdoor acoustic comfort is an essential aspect of urban design that 
affects the use of outdoor spaces, the behavior of city dwellers, and the 
comfort felt in the environment. The increase in the growth of urbani­
zation and the appearance of various types of noise pollution, including 
traffic and construction, weaken the acoustic comfort quality in the 
environment and cause users’ dissatisfaction. However, different envi­
ronmental factors, especially the thermal environment, can improve the 
acoustic conditions and the associated experiences in outdoor spaces. In 
the present study, the effect of outdoor thermal comfort on acoustic 
comfort and related indicators was investigated in four plant-space 
categories of an urban park (El-Goli, Tabriz). 
Regarding the effects of thermal comfort on SQ, TSV and PET 
negatively and TCV positively are related to the perceived quality of 
soundscape. Our results indicated that, during summer, people in the 
cool and comfortable environments perceived a higher soundscape 
quality. It can be stated that by increasing the plant cover and adjusting 
the ambient temperature, the quality of the soundscape significantly will 
be promoted. In other words, the presence of natural elements in urban 
environments, especially trees, creates multiple positive soundscapes, 
such as wind blowing, rustling, birdsong, etc., which makes the envi­
ronment more pleasant. These findings are similar to the results of 
previous studies [44,99,100]; M [34]. that low levels of thermal sensa­
tion (cooling of the environment) in the hot season and high levels of 
thermal comfort significantly increase the quality of the soundscape.

tween thermal comfort and LQ. Several studies [14,106] stated that 
visual aesthetics can be effective in judging thermal comfort and users 
perceive higher comfort conditions in beautiful environments. However, 
according to the results of this study, in reverse mode, no relationship 
was found between thermal comfort and aesthetics. aesthetics. The 
reason can be rooted in the design method and composition of visual 
elements, including the presence of different visual elements, such as 
greenery, water forms, architectural aesthetics, and in some cases, the 
depth of the field of view. Similarly, the mean landscape quality in open 
and semi-open spaces is reported with a relatively slight difference 
compared to closed and vertical spaces. 
The surveys were conducted in a limited time frame (summer 2021); 
hence, the findings may only be true under warm conditions. Further­
more, the role of other related variables such as the SVF index and noise 
indices may help the accuracy of the resulting analysis. Additionally, the 
focus of the current research was on the division of spaces based on plant 
communities, and the role of other effective factors, such as materials, 
water elements, etc., can be investigated in future studies to make the 
findings more accurate. Moreover, the factors that determine tree 
shading and show higher correlations with cooling effect and thermal 
comfort than with acoustic comfort can be analyzed in the future. 
Finally, more research is necessary at different locations considering 
their unique ecological and environmental characteristics and the social, 
economic, and cultural differences of their users. 
5. Conclusions 
Our study suggested that thermal comfort dimensions have signifi­
cant effects on acoustic comfort and related indicators according to a 
questionnaire survey and thermal acoustic measurements. In this regard:  
• Regarding TSV changes, the thermal sensation has a significant effect 
on SQ, %RNDS, OI, and RL. higher TSV results in a significant 
decrease in SQ, %RNDS, and OI. However, with the increase in TSV, 
RL increases significantly in all plant spaces. More precisely, the 
group in the ‘Warm’ thermal condition has a significantly lower SQ, 
%RNDS, and OI, as well as higher RL than the groups with ‘Neutral’ 
or ‘Cool’. Furthermore, it was shown that TSV can be considered as a 
predictor for SQ, %RNDS, and RL in vertical spaces.  
• With respect to changes in TCV, thermal comfort has a significant 
effect on SQ, %RNDS, OI, and RL. Higher TCV results in a significant 
increase in SQ, %RNDS, and OI. On the other hand, with the increase 
in TCV, RL decreases significantly. Specifically, the group under 
thermal condition has a significantly lower SQ,% RNDS and OI, than 
that of group with ‘neutral’ or ‘Comfortable’. However, in terms of 
RL, the group in ‘uncomfortable’ thermal condition has a signifi­
cantly higher RL only with the ‘comfortable’ group and RL is 
perceived in a similar way to the ‘neutral’ group. Furthermore, it was 
shown that TCV can be considered as a predictor of RL in plant 
spaces.  
• With respect to PET changes, it can be said that PET has a significant 
effect on SQ, % RNDS, OI and RL. higher PET results in a significant 
decrease in SQ, %RNDS, and OI. On the other hand, with the increase 
in PET, RL increases significantly in all plant spaces. However, our 
analyzes showed that PET has lower effectiveness compared to TSV 
and TCV. 
In addition, it should be noted that all three indicators of thermal 
comfort did not show any significant relationship with LQ and SPL. 
These findings are important for managing and improving outdoor 
acoustic comfort and soundscapes, since, for example, to improve a 
given soundscape, the outdoor thermal comfort condition should also be 
assessed too, and then, specific actions can be implemented to eliminate 
or reduce negative thermal comfort factors and promote positive factors. 
Therefore, more work is needed to identify the extensive and complex 
relationships of environmental factors to improve acoustic comfort in 
urban landscapes.
