A B S T R A C T
Although soundscapes modulate outdoor thermal perception, sex differences in physiological and psychological 
mechanisms under thermal stress remain elusive. This study examined thermo-acoustic interactions across six 
acoustic conditions that varied in diversity (low/medium/high) and environmental context (nature-dominant 
versus anthropogenic-dominant) in male and female participants exposed to graded thermal stress. Physiological 
indicators (blood pressure, heart rate, and eardrum temperature), electroencephalographic (EEG) activity, and 
psychological metrics were continuously monitored alongside microclimatic parameters. Results demonstrate 
marked sexual dimorphism in thermo-acoustic perception, primarily within nature-dominant environments. 
Males exhibited cardiovascular attenuation under slight thermal stress, with systolic pressure declining 6.08 
mmHg (medium diversity) and diastolic pressure 6.61 mmHg (high diversity) versus low diversity (p < 0.05), 
though physiological responsivity diminished as thermal stress escalated. Females conversely demonstrated 
intensified physiological reactions with increasing thermal load, including reduced heart rate under no thermal 
stress conditions in anthropogenic settings (p < 0.05). In nature-dominant sound environments, low soundscape 
diversity attenuated male δ power while potentiating α power, consistent with diminished somnolence and 
heightened relaxation; elevated female θ power, conversely, indexed enhanced positive affect. Psychologically, 
males demonstrated consistently higher sensitivity, while females exhibited a biphasic pattern of initial atten­
uation followed by enhancement. In anthropogenic-dominant environments, males displayed heightened phys­
iological and psychological sensitivity to acoustic diversity, whereas females showed asynchronous patterns: 
physiological responses weakened then strengthened, while psychological responses progressively intensified 
with increasing thermal stress. Our findings inform sex-sensitive acoustic optimization frameworks for urban 
design, advancing evidence-based healthy built environment strategies.
1. Introduction
1.1. Background
Human environmental perception is mediated through five sensory 
modalities, with vision dominantly governing spatial cognition and 
audition serving as the secondary influential channel [1]. Multisensory 
integration research demonstrates that perceptual modalities function 
synergistically rather than independently, converging to construct ho­
listic environmental experiences [2]. The soundscape, defined as the 
acoustic environment perceived, experienced, and understood within 
specific 
contexts, 
significantly 
modulates 
both 
physiological 
homeostasis and psychological states [3]. Biophilic acoustic stimuli 
promote psychophysiological restoration and affective regulation [4–6], 
whereas anthropogenic noise, particularly traffic-related emissions, 
elicits annoyance and compromises wellbeing [7,8]. Consequently, 
outdoor environmental research that privileges visual parameters while 
neglecting acoustic dimensions remains fundamentally incomplete.
Outdoor thermal perception emerges from the interplay of meteo­
rological parameters and non-thermal environmental modulators, with 
the acoustic environment serving as a critical regulatory factor [9]. 
Empirical evidence indicates that biophilic acoustic stimuli mitigate 
thermal discomfort and enhance environmental acceptability, whereas 
anthropogenic noise exacerbates thermal stress [10]. Notably, this 
thermo-acoustic interaction exhibits thermal-intensity dependence:

Soundscape-mediated thermal mitigation has emerged as a pivotal 
research frontier in landscape architecture and built environment 
studies. As a perceptual regulatory strategy, acoustic optimization offers 
a cost-effective alternative to energy-intensive physical cooling in­
terventions [12]. Systematic elucidation of these thermo-acoustic 
mechanisms and their translation into spatial design strategies thus 
hold substantial implications for advancing urban environmental 
quality.
1.2. Literature review
Soundscapes modulate autonomic function beyond subjective 
perception, operating through central and peripheral pathways to in­
fluence cardiovascular and electrodermal activity, as well as affective 
dimensions of valence and arousal [13]. These psychophysiological ef­
fects are source-dependent: natural stimuli generally facilitate stress 
recovery, whereas anthropogenic noise elicits adverse autonomic and 
emotional responses [14–16]. Sound pressure level represents a critical 
physical determinant of these responses [17,18].
As soundscape assessment has shifted from unidimensional metrics 
to multidimensional structural complexity, soundscape diversity has 
emerged as a key indicator of environmental information richness [19]. 
Particularly within urban green spaces, diversity significantly mediates 
acoustic preference, affective quality, and overall soundscape appraisal, 
serving as a critical bridge between spatial characteristics and percep­
tual evaluation [20,21]. Elevated biophonic diversity promotes psy­
chophysiological restoration [22]; however, this relationship follows an 
inverted-U pattern, wherein excessive acoustic layering attenuates 
beneficial effects [23].
Thermal environments modulate cognitive and affective states, 
thereby governing perceptual processing of ambient stimuli [24]. 
Thermal stress activates autonomic thermoregulatory mechanisms, 
manifesting as altered cardiovascular activity, cutaneous temperature, 
and endocrine profiles [25]. In actual outdoor contexts, thermal and 
acoustic parameters exert synergistic effects on environmental percep­
tion and comfort evaluation [12]. Elevated sound pressure levels 
amplify thermal sensation and compromise thermal comfort, whereas 
biophilic soundscapes attenuate perceived warmth relative to anthro­
pogenic 
acoustic 
environments 
[26,27]. 
This 
thermo-acoustic 
interaction is stress-dependent: under moderate thermal loads, acous­
tically pleasant environments significantly diminish subjective warmth 
sensation [24], while acoustic dissatisfaction predicts heightened heat 
discomfort [28]. Reciprocally, microclimatic parameters, encompassing 
air temperature, humidity, and solar radiation, critically shape sound­
scape appraisal in open-space settings [29,30].
Sex constitutes a critical individual difference variable mediating 
psychophysiological reactivity to acoustic environments [31,32]. Fe­
males demonstrate heightened auditory sensitivity and noise suscepti­
bility, whereas males exhibit exaggerated cardiovascular and autonomic 
responses to acoustic stimulation [33]. Sexual dimorphism extends to 
soundscape preferences; for example, females demonstrate particular 
affinity for wind sounds [34].
These disparities persist under thermal challenge. Females display 
enhanced thermal sensitivity and more volatile subjective thermal 
evaluations than males under identical microclimatic conditions [35], 
potentially reflecting fundamental differences in adipose distribution 
and metabolic thermoregulation [36]. Consequently, females exhibit 
elevated vulnerability to thermal fatigue and negative affective states. 
Thus, soundscape diversity effects likely manifest sexually dimorphic 
patterns across thermal stress gradients, necessitating systematic 
investigation.
1.3. Research gap
Extant research on soundscape-thermo-physiological interactions 
has predominantly examined acoustic intensity and source typologies, 
yet remains largely unidimensional, neglecting structural complexity 
and diversity effects. Critically, the mechanisms by which soundscape 
diversity modulates psychophysiological responses across thermal stress 
gradients remain elusive. Furthermore, the moderating effects of sex on 
these restorative effects has been largely overlooked, with sexual 
dimorphism in diversity perception and thermo-physiological reactivity 
poorly characterized. This necessitates a systematic, sex-differentiated 
investigation of soundscape diversity mechanisms to inform nuanced, 
sex-sensitive design interventions for urban open spaces, ultimately 
advancing population health and wellbeing.

2. Method
2.1. Experimental design
2.1.1. Study site
Xi’an occupies the K¨oppen climate boundary between cold semi-arid 
(BSk) and humid subtropical (Cwa) regimes, exhibiting a semi-humid 
continental monsoon climate with pronounced seasonal contrast, 
characterized by hot, rainy summers and cold, dry winters [37]. Local 
climatological records indicate maximum monthly mean air tempera­
ture (Ta) of 28.08 ◦C in July (absolute maximum 39.33 ◦C), and mini­
mum monthly mean Ta of 0.62 ◦C in January (absolute minimum −8.01 
◦C), with annual relative humidity (RH) ranging 51.28–73.68 % [38].
The trials were conducted at a 170 ha university campus in Xi'an 
(34◦15′44″N, 108◦04′37″E), selected for its morphological heterogeneity. 
Three microclimatic contexts, including open (OS), partially shaded 
(PS), and shaded (SS), were stratified by landscape configuration and 
Sky View Factor (SVF), with site selection prioritizing acoustic isolation 
to preclude ambient noise interference (Fig. 1). SVF was quantified via 
hemispherical photography (Canon EOS 6D; EF 8–15 mm f/4 L fisheye 
lens) and subsequent RayMan analysis.
2.1.2. Soundscape selection
Soundscape diversity and source typology significantly modulate 
emotional appraisal and overall acoustic evaluation [20,24,39,40]. 
Accordingly, six experimental conditions were synthesized via orthog­
onal combination of three diversity levels (LSD, MSD, and HSD) and two 
dominant source contexts: natural versus anthropogenic. Ten elemental 
sources were curated: five natural (birdsong, flowing water, insect 
chirps, wind, and frog croaks) and five anthropogenic (traffic, human 
conversation, children’s play, horn sounds, and construction noise), 
obtained from open-access acoustic repositories (https://www.freesfx. 
co.uk/soundeffects; https://freesound.org). Using Adobe Audition 
2024, these were mixed to achieve distinct Soundscape Diversity Index 
(SDI), Richness Index (SRI), Dominance Index (SDO), and Evenness 
Index (SEI) indices while maintaining consistent source typology per 
condition. Each 15-minute stimulus was normalized for sound pressure

2.1.3. Participants selection
A total of 864 right-handed participants (Male:Female = 1:1.2; Ap­
pendix Table A) were recruited under stringent inclusion criteria: normal 
auditory and cognitive function; non-native English proficiency suffi­
cient for psychometric assessment; capacity for independent procedural 
comprehension and questionnaire completion; stable metabolic status 
(adequate sleep, balanced diet); 3 h pre-testing abstention from psy­
choactive substances (nicotine, caffeine, alcohol); and clean scalp for 
optimal EEG signal acquisition.
2.1.4. Experimental procedure
Experimental data were acquired during two seasonal campaigns (30 
July–4 August and 2–8 November 2025), encompassing 12 clear-sky 
days with eight daily sessions. Consistent sunny and calm conditions 
within each season standardized thermal parameters. Each session 
randomized nine participants across three microclimatic contexts to one 
of six acoustic conditions; participants underwent single-condition 
exposure per session (Appendix Table B). Conditions were counter­
balanced via a modified Latin-square design and evenly distributed 
across contexts [42]. Prior to each session, loudspeakers were concealed 
from view and preloaded with acoustic stimuli. Loudspeaker placement, 
participant distance, exposure duration, and procedural parameters 
were standardized across participants. The protocol comprised three 
standardized phases:
Stage 1: Acclimatization (15 min). Following informed consent and 
EEG electrode placement, participants underwent seated rest with 
standardized visual fixation (viewfinder-controlled landscape element 
ratios) to stabilize autonomic tone. Five-minute basal electroencepha­
lographic recording preceded demographic data collection and triplicate 
autonomic assessment (blood pressure [BP], heart rate [HR], ear tem­
perature [ET]), with mean values derived for baseline metrics.
Stage 2: Stress Induction (5 min). Participants completed an English- 
language stressor task, an established psychophysiological stressor, with 
continuous EEG monitoring. Post-task psychometric evaluation and 
triplicate autonomic measurements followed [43].
Stage 3: Acoustic Restoration (15 min). Randomized exposure to one 
of six soundscape conditions was administered with concurrent EEG 
recording. The protocol concluded with post-intervention psychometric 
assessment and final triplicate autonomic measurements (Fig. 3).

OHM HD2017.2 (Delta OHM, Italy), respectively (Appendix Table C; 
Appendix Table D). All instruments were set to record at 1-min intervals 
and were positioned at a height of 1.1 m above ground level. Mean 
radiant temperature (Tmrt) was calculated according to ISO Standard

2.2.2. Questionnaire survey
The questionnaire comprised three sections: demographic informa­
tion, subjective perception votes, and emotion scales (Appendix Fig. A). 
Demographic data encompassed age, sex, stature, body mass, and 
clothing insulation [44], collected once during thermal acclimatization 
with participants remaining seated. Perceptual assessment included 
Thermal Sensation Vote (TSV; 7-point ASHRAE Standard 55 scale; 
ASHRAE, 2020), Thermal Comfort Vote (TCV; 5-point symmetric scale), 
Soundscape Diversity Vote (SDV; 7-point richness scale), and Sound­
scape Diversity Pleasantness Vote (SPV; 7-point hedonic scale).
Affective states were evaluated using the Restoration Outcome Scale 
(ROS) and Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS). ROS, vali­
dated for environmental restoration research [45,46], comprises six 
items assessing stress regulation, attentional recovery, and cognitive 
clarity (7-point Likert scale; 1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), 
with mean scores representing restorative experience magnitude. 
PANAS quantified orthogonal positive and negative affect via twenty 
adjectives (ten per dimension) on 5-point scales (1 = very slightly/not at 
all, 5 = extremely) [47,48]. Operational clarifications were provided for 
ambiguous items to ensure valid construct measurement.
2.2.3. Physiological measurements
Cardiovascular (systolic/diastolic blood pressure [SBP/DBP], heart 
rate [HR]) and thermal (eardrum temperature [ET]) metrics were 
quantified via automated oscillometry (Omron HEM-7211, Japan) and 
infrared tympanic thermometry (Braun IRT6520), respectively. Blood 
pressure assessment followed standardized seated protocols: arm sup­
ported at heart level with cuff positioned 1–2 cm above the antecubital 
fossa [49]. Tympanic temperature was measured with probe orientation 
toward the nasal bridge to ensure alignment with the tympanic mem­
brane [50]. Triplicate measurements were obtained per condition, with 
mean values retained for statistical analysis.
2.2.4. Psychological measurements
Electroencephalography (EEG) provides validated assessment of 
psychophysiological states [51–53]. Spectral power was quantified 
across four functionally defined frequency bands: δ (1–4 Hz; somno­
lence/fatigue), θ (4–8 Hz; affective processing), α (8–14 Hz; relaxation), 
and β (14–30 Hz; attention/arousal) [52,54]. Neural signals were ac­
quired using a 14-channel EPOC X wireless headset (EMOTIV Inc., USA; 
AF3, AF4, F3, F4, F7, F8, FC5, FC6, T7, T8, P7, P8, O1, O2) with bilateral 
references (CMS, DRL), arranged according to the International 10–20 
system [55]. Data were sampled at 2048 Hz, downsampled to 128 Hz 
(7.8-ms resolution), and wirelessly transmitted to a tablet interface 
(Fig. 4). Throughout recording, participants maintained postural 
immobility and abstained from verbalization and electronic device 
usage to minimize motion and electromagnetic artifacts. Sensor felts

2.3. Thermal index
Mean thermal sensation votes (MTSV) were computed across 
experimental phases (baseline, stress, restoration) within 1 ◦C Physio­
logical Equivalent Temperature (PET) strata to parameterize thermal 
comfort functions [52]. Thermal stress categories were operationalized 
via PET thresholds: slight cold stress (SCS; −1.5 ≤TSV < −0.5; PET 
0.54–15.82 ◦C), no thermal stress (NTS; −0.5 ≤TSV < 0.5; PET 
15.82–31.09 ◦C), slight thermal stress (STS; 0.5 ≤TSV < 1.5; PET 
31.09–46.36 ◦C), and moderate thermal stress (MTS; 1.5 ≤TSV < 2.5; 
PET 46.36–61.63 ◦C) (Fig. 5).
2.4. Statistical analysis
Data preprocessing entailed outlier detection and missing value 
treatment. Differential effects of soundscape diversity across thermal 
stress gradients and dominant source contexts were analyzed via para­
metric (one-way ANOVA) or nonparametric (Kruskal–Wallis) proced­
ures, contingent upon distributional properties (SPSS v26.0).

were retained as influential predictors [62,63].

3. Results
3.1. Thermal and auditory perceptions
3.1.1. TSV and TCV
Kruskal–Wallis analyses revealed sexual dimorphism in thermo- 
acoustic perception contingent upon dominant source context. Within 
natural environments, soundscape diversity did not modulate male TSV 
(p > 0.05) but significantly predicted TCV under STS conditions (p <
0.05). Conversely, under slight cold stress, diversity significantly pre­
dicted female TSV (p < 0.05) without affecting TCV (p > 0.05). In 
anthropogenic- settings, diversity exerted no significant effects on either 
TSV or TCV for either sex (p > 0.05). These results demonstrate that 
soundscape diversity effects on thermal perception are sexually dimor­
phic and context-dependent, with pronounced sex differences emerging 
primarily within nature-dominant acoustic environments (Appendix

Mann–Whitney U analyses revealed sexually antagonistic thermal

Under NTS conditions, MSD and HSD elevated male TSV above LSD

diminished male TSV compared with LSD and HSD, yet elevated female 
TSV. These results demonstrate consistent sexual dimorphism in thermo- 
acoustic perception, wherein acoustic diversity elicits opposing thermal 
perceptual effects across sexes (Fig. 6a, b).
Mann–Whitney U analyses further revealed sexually dimorphic ef­
fects of acoustic diversity on thermal comfort within nature-dominant 
environments. Under NTS conditions, diversity minimally modulated 
male TCV, whereas MSD significantly elevated female thermal comfort 
above LSD and HSD levels, indicating heightened female responsivity to 
acoustic complexity. Under STS conditions, LSD significantly improved 
male TCV relative to MSD (p < 0.05), while exerting negligible effects on 
females, demonstrating reversed sexual dimorphism in thermo-acoustic 
comfort responsivity. During MTS conditions, HSD aggravated male 
thermal discomfort but alleviated female discomfort, revealing sexually 
antagonistic patterns wherein acoustic enrichment elicits opposite 
comfort trajectories across sexes (Fig. 6c, d).
3.1.2. SDV and SPV
Kruskal–Wallis analyses revealed sexually dimorphic perceptual re­
sponses to acoustic diversity contingent upon dominant source context. 
Within natural environments, diversity significantly modulated male 
SDV (SCS and MTS) and SPV (SCS) (p < 0.05), without affecting female 
perception (p > 0.05). Conversely, in anthropogenic-dominant settings, 
diversity significantly influenced SDV in both sexes under SCS, and SPV 
under NTS conditions (p < 0.05), with females exhibiting additional 
significant SDV responsivity (p < 0.001). These results indicate that 
acoustic diversity effects on soundscape perception are sexually 
dimorphic and context-specific, with pronounced sex differences 
emerging primarily within nature-dominant environments (Appendix 
Table F).
Mann–Whitney U analyses revealed sexually dimorphic discrimina­
tory capacity for acoustic diversity within natural environments. Under 
SCS conditions, males exhibited significantly attenuated SDV under LSD 
relative to HSD (p < 0.05), whereas female responsivity was negligible, 
indicating heightened male perceptual acuity for complexity. During 
STS, equivalent discrimination emerged in both sexes. Conversely, 
under MTS conditions, sexually antagonistic patterns emerged: males 
discriminated HSD, whereas females discriminated LSD, suggesting sex- 
specific perceptual anchoring to diversity extremes.
Within anthropogenic-dominant environments, sexual dimorphism 
in acoustic diversity discrimination varied with thermal stress intensity. 
Under SCS conditions, both sexes discriminated LSD from higher di­
versity levels, though males exhibited larger effect sizes than females (p 
< 0.05). During NTS, male responsivity was attenuated, whereas fe­
males discriminated LSD from MSD and HSD with high significance (p <
0.001). This pattern persisted under STS: males showed negligible 
discrimination, while females reliably distinguished LSD from MSD/ 
HSD. Conversely, under MTS conditions, sexually antagonistic patterns 
emerged: males discriminated LSD, whereas females discriminated HSD, 
indicating sex-specific perceptual salience of complexity extremes 
(Fig. 7a, b).
Within anthropogenic-dominant environments, acoustic diversity 
elicited sexually dimorphic hedonic responses across thermal gradients. 
Under SCS conditions, LSD significantly diminished male pleasantness 
relative to MSD (p < 0.05), whereas female SPV exhibited minimal 
diversity-dependent modulation. During NTS, sexually antagonistic 
patterns emerged: HSD attenuated male pleasantness, while LSD atten­
uated female pleasantness, indicating divergent acoustic preferences. 
Conversely, under MTS conditions, HSD enhanced male pleasantness, 
whereas MSD enhanced female pleasantness, revealing sex-specific op­
tima for acoustic complexity (Fig. 7c, d).
3.2. Physiological responses
3.2.1. Systolic blood pressure
One-way ANOVA revealed sexually dimorphic cardiovascular 
responsivity to acoustic diversity. The SBP was significantly modulated 
in males under STS conditions within natural environments (p < 0.05), 
whereas females exhibited no significant modulation (p > 0.05), indi­
cating context-dependent male susceptibility. The DBP showed similar 
sexual dimorphism, with significant male responses under SCS (natural) 
and NTS (anthropogenic) (p < 0.05), contrasting with female non- 
responsivity (p > 0.05). These findings demonstrate that male cardio­
vascular regulation is more sensitive to soundscape diversity than fe­

3.2.2. Heart rate and eardrum temperature
One-way ANOVA revealed sexually dimorphic chronotropic 
responsivity: male HR was significantly modulated under STS within 
natural environments (p < 0.05), whereas female HR exhibited signifi­
cant responsivity under NTS anthropogenic conditions (p < 0.05). 
Conversely, ET showed no significant modulation across conditions (p >
0.05), indicating negligible thermoregulatory influence of acoustic di­
versity (Appendix Table H).
Within natural environments under STS conditions, LSD significantly 
attenuated male HR relative to MSD (p < 0.05). Conversely, under NTS 
anthropogenic conditions, HSD significantly lowered female HR 
compared to LSD (p < 0.05) (Fig. 9a, b). Under SCS and NTS conditions, 
males exhibited minimal tympanic temperature at MSD, whereas fe­
males showed minimal temperatures at HSD. Under STS and MTS con­
ditions, male ET minima shifted to LSD, while females maintained HSD 
optima. Within anthropogenic environments, males consistently 
demonstrated MSD-associated ET minima across thermal gradients, 
whereas females exhibited no clear diversity-dependent thermoregula­
tory pattern (Fig. 9c and d).

4. Discussion
4.1. Thermal and soundscape perceptions
Our findings demonstrate that significant thermo-modulatory effects 
of acoustic diversity were confined to natural contexts, consistent with 
established restorative properties of natural soundscapes [10,64]. 
Anthropogenic acoustic environments, by contrast, elicit negative ap­
praisals that attenuate perceived environmental quality [65], poten­
tially masking the salutogenic effects of acoustic diversity and thereby 
attenuating its thermoregulatory influence.
Within natural contexts under slight cold stress, HSD significantly 
attenuated female subjective cold sensation while exerting minimal 
male effect. This sexual dimorphism may reflect enhanced female 
auditory sensitivity and attentional engagement with environmental 
acoustic detail [66], whereby enriched natural soundscapes capture 
attentional resources and divert cognitive focus from thermal discom­
fort. Conversely, under STS conditions, LSD significantly improved male 
thermal comfort. This pattern likely reflects sex-specific cognitive load 
management: elevated temperatures impair male executive function and 
increase cognitive demands [67], and LSD may provide a lower-load 
acoustic environment that mitigates cognitive stress and enhances 
thermal comfort.
Sex and thermal stress interactively govern acoustic diversity 
perception. Individual attributes substantially modulate soundscape 
appraisal, with sexual dimorphism particularly salient [68,69]. Thermal 
conditions further constrain perceptual processing through affective and 
attentional mechanisms, thereby modulating acoustic evaluation [32,
70].
Within anthropogenic environments, LSD significantly attenuated 
male pleasantness (SPV) under slight cold stress, whereas female 
responsivity remained muted. This sexual dimorphism likely reflects 
inadequate acoustic masking: monotonous LSD insufficiently obscures 
negative sources, amplifying perceived noise exposure persistence and 
degrading male hedonic response [71]. Conversely, females demon­
strate heightened susceptibility to acoustic intensity and noise-related 
attributes, potentially attenuating the regulatory efficacy of diversity

4.3. Psychological effects
Our findings suggest that soundscape diversity may contribute to 
psychological restoration. Within natural environments, thermal esca­
lation potentiated male cortical responsivity to diversity, whereas fe­
males exhibited increasing θ responsivity. This aligns with established 
restorative potentiation under environmental challenge [14,79].
Within anthropogenic environments, male spectral responsivity (δ, θ, 
β) intensified monotonically with thermal stress, whereas females 
demonstrated restricted diversity sensitivity confined to NTS and STS 
conditions. This sexual dimorphism may reflect differential attentional 
resource allocation under environmental load [80]. Moderate stress may 
facilitate external information processing; however, escalating thermal 
challenge may prioritize physiological coping, thereby attenuating 
environmental detail perception [75]. Thermal stress alters EEG dy­
namics and recruits cognitive resources [81], with combined acous­
tic–thermal demands potentially degrading cognitive performance [82].

4.4. Limitations and future directions
This study examined acoustic diversity effects from a sex- 
differentiated perspective. However, the sample comprised university 
students with homogeneous demographic characteristics, potentially 
constraining external validity. Subsequent research should stratify par­
ticipants across age cohorts, occupations, and cultural contexts, incor­
porating anthropometric and metabolic indices (BMI and metabolic 
rate) to elucidate population-specific psychophysiological responsivity. 
Second, acoustic stimuli were experimentally synthesized using 
controlled diversity indices, potentially limiting ecological validity 
relative to in situ acoustic heterogeneity. Future investigations should 
explore extended diversity gradients using ecologically authentic 
soundscapes or immersive virtual environments. Finally, the acute 
exposure protocol precludes assessment of chronic adaptation dynamics. 
Longitudinal or long-term field monitoring designs are warranted to 
characterize cumulative exposure effects, habituation mechanisms, and 
potential interactions with olfactory and visual environmental 
complexity in multisensory urban contexts.
5. Conclusions
This study investigated sexual dimorphism in thermo-acoustic 
responsivity across three microclimatic contexts (open, partially 
shaded, shaded) within an urban green space in Xi'an, China. Thermal 
stress was operationalized via four PET categories. Eight hundred sixty- 
four university students (sex ratio 1:1.2) underwent orthogonal expo­
sure to six acoustic conditions: LSD, MSD and HSD within natural and 
anthropogenic-dominant environments. Multimodal assessment inte­
grated micrometeorological monitoring, psychometric evaluation, car­
diovascular and thermal physiology, and electroencephalography to 
characterize sex-differentiated psychophysiological responsivity. Prin­
cipal findings indicate: 
1) Sexual dimorphism characterized thermo-perceptual responsivity 
predominantly within natural environments: males demonstrated 
significant TCV modulation under STS conditions, SDV sensitivity 
under SCS and MTS conditions, and SPV responsivity under SCS 
conditions (p < 0.05), whereas females exhibited exclusive TSV 
sensitivity under SCS conditions (p < 0.05). Within anthropogenic 
environments, diversity exerted no significant thermo-perceptual 
effects, though SDV responsivity emerged under SCS in both sexes 
(p < 0.05) and SPV under NTS (p < 0.05), with females demon­
strating heightened SDV sensitivity (p < 0.001).
2) Cardiovascular responsivity demonstrated sexual dimorphism and 
context-dependency, whereas ET responses were non-significant. 
Within natural environments under STS conditions, males exhibi­
ted significant hemodynamic attenuation: SBP decreased 6.08 
mmHg (MSD vs. LSD) and DBP decreased 6.61 mmHg (HSD vs. LSD; 
p < 0.05). Females demonstrated significant chronotropic reduction 
exclusively under NTS anthropogenic conditions (HR decreased 5.32 
bpm, HSD vs. LSD; p < 0.05).
3) Under MTS within natural environments, neuropsychological resto­
ration exhibited sex-convergent efficacy with divergent neural sig­
natures. LSD significantly attenuated male frontal δ power while 
enhancing occipital α power (p < 0.05), concomitant with improved 
attention, stress recovery, and affective valence. Females exhibited 
elevated θ power (p < 0.05) with parallel improvements in stress 
recovery, cognition, and affective state. Within anthropogenic en­
vironments, diversity effects on psychological states were more 
heterogeneous.
4) Thermal stress gradients elicited sexually dimorphic, domain- 
specific diversity effects. Within biophonic environments, male 
thermal perception strengthened monotonically with escalating 
stress, whereas females exhibited biphasic (attenuation-recovery) 
trajectories; soundscape perception followed biphasic patterns 
across sexes. Physiologically, male responsivity attenuated while 
female responsivity intensified; psychologically, males maintained 
heightened sensitivity, whereas females demonstrated biphasic tra­
jectories. Within anthropogenic environments, male thermal 
perception strengthened monotonically, whereas females exhibited 
inverted-U patterns; soundscape perception demonstrated inverted- 
U trajectories in males and monotonic attenuation in females. 
Males exhibited greater overall psychophysiological sensitivity; fe­
males demonstrated U-shaped physiological responsivity and pro­
gressively intensifying psychological responsivity.
