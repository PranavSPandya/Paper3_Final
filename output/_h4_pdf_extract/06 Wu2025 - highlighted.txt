A B S T R A C T
Urban parks play a crucial role in enhancing residents’ quality of life; however, research on the combined effects 
of thermal and acoustic environments on subjective evaluation remains limited. This study explores these in­
teractions in cold-region urban parks, focusing on People’s Park in Zhangjiakou, China. A combination of field 
measurements and questionnaire surveys was conducted across three representative spaces characterized by 
three common sound types: human-generated, artificial, and natural sounds. The results indicate that, in terms of 
thermal perception, natural sounds significantly reduce heat stress by lowering the thermal sensation vote and 
improving thermal comfort, whereas artificial sounds exacerbate it. Additionally, high sound pressure levels 
(LAeq ≥60 dB(A)) exacerbate thermal discomfort, making individuals feel hotter in summer and colder in winter, 
thereby reducing thermal comfort. Regarding acoustic perception, a thermally comfortable environment en­
hances the acoustic sensation vote and improves acoustic comfort. For overall comfort, during summer and 
transitional seasons, the overall comfort vote is negatively correlated with both the Universal Thermal Climate 
Index (UTCI) and sound pressure levels, while in winter, overall comfort vote is positively correlated with UTCI 
but negatively correlated with sound pressure levels. The analysis of equivalent overall comfort vote further 
reveals that in summer and transitional seasons, rising temperatures under extreme heat conditions (UTCI ≥
35◦C) have a more pronounced negative impact on overall comfort vote than decreasing temperatures in winter. 
Conversely, in winter, variations in sound pressure levels exert a stronger influence on overall comfort vote 
compared to those in summer and transitional seasons. This study addresses a research gap in understanding the 
interaction between thermal and acoustic environments in cold-region urban parks and provides valuable in­
sights for the future planning and optimization of urban green spaces in such climates.
1. Introduction
Throughout human history, outdoor spaces have played an indis­
pensable role in enhancing quality of life, improving residential comfort, 
and fostering social interactions [1]. High-quality outdoor environments 
not only provide opportunities for contact with nature and physical and 
mental relaxation but also help alleviate psychological stress and 
physical fatigue, thereby reducing the risk of related health issues [2]. 
However, the comfort of outdoor environments is influenced by a 
complex interplay of multiple environmental factors, which often 
interact in dynamic and sometimes contradictory ways [3]. A change in 
any single factor can trigger cascading effects that ultimately impact 
overall environmental comfort [4]. Key environmental components that 
influence comfort include, but are not limited to, thermal conditions 
[5,6], acoustic environments [7,8], lighting [9,10], and air quality 
[11,12]. Among these, thermal and acoustic environments have 
received particular attention due to their frequent and pervasive dis­
turbances. Thermal discomfort is widely recognized as a major 
constraint on individuals’ willingness to engage in outdoor activities 
[13]. Adverse thermal conditions can impair thermoregulation, induce 
heat stress, and significantly reduce the desire for outdoor participation, 
thereby negatively affecting quality of life [14]. In the context of 
intensifying urban heat island effects [15], improving thermal comfort 
in outdoor spaces has become increasingly critical. Similarly, an unfa­
vorable acoustic environment not only causes direct harm to the audi­
tory system but also triggers stress responses and affects emotional 
states, leading to long-term negative impacts on physical and mental 
health [16,17]. Studies have shown that sound satisfaction is a crucial 
component of overall satisfaction with outdoor spaces [18]. Compared 
to factors such as lighting and air quality, acoustic discomfort has a more 
immediate and pronounced negative impact on overall comfort [19]. In

on outdoor space comfort, as well as their impact on human behavior

Extensive research has been conducted on the individual effects of 
thermal and acoustic environments in outdoor spaces, leading to the 
establishment of relevant building thermal environment standards, 
acoustic technical indices, and design guidelines [22,23]. These studies 
and standards provide a scientific basis for improving comfort related to 
specific environmental factors. However, in real-life settings, the envi­
ronment is a complex system influenced by multiple interacting factors 
rather than isolated elements acting independently [24–28]. Despite 
significant progress in understanding thermal and acoustic environ­
ments separately, research on environmental evaluation under multi- 
factor interactions remains insufficient.
In recent years, increasing attention has been given to the study of 
thermal-acoustic interactions, with various environmental contexts 
explored to understand their underlying mechanisms, as summarized in 
Table 1. However, a review of the existing literature reveals significant 
research gaps in this field. First, most studies have focused on temperate 
and tropical regions, while research on cold regions remains limited. 
This gap hinders a comprehensive understanding of the unique char­
acteristics of thermal-acoustic interactions under extreme climatic 
conditions. Second, previous studies have predominantly examined 
urban functional areas such as plazas and campuses, whereas limited 
attention has been given to urban parks in cold regions, which serve as 
essential spaces for both ecological conservation and recreational ac­
tivities. Furthermore, existing research primarily investigates the inde­
pendent effects of thermal and acoustic environments on comfort, with 
relatively superficial discussions on the interaction mechanisms be­
tween these factors. Moreover, existing studies have predominantly 
focused on the independent effects of either thermal or acoustic envi­
ronments on comfort, while research on the interactive mechanisms 
between the two remains relatively limited. Although recent studies 
have begun to address thermal perceptions under dynamic environ­
mental conditions [29], comprehensive investigations that incorporate 
both dynamic variability and seasonal differences across broader pop­
ulations and diverse outdoor settings are still limited. Urban parks in 
cold regions of China exhibit unique environmental complexities due to 
distinct seasonal variations and frequent extreme winter weather events. 
The impact of thermal-acoustic environments on human behavior and 
psychological comfort under low-temperature and snow-covered con­
ditions remains an underexplored yet critical research area. Addition­
ally, current studies tend to focus on single sensory dimensions, such as 
thermal or acoustic comfort, while overlooking the multisensory in­
teractions among thermal, acoustic, and visual environments. This 
limitation restricts the ability to identify key factors influencing overall 
human comfort in outdoor spaces.
Against the backdrop of China’s “dual carbon” goals and the push for 
green urban development, urban parks in cold regions play a crucial role 
as public spaces, offering opportunities for relaxation, recreation, and 
physical activity [30]. These parks significantly contribute to improving 
public health and overall well-being. A deeper investigation into the 
thermal-acoustic interactions in urban parks in cold regions can not only 
bridge existing research gaps but also provide scientific insights for 
enhancing environmental comfort, increasing the attractiveness of out­
door spaces during winter, and supporting the sustainable development 
of cold-region cities [31]. Therefore, studying the interaction mecha­
nisms of thermal-acoustic environments in urban parks in cold regions is 
of great theoretical significance and urgent practical value.
This study focuses on an urban park in Zhangjiakou, a representative 
cold-region city in China, selecting three typical open spaces with 
distinct soundscape characteristics to comprehensively investigate the 
combined effects of thermal and acoustic environments on human 
perception and comfort under different seasonal conditions. By inte­
grating field measurements with questionnaire surveys, this research 
aims to reveal the mechanisms through which thermal-acoustic envi­
ronments influence multisensory experiences in urban parks in cold 
regions. The specific objectives of this study are as follows: (1) To 
analyze the effects of various thermal and acoustic conditions in open 
spaces within urban parks in cold regions on thermal and acoustic 
comfort. This will help identify patterns of thermal and acoustic 
perception across different environmental conditions, providing funda­
mental data to support improvements in outdoor comfort in cold re­
gions. (2) To investigate the interaction between thermal and acoustic 
perception in open spaces of urban parks in cold regions. This includes 
analyzing the dynamic relationships between thermal and acoustic 
comfort, as well as their mutual regulatory effects. By clarifying the 
synergistic or inhibitory mechanisms in complex environmental set­
tings, this study aims to establish a theoretical foundation for 
multisensory-integrated environmental design. (3) To quantify the in­
fluence of thermal perception, acoustic perception, and their in­
teractions on subjective human experiences from an overall comfort 
perspective. This will enable the exploration of the integrative effects of 
multidimensional environmental perception in cold-region urban parks 
and inform specific strategies for optimizing thermal-acoustic 
environments.
The ultimate goal of this study is to develop a scientifically grounded 
thermal-acoustic interaction model based on the unique climatic and 
soundscape characteristics of cold regions. This model will provide 
theoretical support and technical guidance for creating comfortable 
environments under combined thermal-acoustic effects in open spaces of 
urban parks in cold regions. By addressing existing research gaps, this 
study will also offer valuable insights for the planning and optimization 
of green spaces in cold-region cities.

This study was conducted in People’s Park, Zhangjiakou 
(114◦53′24″E, 40◦49′33″N), where meteorological measurements and 
questionnaire surveys were carried out. Covering an area of 104,300 m2, 
the park features diverse landscape elements, convenient accessibility,

investigating the interaction between thermal and acoustic comfort in

Urban sounds can be broadly categorized into three types based on 
their sources: natural sounds, human-generated sounds, and artificial 
sounds [45]. Considering the diversity of landscape features and 
thermal-acoustic environments, three representative spaces within the 
park were selected: the fitness activity area (P1), the ecological recrea­
tion area (P2), and the central waterscape area (P3). These areas exhibit 
distinct environmental characteristics. The ecological recreation area 
(P2) is predominantly shaded by trees, with natural sounds such as 
birdsong and insect chirping being the dominant auditory elements. The 
central waterscape area (P3) contains extensive water bodies with some 
vegetation and is located near urban roads, where artificial sounds such 
as traffic noise and construction machinery are prominent. In contrast, 
the fitness activity area (P1) has minimal tree cover, and the primary 
environmental sounds include human-generated noises such as con­
versation and exercise-related activities. Details of these selected spaces 
are presented in Fig. 1. These three areas reflect the typical spatial and 
acoustic heterogeneity of urban parks, providing a suitable basis for 
examining the interplay between thermal and acoustic environments 
and their impact on public perception and comfort.

To ensure temporal synchronization and data comparability across 
different sites, identical sets of meteorological and acoustic monitoring 
instruments were installed at each measurement point (see Table 2). The 
equipment was fixed in place throughout the measurement period and 
used to continuously record key environmental parameters, including 
air temperature (Ta), relative humidity (RH), wind speed (Va), globe

Thermal environmental parameters were collected at one-minute 
intervals, while acoustic data were sampled every 10 s and summa­
rized in 10-minute intervals as a full measurement cycle. To enhance the 
alignment between subjective evaluations and objective measurements, 
questionnaire surveys were conducted shortly before or after each 
hourly acoustic sampling cycle (e.g., 10:30, 11:30, etc.). Respondents 
were positioned within 2 m of the monitoring equipment to ensure 
consistency between their perceived thermal-acoustic environment and 
the measured data. Each questionnaire included precise time and 
location stamps, allowing for accurate matching of subjective responses 
with the corresponding microclimatic and acoustic conditions for sub­
sequent model analysis.
2.2.2. Questionnaire survey
This study conducted on-site surveys in three representative func­
tional zones (P1, P2, and P3) of People’s Park in Zhangjiakou. To ensure 
the consistency between subjective survey data and objective environ­
mental measurements, all questionnaires were distributed and collected 
within the immediate vicinity of each monitoring site, with respondents 
positioned no more than 2 m from the instruments. Investigators 
approached passersby near the equipment and invited them to complete 
the questionnaires through face-to-face interaction. This approach 
ensured that participants’ subjective evaluations reflected the same 
microclimatic and acoustic conditions being measured by the moni­
toring devices, thereby strengthening the reliability and explanatory 
power of the relationship between environmental variables and human 
perception.
As summarized in Table 3, the questionnaire consisted of the 
following components: (1) demographic and personal information, 
including gender, age, height, weight, clothing details (upper garment, 
lower garment, footwear), current activity status (e.g., sitting, standing, 
walking, exercising), and length of time spent at the survey location; (2) 
subjective environmental assessments, including thermal sensation vote 
(TSV, 9-point scale from −4 to +4), acoustic sensation vote (ASV), 
acoustic comfort vote (ACV), overall comfort vote (OCV), thermal 
comfort vote (TCV) (on a 7-point scale from −3 to +3 [46]). The surveys

2.3. Environmental evaluation indicators
2.3.1. Thermal environment evaluation indicators
The Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) was adopted in this 
study as the primary metric for evaluating outdoor thermal conditions 
[48]. Developed by the International Society of Biometeorology (ISB), 
the UTCI integrates multiple meteorological variables—including air 
temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, and mean radiant temper­
ature—alongside individual physiological characteristics such as age, 
gender, height, weight, clothing insulation, and metabolic rate, to 
simulate the human body’s comprehensive thermal stress response to 
outdoor environments.
To ensure temporal and spatial alignment between environmental 
conditions and subjective thermal perception, the date, exact time, and 
geographic coordinates of each questionnaire response were recorded 
during the survey. These inputs, along with the respondents’ individual 
characteristics and real-time meteorological data (including air tem­
perature, relative humidity, wind speed, and mean radiant tempera­
ture), were used as inputs for the Rayman model [49] to calculate the 
UTCI corresponding to each respondent’s microclimatic exposure at the 
time of survey completion. This method enabled precise matching be­
tween objective environmental data and subjective thermal responses, 
thereby improving the accuracy and scientific validity of the model 
analysis.

values ranged from −27.8 ◦C to 42.2 ◦C. Notably, no values within the 
0 ◦C to 9 ◦C range were recorded, resulting in the representation of seven

Table 4. According to Table 5, no UTCI values were observed within the

2.3.2. Acoustic environment evaluation indicators
Given the discontinuous nature of sound sources at the measurement 
sites, this study utilized the equivalent continuous sound level (LAeq) as 
the primary acoustic environment evaluation metric. The daytime LAeq 
levels in the urban park ranged from 44.4 dB(A) to 71.2 dB(A) 
throughout the year. These values were classified into four categories in 
10 dB increments: 40 dB(A)–50 dB(A), 50 dB(A)–60 dB(A), 60 dB(A)–70 
dB(A), and above 70 dB(A).
2.3.3. Overall environmental evaluation indicators
Common indicators for overall environmental assessment include 
satisfaction level, annoyance level, and overall comfort. In this study, 
overall comfort was selected as the primary evaluation metric. A ques­
tionnaire survey was conducted among urban park users to assess their 
perceptions of overall comfort in the park environment.
2.4. Statistical analysis
Data preprocessing, including counting, classification, and cleaning, 
was conducted using Microsoft Excel. Subsequent statistical analyses 
were carried out using SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM), MATLAB, and Origin. A 
combination of correlation analysis, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and 
multiple regression analysis was applied to integrate and interpret both 
field measurements and questionnaire data.
Multiple linear regression models were developed to investigate the 
relationship between OCV and environmental variables across three 
seasonal contexts: summer, transitional seasons, and winter. The 
selected independent variables included the UTCI, equivalent contin­
uous sound level (LAeq), TCV, and ACV. Model performance was assessed 
using the coefficient of determination (R2), and multicollinearity di­
agnostics were conducted prior to regression modeling to ensure the 
validity of assumptions.
To assess the robustness and generalizability of the models, 10-fold 
cross-validation was performed. For each seasonal dataset, the data 
were randomly partitioned into 10 subsets. In each iteration, one subset 
was used for validation and the remaining nine for training. This process 
was repeated 10 times, and the mean and standard deviation of R2 across 
the folds were computed to evaluate model stability.

4. Discussion
4.1. Combined effects of temperature and sound type
Our study found that sound type significantly influenced TSV and 
TCV. Artificial sounds intensified subjective TSV in hot environments 
while mitigating the perception of cold in cold environments, consistent 
with previous findings [33]. In hot conditions, natural sounds alleviated 
subjective TSV and enhanced thermal comfort. Jin et al. [32] observed 
that bird songs significantly reduced TSV in hot environments, which 
aligns with our results. Sound type also had a significant impact on TCV. 
In hot environments, artificial sounds decreased subjective TCV, exac­
erbating thermal discomfort, whereas natural sounds increased subjec­
tive TCV, alleviating thermal discomfort. These findings are consistent 
with those of Jin et al. [32,50], possibly because artificial sounds are 
often associated with noisy, heat-intensive work environments, ampli­
fying discomfort, whereas natural sounds evoke images of cooling 
breezes, thereby reducing thermal distress.
ACV significantly affected subjective TSV and TCV. In both warm 
and hot environments, a comfortable soundscape reduced TSV. 
Regardless of thermal conditions, a comfortable sound environment 
significantly enhanced TCV. This effect may stem from the psychological 
benefits of an acoustically pleasant environment, which fosters a more 
positive mental state and increases tolerance to external conditions. TCV 
represents a complex perceptual experience that depends not only on 
physiological factors such as thermoregulation and skin sensation but 
also on psychological influences. These psychological effects are shaped 
by multisensory stimuli, individual preferences, and a range of cognitive 
mechanisms [51]. Psychological factors, in turn, can affect human 
behavior and interactions with the surrounding environment [52].
ASV and ACV were primarily influenced by the acoustic environ­
ment, but certain sound types were also affected by thermal conditions, 
in line with previous research [53–55]. Under natural sound exposure, 
ASV decreased as temperature increased, indicating that higher tem­
peratures made the environment feel noisier. This phenomenon may be 
attributed to the fact that excessive heat induces irritability, leading to a 
more negative psychological state toward the surroundings. A similar 
trend was observed for ACV in natural sound environments, where ACV 
was highest under neutral or moderate thermal stress, but declined as 
thermal conditions became either excessively hot or cold. This is 
consistent with Jin et al.’s findings that both high and low temperatures 
exacerbate acoustic discomfort [50].
In the transitional season, TCV significantly influenced subjective 
ASV and ACV. Across artificial, natural, and mechanical sound envi­
ronments, higher TCV corresponded to higher ASV, suggesting that in­
dividuals who felt more thermally comfortable perceived their 
surroundings as quieter, whereas those experiencing thermal discomfort 
found their environment noisier. This may be because tolerance to noise 
decreases as thermal discomfort increases. In natural sound environ­
ments, ACV increased with TCV, indicating that individuals who felt 
thermally comfortable also perceived their sound environment as more 
pleasant. However, when TCV ≤0, ACV remained at a low level, 
implying that individuals who experienced thermal discomfort also 
perceived greater acoustic discomfort. Chinazzo [56] posited that 
human sensory perception is inherently multimodal, meaning that 
environmental perception is influenced by multiple sensory inputs 
simultaneously, a conclusion that aligns with the findings of this study. 
However, the correlations between thermal and acoustic perception 
were noticeably weaker in summer and winter. Under such extreme 
thermal conditions, perception tends to be dominated by thermal 
sensation itself, leaving limited room for the acoustic environment to 
modulate TSV, TCV, ASV, or ACV.
4.2. Combined effects of temperature and sound pressure level
Our study demonstrated that SPL significantly influenced TSV and 
TCV across all three seasons. In hot environments, TSV increased with 
rising SPL, whereas in cold environments, TSV decreased as SPL 
increased. This suggests that loud sounds amplify the perception of heat 
in summer and cold in autumn and winter. In contrast, Guan [57] found 
that TSV remained unchanged despite variations in SPL, likely because 
their experiment was conducted within a near-comfort thermal range. In 
summer, TCV was lowest when SPL ranged from 60 to 70 dB(A), with an 
average range of 0.4. In winter, TCV increased as SPL decreased, indi­
cating that excessive SPL reduces thermal comfort, a finding consistent 
with Yang et al. [58].
UTCI significantly influenced ASV in summer and ACV in both 
summer and winter. Nagano and Horikoshi [59] reported that thermal- 
acoustic interactions significantly affect auditory perception, aligning 
with our results. Specifically, in summer, when heat stress levels were

4.3. Evaluation of equivalent OCV under different conditions
Our findings indicate that thermal, acoustic, and combined thermal- 
acoustic environments significantly influence OCV, consistent with 
previous studies [42,61]. In the study by Nagano and Horikoshi [62], 
OCV decreased as ambient temperature increased within the range of 
27◦C to 39◦C, while within the range of 19◦C to 28◦C, OCV increased 
accordingly. Furthermore, when either the thermal or acoustic envi­
ronment reached extreme conditions, OCV remained in an uncomfort­
able state regardless of variations in other environmental factors. To 
process the OCV data, we applied Kriging interpolation, a surrogate 
modeling technique that interpolates limited data points to simulate 
results across a global domain [63,64].

4.4. OCV model
To accurately quantify the relationships between different environ­
mental factors and overall comfort, mathematical models and evalua­
tion formulas can be utilized. In this study, a comprehensive dataset was 
obtained through field measurements and questionnaire surveys, 
enabling the construction of an environmental comfort evaluation 
model using regression analysis. The model was developed to assess 
overall comfort by integrating both objective environmental parameters 
and subjective perceptions reported by participants.

Effect of the thermal environment (UTCI): In both summer and the

increase of 1 ◦C in UTCI led to a 0.339-unit reduction in OCV. 
Conversely, in winter, UTCI exhibited a positive correlation with OCV,

(with a 1 ◦C increase resulting in a 0.136-unit rise in OCV), which aligns 
with people’s preference for warmer conditions in cold climates.

LAeq showed a consistent negative correlation with OCV, demonstrating 
that increased noise levels reduced perceived overall comfort regardless 
of climate conditions. The magnitude of this effect varied seasonally, 
with the strongest impact observed in summer (a 1  dB(A) increase in

(a 0.066-unit decline). Together, these models confirm the co-regulatory 
effects of thermal and acoustic environments on subjective comfort, 
with their relative influence varying by season.
To evaluate the robustness and generalization capability of the 
regression models across different seasons, we performed 10-fold cross- 
validation for each of the models developed for summer, autumn, and 
winter datasets. The R2 values from each fold were recorded to assess 
predictive performance and stability. The summer model achieved a 
mean R2 of 0.711 with a standard deviation of 0.044, indicating stable 
performance. The autumn model yielded a mean R2 of 0.706 and a 
standard deviation of 0.106, suggesting good overall performance with 
some variability between folds. The winter model produced R2 values 
ranging from 0.377 to 0.882, with a mean R2 of 0.744 and a standard 
deviation of 0.140, closely aligning with the training R2 of 0.770. This 
indicates that the model generalizes well to new data. In conclusion, the 
models exhibited stable and consistent performance across seasonal 
datasets, supporting their effectiveness and strong generalization 
capabilities.

4.5. Limitations
Although this study systematically examined the impact of thermal- 
acoustic environments on overall comfort in cold-region urban parks 
and classified sounds into natural, human-induced, and artificial types, 
several limitations remain:
First, spatial representativeness is limited. While 798 valid ques­
tionnaires were collected across three seasons with balanced gender and 
age distributions, most data came from Zhangjiakou, with over 90 % of 
participants being local residents, limiting geographic and cultural 
diversity.
Second, comfort perception is highly subjective and context- 
dependent, influenced by activity type, psychological expectations, 
and social settings. Although objective indicators such as UTCI and LAeq 
were introduced and survey periods controlled, external variability 
could not be fully eliminated.
Third, environmental variables such as lighting and air quality were 
not included, and the current sound classification is coarse, limiting the 
ability to capture the complexity of park soundscapes. Moreover, the 
distribution of different sound types across specific sound pressure levels 
(e.g., 40–50 dB, 60–70 dB) was not analyzed. Due to the use of overall 
LAeq measurements without source recognition systems, quantitative 
associations between sound types and sound levels could not be 
established.
Future research will: (1) expand the sample scope to include diverse 
urban spaces across different climates and cultural contexts to improve 
model generalizability; (2) incorporate more refined sound classifica­
tions and additional environmental parameters (e.g., lighting, air

5. Conclusions
In this study, three representative spaces within an urban park in 
Zhangjiakou, a cold-region city in China, were selected to investigate 
people’s subjective perception of the thermal-acoustic environment. By 
integrating on-site meteorological measurements with questionnaire 
surveys, this study explored the combined effects of the thermal-acoustic 
interactive environment on subjective evaluations in urban parks 
located in cold regions of China. The key findings are as follows: 
1. Thermal Perception: Sound type and sound pressure level had sig­
nificant effects on TSV and TCV. In hot environments, exposure to 
natural sounds significantly reduced subjective TSV and improved 
TCV. High sound pressure levels (LAeq ≥60 dB(A)) increased TSV in 
summer but decreased it in winter while reducing TCV. Additionally, 
the more extreme the thermal environment, the greater the impact of 
sound pressure level on TSV.
2. Acoustic Perception: The influence of UTCI on ASV was significant 
only in summer, where ASV increased with UTCI. The effect of UTCI 
on ACV was significant in both summer and winter, with ACV 
increasing as UTCI rose. In a natural sound environment, ASV 
decreased as temperature increased, while both extremely low and 
high temperatures led to higher acoustic discomfort. A thermally 
comfortable environment contributed to higher ASV and increased 
ACV.
3. Overall Comfort: Both thermal and acoustic environments had sig­
nificant effects on OCV. In summer and transitional seasons, OCV 
exhibited a negative correlation with both UTCI and sound pressure 
level. In winter, OCV was positively correlated with UTCI but 
negatively correlated with sound pressure level.
4. Thermal-Acoustic Effects in Cold-Region Urban Parks: The impact of 
high-temperature environments (UTCI ≥35◦C) on OCV during 
summer and transitional seasons was greater than that of low tem­
peratures in winter. Conversely, variations in sound pressure level 
had a more pronounced effect on OCV in winter than in summer and 
transitional seasons. Specifically, in summer, when UTCI exceeded 
42◦C, OCV remained below the “moderate” level regardless of sound 
level variations. During transitional seasons, when UTCI exceeded 
38◦C, OCV remained below “moderate” irrespective of park noise 
levels. In winter, when UTCI fell below –22◦C or LAeq exceeded 63 dB 
(A), OCV remained below “moderate” regardless of other environ­
mental conditions.
