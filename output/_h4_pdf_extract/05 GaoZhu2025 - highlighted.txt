A B S T R A C T
As environmental pressures from high-density urbanization increase, the comfort of outdoor spaces and their 
restorative benefits for urban residents have become increasingly important. Although thermal and acoustic 
environments are key factors influencing outdoor comfort, evidence linking thermal and acoustic comfort with 
restorative benefits is scarce. This study explores the impact of the thermal-acoustic environment on psycho­
logical restorative benefits in urban greenways through on-site meteorological measurements and questionnaire 
surveys. The results indicate that, during the hot season, compared to densely vegetated greenways, sparsely 
vegetated greenways are perceived as hotter and noisier. Furthermore, the relationship between the thermal- 
acoustic environment and restorative benefits is more strongly correlated in sparsely vegetated greenways 
than in densely vegetated ones. In other words, the environmental comfort in different types of greenways can be 
targeted for improvement. Secondly, the interaction between thermal and acoustic environments influences 
restorative benefits. At the same sound pressure levels, restorative benefits decrease as the Universal Thermal 
Climate Index (UTCI) increases. However, on sparsely vegetated greenways, under conditions of 70–75 dB(A), 
the increase in UTCI does not significantly affect restoration. Finally, to identify the factors hindering psycho­
logical restoration, the study compares the veto power of thermal-acoustic comfort voting (TCV, ACV). This veto 
power varies according to greenway type. In densely vegetated greenways, ACV has greater veto power than 
TCV, while in sparsely vegetated greenways, the reverse is true. The findings offer valuable insights for urban 
planners and policymakers, suggesting that creating comfortable thermal-acoustic environments in outdoor 
spaces can enhance restorative benefits and contribute to the sustainability of high-density urban areas.
1. Introduction
The rapid pace of urbanization has intensified environmental pres­
sures on residents living in high-density cities, such as noise pollution 
and heatwaves [1,2], contributing to increased rates of anxiety and 
cardiovascular diseases [3]. Consequently, the renewal and redevelop­
ment of urban outdoor spaces have been increasingly employed to 
address the challenges posed by highly fragmented spatial patterns, 
gradually becoming a central issue in urban planning literature and 
practice worldwide [4]. The comfort of these spaces not only affects the 
psychological restoration of outdoor users but also contributes to the 
livability and sustainability of cities [5,6]. Among various environ­
mental factors, thermal and acoustic conditions are primary 
determinants of the comfort of urban open spaces [7].
Urban green spaces, as an indispensable component of outdoor 
spaces, are becoming increasingly important and urgent due to their 
restorative benefits. On one hand, exposure to nature in cities can alle­
viate stress [8], while on the other, it supports outdoor recreational 
activities that promote positive emotions [9]. Urban greenways, a key 
type of urban green space [10], are particularly significant in 
high-density cities due to their multifunctionality [11]. They provide 
multidimensional benefits to cities, such as increasing the density of 
green areas within strict spatial constraints, offering public spaces for 
leisure and sports activities, and improving community quality of life 
[12]. However, in high-density cities in subtropical regions, the poten­
tial role of urban greenways in promoting environmental restoration

recreational activities, the thermal and acoustic environments of urban 
greenways may impact the psychological restoration of users [13]. As a 
result, urban planners and policymakers around the world are increas­
ingly focused on maximizing the comfort of outdoor spaces with limited 
resources, whether through greenway planning or large-scale redevel­

This study employs linear regression models and variance analysis 
applied to field data collected from urban greenways in Shenzhen, a 
high-density city in China, aiming to address three research questions: 
• Research Question 1 (RQ1): What are the differences in thermal- 
acoustic perception between two greenway spaces with contrasting 
vegetation density?
• Research Question 2 (RQ2): How do thermal-acoustic environments 
influence psychological restoration across these two greenway 
spaces?
• Research Question 3 (RQ3): Do thermal and acoustic environments 
exert equal influence on psychological restoration in these greenway 
spaces?
This research makes threefold contributions to the literature on 
urban greenway planning and the practice of creating comfortable 
outdoor environments. First, it utilizes a unique field-based approach to 
investigate residents’ perceptions of thermal and acoustic comfort in 
urban greenways within high-density environments. By integrating 
direct measurements of environmental conditions, the study provides 
nuanced, data-driven insights to guide practical interventions and 
inform sustainable outdoor design strategies. Second, the study explores 
the relationship between thermal-acoustic comfort and psychological 
restoration from a sensory interaction perspective. This perspective 
highlights how specific sensory conditions can disproportionately in­
fluence users’ overall experiences, thus broadening planners’ under­
standing of the complex interplay between environmental factors and 
psychological outcomes. The findings encourage further examination of 
multisensory dynamics in both theoretical frameworks and practical 
applications. Third, by analyzing variations in vegetation density within 
greenways, the study clarifies how different thermal-acoustic environ­
ments affect restorative outcomes. Addressing an under-examined 
aspect of urban greenway research, this work proposes an alternative 
to traditional approaches that rely solely on physical modifications. By 
explicitly connecting sensory experiences to psychological restoration, 
the results offer practical guidance for planners and policymakers 
seeking to enhance urban sustainability and improve quality-of-life 
outcomes through greenway design.
2. Literature review
2.1. The importance of urban greenways as outdoor spaces
Compared to traditional parks, urban greenways are often more 
suitable for recreational activities [14]. Their environmental design has 
become an important entry point for actively intervening in public 
health [15]. In recent years, the development of greenways has been 
vigorously promoted. As a form of small-scale linear green space, 
greenways play a crucial role for residents in high-density urban areas, 
providing essential spaces for daily activities [11]. They offer urban 
residents more convenient outdoor spaces and contribute to the creation 
of comfortable, livable environments [10]. Furthermore, high-quality 
urban greenways enhance exposure to the natural environment, 
thereby reducing perceived environmental stress and alleviating anxiety 
and depression [16], serving as restorative environments that promote 
both physical and mental well-being.
Research on restorative environments is largely anchored in two 
theoretical frameworks: Attention Restoration Theory (ART) and Stress 
Reduction Theory (SRT) [17,18]. According to SRT, exposure to natural 
settings helps alleviate stress and calm arousal, thereby promoting rapid 
recovery from acute stress [18]. In contrast, ART posits that 
non-directed attention activities—such as immersing oneself in nature­
—can restore directed attention, which is vital for tasks requiring 
focused effort [17]. While the restorative benefits of urban green spaces 
have been extensively documented, the specific attributes of greenways 
may influence their use through environmental perceptions [19], ulti­
mately enhancing residents’ well-being. Although the restorative effects 
of traditional, large-scale green spaces have been emphasized, ongoing 
urban land transformation and fragmented spatial patterns, coupled 
with the growing demand for accessible daily spaces [20], highlight the 
necessity and urgency of studying outdoor spaces—particularly green­
ways in high-density urban areas [11].
2.2. The impact of environmental comfort on psychological restoration
The comfort of outdoor environments has a positive influence on 
psychological well-being and restoration, a relationship that also in­
forms urban design efforts [5,21]. However, much of the existing 
research on urban outdoor comfort has focused primarily on 
single-sensory perceptions [22,23], which may be insufficient for 
addressing environmental challenges in rapidly urbanizing contexts 
[24]. This narrow focus can also underestimate the role of sensory 
interaction [25,26]; by contrast, a multisensory experience is considered 
an effective intervention to enhance restorative outcomes [27]. In other 
words, multisensory perception is not merely the sum of individual 
sensory inputs but rather a composite effect that operates holistically 
[28]. For example, in summer, thermal comfort and acoustic comfort 
can interact, where high temperatures exacerbate acoustic discomfort, 
collectively influencing overall comfort [29]. Moreover, outdoor ther­
mal comfort is often evaluated using the Universal Thermal Climate 
Index (UTCI) [30], owing to its robust accuracy and applicability in 
complex urban microclimates [31]. Meanwhile, the comfort of outdoor 
spaces is frequently assessed through their acoustic environment [32]. 
Specifically, the A-weighted equivalent sound pressure level (LAeq) is 
employed to characterize the intensity of ambient sound, and measures 
of acoustic comfort and preference are used to evaluate psychological 
impacts [33]. Consequently, there is a clear need to conduct a joint 
assessment of environmental comfort from both thermal and acoustic 
perspectives.
Although environmental comfort under multisensory interaction has 
been investigated [34], its relationship with psychological restoration in 
outdoor spaces remains largely unexplored. In addition, while acoustic 
comfort may enhance restorative benefits [35], variations in acoustic 
type and loudness under the same temperature conditions can shift 
thermal comfort evaluations [36,37]. These insights underscore the 
interactive effects of thermal and acoustic environments, but often 
overlook whether each factor contributes equally to perceived restora­
tion. Prior research has introduced the notion of veto power—a situation 
in which a single severely negative stimulus, such as excessive noise or 
extreme heat, can override otherwise favorable conditions and sub­
stantially reduce overall satisfaction [25], prompting the question of 
whether a similar veto effect exists in the thermal-acoustic environment 
with respect to psychological restoration. Moreover, current research on 
thermal-acoustic comfort predominantly takes place in laboratory set­
tings [37], potentially restricting our understanding of how complex 
environmental factors and psychological perceptions intertwine in green 
spaces. This gap is particularly pressing in high-density urban contexts, 
where visitors to green spaces encounter more severe environmental 
conditions, which may obscure potential links between environmental 
comfort and psychological restoration.

3. Methods
3.1. Site selection
This study conducted a field experiment in Shenzhen, China. 
Shenzhen’s greenways have been developed alongside existing urban 
roads, forming a mature network that connects public green spaces and 
service facilities. Characterized by extensive coverage and flexible 
configurations [15], this network makes Shenzhen an ideal hotspot for 
research. Moreover, Shenzhen is not only representative of high-density 
urban environments but also experiences hot, humid summers, with 
average daily temperatures typically exceeding 30 ◦C [38]. As such, the 
city’s greenways offer a compelling context in which to investigate the 
relationship between outdoor thermal-acoustic comfort and psycho­
logical restoration.
In selecting specific greenways, we relied on the Shenzhen Greenway 
Atlas published by the Shenzhen Municipal Administration and 
Comprehensive Enforcement Bureau, along with local greenway web 
maps and on-site surveys. Ultimately, greenways in the Nanshan District 
were chosen as study sites. While both selected sites fall under the 
official category of community greenways as defined in the Shenzhen 
Greenway Atlas, we further classified them from an urban greening 
perspective into two categories based on vegetation density: densely 
vegetated and sparsely vegetated greenways (Fig. 1). This vegetation- 
based distinction complements earlier functional classifications (e.g., 
landscape-recreational vs. service-oriented) proposed in previous 
studies [15], and captures differences in ecological exposure and spatial 
enclosure relevant to users’ thermal-acoustic experiences. Densely 
vegetated greenways are typically located along sidewalks adjacent to 
urban parks, situated near major roads, and distributed in relatively 
discrete segments. They primarily serve as important recreational and 
exercise spaces for local residents. In contrast, sparsely vegetated 
greenways usually follow sidewalks along secondary or local streets, and 
are embedded within mixed-use urban settings that combine shopping, 
commuting, and leisure functions. Despite differences in immediate 
surroundings and vegetation density, both types belong to the broader 
urban greenway system designed to support non-motorized mobility and 
public open space access. Their inclusion enables a focused comparison 
of how variations in vegetation influence user experiences and percep­
tual outcomes within greenway spaces. Both greenways featured similar 
pedestrian surfaces, including asphalt, poured concrete, and concrete 
pavers commonly used in urban sidewalk construction.

For thermal assessment, the UTCI was adopted to evaluate the

UTCI’s wide acceptance and robust methodology in previous studies, it 
was used to represent thermal comfort [38], with corresponding stress 
categories based on specific UTCI ranges [39]. The thermal stress cate­
gories used in this study—very strong heat stress (+38 to +46 ◦C), strong 
heat stress (+32 to +38 ◦C), and moderate heat stress (+26 to +32 ◦C)— 
follow internationally recognized UTCI thresholds. These classifications 
have been widely applied in both cold and subtropical climates [6,38]. 
As for the acoustic environment, the A-weighted equivalent continuous 
sound level (LAeq) was selected to account for the intermittent nature of 
traffic noise, thereby serving as a suitable metric for assessing the 
greenway sound environment. Following the categories used in prior 
studies [29], LAeq was classified into four ranges: 55–60 dB(A), 60–65 
dB(A), 65–70 dB(A), and 70–75 dB(A). Field observations confirmed 
that the main sound sources at this site were urban traffic, human ac­
tivity, and occasional mechanical noise, with intermittent natural 
sounds such as wind in the vegetation and birdsong. No sea-related 
sounds were perceptible.
Following the experiment, we randomly selected 47 partic­
ipants—each of whom had completed the questionnaire and provided 
verbal consent—for brief, on-site interviews. Conducted outdoors and 
lasting 3 to 5 min, the semi-structured interviews explored participants’ 
perceptions of thermal comfort and acoustic disturbance, with questions 
including, for example, “what would make this greenway feel more 
comfortable to you?” and “were there any sounds that made you feel 
uncomfortable?” (Fig. 2).
3.3. Measures
3.3.1. Assessment of thermal and acoustic comfort
In this study, we assessed thermal-acoustic comfort in the selected 
greenway environments by designing a questionnaire informed by ISO 
10551 guidelines, ASHRAE Standards [40], and previous research on 
evaluating outdoor thermal and acoustic environments [25,29]. Spe­
cifically, the questionnaire included the Thermal Sensation Vote (TSV), 
the Thermal Comfort Vote (TCV), the Acoustic Sensation Vote (ASV), 
and the Acoustic Comfort Vote (ACV) (Appendix A). TSV captures 
consciously perceived thermal sensation, while ASV measures perceived 
loudness. TCV and ACV reflect satisfaction with the thermal and acoustic 
conditions, respectively. TCV and ACV were assessed using a 7-point 
bipolar Likert scale with a neutral midpoint, representing a state of 
neither satisfaction nor dissatisfaction, a format widely adopted in 
outdoor environmental comfort research to enable nuanced measure­
ment of comfort perceptions in field settings [25] (Table 1).
3.3.2. Measurement of psychological restorative benefits
The Restorative Outcome Scale (ROS) was used to measure partici­
pants’ level of psychological restoration. This scale has been widely 
employed and validated in studies examining urban outdoor environ­
ments and psychological restoration [41,42]. It consists of six items 
[43]: one item assesses attentional recovery (e.g., “my concentration 
and alertness clearly increase here”), two items address stress relief (e.g., 
“I can forget everyday worries here”), and three items evaluate mental 
relaxation and calm (e.g., “after visiting this place, I always feel restored 
and relaxed”). Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging 
from “disagree strongly” (−2) to “agree strongly” (+2). A composite 
score is obtained by averaging all six items, representing the overall 
restorative benefit.
3.3.3. Measurement of environmental parameters
During the field experiment, the thermal and acoustic conditions 
were recorded in parallel. The thermal parameters measured included 
air temperature (Ta), relative humidity (RH), wind speed (Va), and 
globe temperature (Tg). The acoustic parameter was the A-weighted 
equivalent continuous sound pressure level (LAeq). A portable Kestrel 
5500 weather station was used to record wind speed and direction, 
while the BES-01 temperature recorder measured Tg. The black globe 
measured 0.08 m in diameter, and its surface material had a scattering 
coefficient of 0.95. A BES-02 temperature and humidity recorder 
measured air temperature and humidity. All devices were placed under a 
radiation-resistant aluminum hood to minimize interference from direct 
solar radiation and wind. To assess the acoustic environment, an NC-75 
acoustic calibrator was employed to calibrate the NL-52A sound level 
meter, ensuring accurate measurements. All instruments were posi­
tioned 1.5 m above the ground. Table 2 provides further details on the 
recorded parameters.

study procedures, and they were assured that all questionnaire data 
would be used exclusively for academic research purposes. Additionally, 
confidentiality was strictly maintained, and no personal information 
was disclosed under any circumstances.
3.5. Data analysis
First, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to examine 
whether there were significant differences in thermal-acoustic percep­
tions across different types of greenways, as well as to identify the role of 
thermal-acoustic environments in restorative outcomes. Second, 
weighted linear regression models were used to explore the relationships 
between ROS scores and the measured UTCI and LAeq values. To facil­
itate analysis, all data were categorized by greenway type, and within 
each category, data points were grouped in increments of 1 ◦C UTCI, 
after which the mean values were computed. Subsequently, a two-way 
ANOVA was conducted to investigate the combined effects of thermal 
and acoustic conditions on psychological restoration. Finally, condi­
tional probability analysis was utilized to test for potential “veto ef­
fects,” wherein the interaction of thermal and acoustic comfort could 
significantly affect psychological restoration. All statistical analyses 
were performed using SPSS version 26.0.
4. Results
4.1. Comparison of thermal-acoustic comfort across different greenway 
types
To answer RQ1, we conducted analysis of variance to compare 
thermal-acoustic perceptions between different types of greenways: 
densely vegetated and sparsely vegetated. Participants reported signif­
icantly higher levels of comfort in densely vegetated greenways 
compared to sparsely vegetated ones, evident in both TCV (densely: 0.97 
vs sparsely: 0.28; p < 0.01) and ACV (densely: 1.33 vs sparsely: 0.36; p <
0.01). Conversely, compared to densely vegetated greenways, partici­
pants perceived sparsely vegetated greenways as hotter (TSV, densely: 
0.70 vs sparsely: 1.47; p < 0.01) and noisier (ASV, densely: 0.77 vs 
sparsely: −0.47; p < 0.01) (Fig. 3).
4.2. Impact of thermal environment on psychological restoration
Fig. 4 illustrates the relationship between UTCI and restorative 
outcomes. In both densely vegetated (R² = 0.552) and sparsely vege­
tated (R² = 0.688) greenways, UTCI exhibits a moderate negative cor­
relation with ROS. This indicates that regardless of greenway type, an 
increase in UTCI is associated with a gradual decline in restorative 
benefits. Moreover, this trend is more pronounced in sparsely vegetated 
greenways.
To further investigate the impact of thermal comfort votes (TCV) on 
ROS under different thermal stress conditions, an analysis of variance 
was conducted. The results indicate that under moderate heat stress, 
strong heat stress, and very strong heat stress conditions, TCV signifi­
cantly influenced ROS in both densely vegetated and sparsely vegetated 
greenways. Notably, ROS evaluations were higher in densely vegetated 
greenways. Fig. 5 illustrates the relationship between TCV and ROS. 
When TCV ≥−1, restorative benefits were only absent under very strong 
heat stress conditions. However, when TCV ≥−2, despite thermal 
discomfort in the densely vegetated greenway, ROS still exhibited 
restorative benefits under moderate heat stress. Across all thermal stress 
conditions, ROS increased with rising TCV. In sparsely vegetated 
greenways, the differences in ROS between thermally comfortable and 
uncomfortable visitors were 2.42 under very strong heat stress, 2.34 
under strong heat stress, and 2.61 under moderate heat stress. In 
contrast, in densely vegetated greenways, ROS differences remained 
relatively stable across thermal stress conditions, averaging around 2.3. 
This suggests that the influence of TCV on ROS is stronger in sparsely 
vegetated greenways. These findings provide an initial response to RQ2.
4.3. Impact of acoustic environment on psychological restoration
Fig. 6 illustrates a moderate negative correlation between LAeq and 
ROS. Specifically, the relationship between LAeq and ROS is more pro­
nounced in densely vegetated greenways (R² = 0.622) compared to 
sparsely vegetated greenways (R² = 0.536). This suggests that the 
acoustic environment has a greater impact on ROS in densely vegetated 
greenways than in sparsely vegetated ones. As LAeq increases, restor­
ative benefits gradually decline.
Fig. 7 illustrates the relationship between ACV and restorative ben­
efits under different acoustic conditions. ANOVA comparisons between 
greenway types reveal a significant difference in the impact of ACV on 
restoration. In densely vegetated greenways, even when ACV ≥−2, ROS 
continues to exhibit restorative benefits at 55–60 dB(A). However, this 
effect is not observed in sparsely vegetated greenways, where ROS only 
demonstrates restorative benefits when ACV ≥−1 under 55–60 dB(A) 
conditions. Regarding the influence of ACV on ROS, at the same LAeq 
level, ROS significantly increases as ACV improves. The average range (i. 
e., the difference between the maximum and minimum values) was 1.71 
in densely vegetated greenways and 1.86 in sparsely vegetated green­
ways. Concerning the impact of the acoustic environment on ROS, at the

4.5. Influence of thermal-acoustic comfort on psychological restoration
To address RQ3, we employed conditional probability analysis to 
elucidate the relationship between thermal-acoustic comfort and psy­
chological restoration [25]. Conditional probability refers to the likeli­
hood of one event occurring in the context of another event that is 
already known or assumed to have taken place. It is typically denoted as 
P(A | B), representing the probability of event A under the condition 
that event B has occurred. Specifically, we calculated probabilities to 
describe the likelihood of experiencing “adverse psychological restora­
tion” (APR) under different combinations of comfort factors in greenway 
environments with varying vegetation densities. First, we defined 
probability events representing outdoor comfort votes (Table 3). Based 
on previous research, comfort votes within the range of [−3,0] were 
classified as “uncomfortable,” while psychological restoration scores 
within [−2,0] were defined as “APR” [5]. This classification resulted in a 
total of 22 = 4 possible comfort condition combinations, as outlined in 
Table 3.
Table 4 presents the probability of different comfort vote combina­
tions occurring under APR conditions, allowing for an examination of

comfort in both thermal and acoustic conditions, they could still expe­

with an incidence rate of 15.12 %. Notably, acoustic discomfort 
exhibited a stronger veto effect on psychological restoration in densely 
vegetated greenways (32.50 %), whereas thermal discomfort showed a 
higher veto tendency in sparsely vegetated greenways (30.35 %). When 
both thermal and acoustic comfort votes were rated as “uncomfortable,” 
the likelihood of experiencing APR was significantly higher in SVUG 
(73.62 %).
5. Discussion
5.1. Interaction between thermal and acoustic environments
The interaction between thermal and acoustic environments signif­
icantly influences the restorative benefits of different types of green­
ways. Regarding the impact of the thermal environment on ROS in 
densely vegetated greenways, the effect of LAeq on ROS varies across 
different UTCI levels. As UTCI increases from 31.7 ◦C to 41.4 ◦C, ROS 
evaluations decline with rising LAeq, demonstrating a downward trend 
within the same UTCI category. This suggests that when summer tem­
peratures are excessively high and thermal stress is strong, ROS is 
significantly affected, decreasing as LAeq increases. However, this trend 
is relatively moderate within the 55–60 dB(A) range but becomes more 
pronounced under 70–75 dB(A) conditions. In other words, even under 
high thermal stress, a favorable acoustic environment in densely vege­
tated greenways provides an opportunity to mitigate its negative effects 
on restoration. This finding aligns with previous research suggesting 
that acoustic comfort can enhance individuals’ tolerance to thermal 
conditions, thereby fostering more positive perceptual experiences [45]. 
For instance, vegetation with different leaf morphologies can help 
reduce urban noise [46], offering a sustainable and easily implement­
able alternative to constructing noise barriers while also contributing to 
urban greenery. Such approaches provide practical design solutions for 
addressing thermal exposure in outdoor environments. These findings 
support the notion that poor performance in a single sensory dimension 
may obscure the influence of multisensory interactions. Therefore, it is 
crucial to consider multisensory interactions in order to help policy­
makers and urban planners identify appropriate intervention strategies 
to optimize the restorative potential of various outdoor spaces.
Furthermore, another key contribution of this study is its deeper 
insight into the factors contributing to “unfavorable” psychological re­
covery. In hot climates, thermal exposure can negatively impact mental

5.2. Impact of environmental conditions on restoration
First, this study extends previous research on the influence of land­
scape and soundscape on subjective perceptions of restorative benefits 
in outdoor spaces by further exploring the role of thermal and acoustic 
environmental conditions. It further reveals the interrelationship be­
tween thermal and acoustic environments based on their physical con­
ditions. As UTCI and LAeq increase, ROS evaluations gradually decline. 
Regarding the impact of the thermal environment on ROS, the effect of 
TCV varies under different levels of thermal stress. When individuals are 
dissatisfied with the thermal environment, ROS is lower. This finding 
supports the correlation between UTCI and ROS rather than just tem­
perature alone [6]. This relationship can be explained by the influence of 
solar radiation and subjective appraisal of thermal comfort [49], which 
reflects both physical exposure and psychological judgment.
Second, concerning the impact of the acoustic environment on ROS, 
we found that under different acoustic conditions, respondents who 
perceived a comfortable sound environment reported higher ROS levels. 
This effect was particularly evident in greenway settings. In both densely 
vegetated and sparsely vegetated greenways, even when ACV was rated 
as slightly uncomfortable (ACV ≥−1), psychological restoration was 
still observed under sound environments ranging from 55 to 60 dB(A) 
and 60 to 65 dB(A). This phenomenon can be explained by the restor­
ative potential of preferred soundscapes [50], where natural sounds are 
often more accepted [51], especially in urban greenways. Additionally, 
ACV may be influenced by individual subjective preferences and 
perceptual sensitivity [52], which should not be overlooked in assessing 
its impact on psychological restoration.
Finally, this study expands previous research on restorative envi­
ronments based on Attention Restoration Theory and Stress Recovery 
Theory, emphasizing that environmental restorative benefits are 
significantly influenced by thermal and acoustic conditions, rather than 
being solely dependent on visual factors [18]. By incorporating objec­
tive measurements of the thermal and acoustic environment, this study 
complements subjective assessments of comfort and helps explain how 
perceived discomfort under different environmental conditions may 
translate into changes in restoration outcomes [21]. This approach 
provides a more scientific and practical foundation for the design and 
application of outdoor spaces.
5.3. Implications for urban greenway design
To support more targeted greenway planning, this study integrates 
both subjective comfort evaluations and objective environmental in­
dicators. While subjective ratings capture individual experiences of 
comfort, objective metrics help identify the physical conditions shaping 
those perceptions. They not only clarify the relative influence of thermal 
and acoustic factors, but also reveal where landscape inter­
ventions—such as shading or vegetation buffers—may be most effective. 
This integrated approach acknowledges that comfort is shaped by both 
environmental conditions and psychological adaptation, and it enables 
more responsive and evidence-based design decisions.
Although research has demonstrated the numerous benefits of nat­
ural environments for physical and mental well-being, urban green­
ways—as typical outdoor spaces—face dual challenges. First, they are 
often underutilized in hot weather, and second, the combined effects of 
soundscape and climate on restorative benefits in high-density urban 
environments remain insufficiently explored. In particular, during hot 
seasons and under noisy conditions, subjective discomfort may 
discourage people from using outdoor spaces. These findings highlight 
the importance of addressing thermal and acoustic discomfort in out­
door environments to maximize their restorative potential. Urban street 
vegetation can enhance outdoor thermal comfort, mitigate noise, and 
alleviate stress [18,46,53]. For example, clusters of trees provide better 
noise reduction than individual trees, and dense greenery offers shade 
that reduces solar radiation, lowers local temperatures, and enhances 
overall comfort. To improve outdoor spatial comfort, landscape de­
signers and policymakers should prioritize these strategies.
Empirically, our findings offer guidance for differentiated greenway 
design. In densely vegetated streets, regulating the sound environ­
ment—such as using vegetation to reduce noise and create a more nat­
ural, comfortable soundscape—emerges as a key strategy for enhancing 
restorative benefits, as soundscape influences are more pronounced in 
such settings. Conversely, in sparsely vegetated greenways, thermal 
conditions play a greater role in restoration. Therefore, implementing 
artificial shading structures (e.g., canopies and street-scale shelters) to 
reduce temperatures is an effective intervention [54]. As urban green­
ways serve as vital outdoor spaces in high-density cities, their spatial 
configuration and design should emphasize comfort. In areas with high 
usage during hot seasons, shade should be prioritized to facilitate out­
door activities in thermally comfortable microclimates. These measures 
not only promote physical and mental restoration but also reduce 
dependence on energy-intensive cooling systems [38], contributing to 
sustainable development goals.
5.4. Limitations and prospects
This study conducted field-based assessments of the thermal and 
acoustic environments of urban greenways during the hot summer 
season in a high-density city. By quantifying visitors’ subjective evalu­
ations of environmental comfort and psychological restoration, the 
research contributes to a deeper understanding of how the interaction 
between thermal and acoustic conditions influences restorative out­
comes in urban outdoor settings. These findings offer valuable insights 
for the evidence-based design and renewal of urban green spaces, 
particularly in contexts where environmental stressors such as heat and 
noise are increasingly prevalent.
Nevertheless, given the dynamic and complex nature of outdoor 
environments, two key limitations of this study must be acknowledged. 
First, although we assessed both the physical parameters of the thermal- 
acoustic environment and participants’ comfort evaluations, we may 
have underestimated users’ subjective soundscape preferences—such as 
perceived pleasantness and eventfulness—which could introduce po­
tential bias in the interpretation of acoustic comfort. Despite this limi­
tation, the findings remain valuable, as the in-situ approach captures the 
lived experiences of greenway users, offering a more holistic under­
standing of outdoor comfort and restorative potential. This, in turn, 
provides urban planners and policymakers with a richer evidence base 
for designing environments that support mental well-being. Second, the 
study focused exclusively on the hot summer period, without accounting 
for seasonal variations in thermal-acoustic comfort experiences. Future 
research should refine this limitation by incorporating seasonal 
dynamics—such as transitional periods and winter conditions—into an 
integrated framework that reflects the interplay between environmental 
and temporal characteristics of urban greenways.
Despite these limitations, the study makes a significant contribution 
to understanding how thermal-acoustic comfort in hot climates can 
enhance the restorative potential of urban greenways. It moves beyond 
traditional approaches that rely solely on physical environmental con­
trols by introducing a perceptual perspective that accounts for the 
combined effects of thermal and acoustic experiences. These findings

6. Conclusions
Under the pressures of urban heat and rising climate stress, this study 
examined how thermal and acoustic conditions in outdoor spaces in­
fluence psychological restoration. The aim was to assess how combined 
thermal-acoustic comfort affects restorative outcomes in high-density 
urban contexts.
First, perceptual differences in thermal-acoustic comfort varied by 
greenway type: thermal conditions were more strongly linked to resto­
ration in sparsely vegetated greenways, while acoustic conditions 
played a greater role in densely vegetated ones. Second, both TCV and 
ACV had distinct impacts under varying levels of thermal stress and 
noise. As thermal stress rose, TCV’s influence on ROS increased in 
sparsely vegetated greenways. Conversely, in densely vegetated green­
ways, ACV remained positively associated with ROS even under higher 
sound pressure levels—highlighting the value of soundscape design in 
hot environments. Finally, a differentiated “veto effect” was observed: 
ACV showed stronger veto tendencies in densely vegetated settings, 
while TCV played a larger veto role in sparse ones. This suggests that 
enhancing acoustic comfort may be more effective in tree-rich envi­
ronments, whereas improving thermal comfort is more critical in open, 
less-shaded spaces. The interaction between thermal and acoustic en­
vironments shapes psychological restoration differently depending on 
vegetation density. In the creation of comfortable urban outdoor spaces, 
improving thermal-acoustic conditions offers a promising pathway for 
enhancing restorative potential.
The comfort of urban greenways is closely linked to public health, 
particularly in high-density cities like Shenzhen, where greenway spaces 
directly impact residents’ everyday experiences. Designing open spaces 
that consider both thermal and acoustic comfort can significantly 
enhance users’ restorative outcomes, encouraging greater participation 
in outdoor leisure and mobility. By addressing the combined effects of 
thermal and acoustic environments, urban designers can more effec­
tively create restorative urban landscapes through the targeted modu­
lation of environmental comfort.
