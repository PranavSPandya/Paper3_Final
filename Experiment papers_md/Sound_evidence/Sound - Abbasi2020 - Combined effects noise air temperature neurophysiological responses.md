1. Introduction

People frequently spend most of their time in closed spaces such as
home as well as commercial or industrial places. Therefore, the quality
of indoor environments can affect their comfort and performance (Tiller
et  al.,  2010).  Some  studies  have  shown  the  significant  effects  of  the
environmental  factors  on  employeesˈ  cognitive  performance  and  job
satisfaction (Golmohammadi et al., 2017; Clausen et al., 2013). Noise
and  air  temperature  are  believed  to  be  the  most  important  indoor
environmental factors that affect human’s health and work performance
in mental activities (Liebl et al., 2012; Lan et al., 2011).

Indoor environmental factors can individually affect the health and
comfort of employees, while noise and air temperature can also have
combined effects on these individuals (Hemmatjo et al., 2017; Lan et al.,
2011a,b;  Varjo  et  al.,  2015).  In  recent  years,  some  researchers  have

designed  experimental  studies  to  investigate  the  individual  effects  of
noise and air temperature on cognitive performance using advanced test
methods (Kaczmarska and Luczak, 2007). As reported by Li et al. (2014),
when subjects were exposed to noise for 5 min, the sum of the relative
average power of electroencephalogram (APEEG) of theta wave and the
relative APEEG of alpha wave increased. The subjects’ annoyance level
based on the sum of relative APEEG of h wave was also aggravated under
this noise exposure scenario. Lan et al. (2009) showed that air temper-
ature had diverse effects on staff cognitive performance depending on
the type of the tasks they were engaged with. Gaoua et al. (2012) re-
ported that the increase in body temperature is apparently a sufficient
physiological response that alters the subjective state of participants and
disturbs decision-making. Lan et al. (2010) also focused on body phys-
iological responses to hot and humid environments, while ignoring the
influential factors. Cui et al. (2013) found that the optimum temperature

Note that, in a real workroom, people are exposed to all these factors
simultaneously. Further, the environmental parameters are all interre-
lated, and human comfort is a complex state involving an occupant’s
sensations  of  all  these  factors  (Zhang  et  al.,  2010;  Huan  et  al.,  2004;
Pellerin and Candas, 2003). ASHRAE guideline10 (2016) concentrated
on interactions among the indoor environmental factors and the possible
ways  through  which  different  factors  might  affect  each  other.  In  this
regard, some studies have tried to investigate the interaction between
noise  and  air  temperature  on  human  body  responses.  Piasecki  et  al.
(2017) showed that people’s perceptions of thermal conditions are not
altered through short-term exposure to different noise levels. However,
thermal conditions affect the subjects’ noise perceptions depending on
the types of noise and relative air humidity. Pellerin and Candas (2004)
also  reported that noise may alter human’s thermal comfort in warm
environments. They observed that a temperature change 1
C had the
same effect as 2.6 dB variation in noise. Yang et al. (2018) reported that
short-term exposure to moderate noise cannot affect thermal sensations;
nonetheless, air temperature and humidity levels impact the perceptions
of loudness and annoyance levels as well as acoustic preferences. Varjo
et  al.  (2015)  investigated  how  irrelevant  speech  and  air  temperature
together  affect  the  mental  performance  and  environmental  comfort
among office staffs. While being exposed to a great deal of irrelevant
speech  and  high  air  temperature,  they  observed  that  the  employeesˈ
cognitive performance diminished especially in mental tasks. Yang and
Moon (2018) also noted that the effects of noise on thermal comfort and
impacts of thermal sensation based on predicted mean vote (PMV) on
acoustic comfort and noise annoyance were statistically significant.

�

Torresin et al. (2018) stated that humans are exposed simultaneously
to acoustic, thermal as well as visual and air quality stimuli in indoor
environments. They confirmed that research is still ongoing to clarify
the  nature  of  the  interaction  and  derive  practical  indications  for  de-
signers and practitioners. Huang et al. (2012) stated that the satisfaction
levels of both air temperature and noise have one-vote veto power over
the satisfaction level of the other indoor environment factors. Choi et al.
(2015) reported that it is necessary to investigate the mechanism behind
indoor environmental factors influencing neurophysiological responses
of the human body. Chao et al. (2013) emphasized that the combined
effects  of  noise  and  air  temperature  are  still  unclear;  therefore,  the
impact of various exposure times, levels and types of noise as well as
dynamic thermal conditions warrant further studies.

Less information is available to draw reliable conclusions on the ef-
fects of simultaneous exposure to noise and relatively extreme air tem-
perature  on  cognitive  performance  and  mental  health.  Until  now,  no
consistent results have been found about these topics (Yang and Moon,
2018).  The  current  experimental  study  aims  to  explore  the  plausible
mechanism  linking  air  temperature  and  noise  to  human  comfort  and
performance through analyzing a wide range of advanced neurophysi-
ological response parameters via electroencephalography and  electro-
cardiography in a simulated office workroom. The current study is an
attempt to understand the interaction in detail. In this way, the effect
size data about different exposure scenarios can help experts analyze the
priorities  in  the  design  of  indoor  environments  and  find  constructive
solutions to control environmental variables.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Participants

Thirty-five  healthy  male  students  from  Hamadan  University  of
Medical  Sciences  were selected as  the participants.  Their age,  height,
weight, and body mass index (BMI) were 22.9 � 2.9 year, 178.0 � 4.9
cm, 72.9 � 10.3 kg, and 23.1 � 3.2 respectively. The participants were
screened for visual and hearing health. The visual test was carried out

using E chart, while the hearing health was tested using an audiometer
(MEVOX  ASB15).  The  participants  with  a  minimum  average  hearing
threshold of 25 dBHL were enrolled in the study. The subjects with a
history of neuropsychiatric diseases and/or drug use that affected their
nervous system were excluded from the study. In addition, the subjects
were  examined  for  right-handedness  and  color  blindness.  They  were
asked to get enough sleep before the test and were recommended not to
smoke and not to use caffeine or any other stimulants 12 h before the
experiments.  All  participants  were  paid  in  order  to  enhance  their
motivation and to encourage them to perform the experiments seriously
and correctly. It seems that, due to the nature of the study experiments,
including  repeated  measurements,  the  same  salary  for  participation
could prevent missing participants during different sessions. As a result,
all of them successfully completed the experimental sessions. It was also
observed that the participants attended the sessions on time and per-
formed  the  tests  continuously.  Further,  in  the  interview  before  each
experiment,  they  stated  their  verbal  satisfaction  with  tests  at  the
different sessions. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of
Hamadan  University  of  Medical  Sciences  (ethic  code:  IR.UMSHA.
REC.1395.457).  After  confirmation,  informed  consent  was  obtained
from the participants.

2.2. Experimental set up

The study was conducted in an air conditioning chamber with the
dimensions of L � W � H ¼ 3.70 � 2.40 � 2.70 m. The walls of the
chamber were covered with bright colored polyurethane foam absorbent
to make it psychologically appropriate to resemble indoor wall surfaces
of typical offices (Dzulkifli and Mustafar, 2013). In this room, there was
a table, a chair, and a personal computer to resemble an office’s work-
station as shown in Fig. 1. Further, the minimum level of room lighting
was 300 lux (Huang et al., 2012), supplied by two LED lights. The indoor
of  the  chamber  was  carefully  furnished  and  finished  to  resemble  a
typical  normal  office  environment  so  that  the  participants  feel  same
about the simulated indoor environment.

It was possible to set and fix the chamber’s thermal conditions using
an air-conditioner system. The relative humidity was kept constant at
40% and air velocity was set at 0.15 m/s during all experiments. The

The  participants  were  asked  to  select  a  specific  day  and  time  to

the  participants  were  individually  tested  in  each  session.  The  experi-
ment  was  carried  out  using  a  within-participants  design,  where  all
participants  were  tested  under  eight  experimental  conditions,  thus
acting as their own controls.

Before the participants entered the chamber, the neurophysiological
responses  (baseline)  were  measured  in  a  room  considered  near  the
chamber for 10 min. Next, the participants entered the chamber reading
books or playing games for 5 min to adapt to the indoor environment.
Following the adaptation, they spent about 5 min on performing the N-
back tests (low workload), during which their physiological responses
were  measured.  After  completing  the  tests,  5  min  were  allocated  to
participantsˈ  rest.  The  participants  spent  again  about  5  min  on  per-
forming the N-back tests (medium workload) and simultaneously their
physiological responses were measured. After completing the second N-
back  tests,  they  were  again  given  5  min  to  have  a  rest.  Finally,  the
participants spent about 5 min on performing the third round of N-back
tests  (high  workload),  while  their  physiological  responses  were
measured. Ten minutes later, their neurophysiological responses were
again measured. The flowchart of different steps of the experiments is
presented in Fig. 3.

In  the  current  study  based  on  a  within-participants  design,  the
possible  carryover  effects  were  partially  controlled  by  the  random
exposure (instead of sequential exposure) to different scenarios (com-
bined air temperature and noise level) and also to different N-back tests.
Further, it is argued that if the subjects are fully trained and familiar
enough with the tasks before the experiment, the learning effect can be
approximately removed (Lan et al., 2009). Thus, a week before starting
the tests, the participants practiced the N-back to get familiar with the

test procedure as well as the N-back software. In this regard, the subjects
were given two separate 30-min training sessions, followed by the ex-
amination of their performance. If a defect was observed, the participant
was  again  given  the  opportunity  to  practice  until  the  defect  was
resolved. In the second training session, a pilot study was performed in
order  to  compare  different  stimulated  workloads  (N-back)  with  the
perceived  workloads  for  10  participants  using  NASA  TLX  and  EEG.
These  trail  experiments  showed  no  significant  differences  between
stimulated  workloads  and  the  perceived  workloads.  In  addition,
following the pilot study, exposure orders to different workloads were
checked in six scenarios based on EEG. There were not significant dif-
ferences between the different exposure orders for the 10 participants.
All participants sat on the chair during the tests whereby the work-
load was increased step by step, from low to high. Each task lasted for 5
min and there was 2500 ms intervals between each stimulus. The par-
ticipants were presented a total of 120 stimuli, which were implemented
at all levels of the task simultaneously under the same air temperature
and  noise  level.  In  order  to  prevent  errors  in  the  measurements,  the
participants were asked to have minimum movement and focus on the
given  task.  After  installing  ECG  electrodes,  three  signal  levels  were
measured before the exposure and during the exposure to each of the air
temperature  and  noise  levels  through  N-back  test  while  performing
three workloads (low, medium, and high) for 5 min. Also, before and
immediately after the exposure to any air temperatures and noise levels,
the  electrical  activities  of  the  brain  and  eyes  were  measured  (Abbasi
et al., 2018).

2.3. Types of measurements

2.3.1. Working memory

N-back test was employed at three cognitive levels (low workload (n
¼ 1), medium workload (n ¼ 2), and high workload (n ¼ 3)) to evaluate
the working memory. N-back task is a cognitive performance measure-
ment task with an executive function originally introduced by Kirchner
(1958). Since this task involves saving and manipulating cognitive in-
formation,  it  is  known  as  a  suitable  method  to  measure  the  working
memory performance. In this test, a number of visual stimuli (from 1 to
9)  randomly  appear  on  the  computer  screen  and  the  subjects  must
respond to each of them in three different conditions with various loads
of working memory. Under the low workload condition, the participants
compared  each  visual  stimulus  with  the  previous  one;  if  the  two
consecutive images were the same, they should have pressed the ques-
tion mark button (?) on the computer keyboard; else the Z Button. The
participants performed the same task in the end-test. Under the medium
workload  condition,  the  subjects  should  have  compared  the  stimulus
with  the  two  previous  stimuli;  if  the  stimuli  were  similar,  they  were
supposed to press question mark button (?); else the Z Button. Finally,
under the high workload condition, the subjects were asked to compare
each stimulus with the three previous stimuli; if the stimuli were similar,
they were supposed to press the question mark (?); else the Z Button
(Kirchner, 1958; Savage and Goghari, 2016).

The  results  of  the  N-back  test  show  the  number  of  correct  and
incorrect answers as well as the average response time (Jaeggi et al.,
2010). In this study, the stimuli were random numbers from 1 to 9. The
number of correct answers (accuracy) and the average response time (in
milliseconds)  were  calculated  by  N-back  software  under  the  four

2.3.3. Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using repeated measure analysis of variance.
To evaluate the normal distribution of the data at different levels, the
Mauchly’s Test of Sphericity was used. When the sphericity hypothesis
was rejected, the Huynh-Feldt’s analysis was used to correct the degree
of freedom. Also, the Tukey’s Test for Post-Hoc analysis was carried out
to examine the difference between the means. The effect size (ES) was
also reported. SPSS-Ver24 was employed for statistical analysises. The
significant level for all tests was set at 5%.

work complexity does not interact with noise and air temperature. In
addition, the effect of increase in air temperature on the mean changes in
theta, alpha and beta amplitudes was more significant than the effect of
exposure to a noise level of up to 75 dB.

As  with  any  other  research,  this  study  suffered  from  some  limita-
tions. The short-term exposures to different scenarios of environmental
conditions  may  have  resulted  in  restricted  human  responses. Further,
field studies are required to show cognitive performance in occupations
that require long-term exposures in extreme environmental conditions.
It is also suggested future researches focus on the effect of gender as an
important variable. It is proposed that some aspects of the interaction
effects  of  these  external  stressors  be  investigated  by  more  advanced
methods (e.g. neuroimaging). The current study focused on the balance
between air temperature and noise in terms of their effects on working
memory  and  psychophysiological  indices.  It  is  now  one  of  the  pre-
requisites for the design of indoor environments, such as offices, control
rooms, and other similar environments. The results of this study provide
enough evidence to modify the managers’  outlook, policies, and prac-
tices in designing workrooms and setting environmental comfort stan-
dards for office buildings.

5. Conclusion

In order to design an acceptable indoor environment, not only should
each environmental parameter, including noise, lighting, and air tem-
perature be in acceptable level, but also the combined impacts of these
parameters  must  be  considered.  This  study  evaluated  the  mechanism
through which mental stress was affected by to two main air environ-
mental  factors  based  on  a  neurophysiological  approach.  It  was  found
that this approach could be used as a basis for future multidimensional
studies. The following conclusions can be drawn from the results of the
current study which are limited to the experiment configurations of air
temperatures (18–30
