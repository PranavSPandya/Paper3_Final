1. Introduction
The onset of urbanization has introduced numerous environmental dilemmas, including the urban heat island effect (Heaviside
et al., 2017)and the widespread occurrence of hot and humid conditions in cities (Zhang et al., 2023a). These challenges significantly
deter urban residents from engaging in outdoor activities, consequently impacting their physical and mental health (Potchter et al.,
2022). Establishing an outdoor environment that is both thermally comfortable and inviting is pivotal in motivating residents to
actively engage in outdoor activities (Kumar and Sharma, 2020; Zhang et al., 2023b). Thermal comfort is a complex concept, influ-
enced by several environmental factors like the acoustic environment (Favero et al., 2023) and humidity (Al-Atrash and Al-Ayyoub,

In 1972, Hardy AC identified humidity as a crucial factor affecting thermal comfort (AC, 1972). This insight has led to further
research into the impact of humidity on thermal comfort (Acero et al., 2022; Amaripadath et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2018; Yuan et al.,
2023). Various studies have focused on humidity’s effect on outdoor thermal comfort and sensation (AC, 1972). Yu et al. found that
high humidity levels impair evaporative cooling, leading to increased thermal stress, reduced thermal comfort, and heightened
thermal sensation (Yuan et al., 2023). Amaripadath et al. noted significant human responses to high humidity, especially when relative
humidity exceeded 70% and temperatures increased (Amaripadath et al., 2023). In Juan A. Acero’s study, maintaining a humidity
range of 70% RH to 90% RH kept the mean Physiological Equivalent Temperature (PET) level within Singapore’s acceptable thermal
comfort range throughout the day and night (Acero et al., 2022). Jiawei Yao’s research in winter Shanghai showed that a humidity
range of 70% RH to 90% RH led to most respondents being comfortable with the thermal environment, with PET temperatures between
7.7 ◦C and 21.8 ◦C (Yao et al., 2018).
Contradicting earlier assertions that outdoor thermal comfort is unaffected by sound (Fanger et al., 2007), recent inquiries have
begun to unearth the interconnection between sound and thermal comfort (Du et al., 2023a; Jin et al., 2020; Lau and Choi, 2021;
Nicolas Pellerin, 2003; Yang and Moon, 2018). Du et al. demonstrated that escalated sound levels intensify stress and heat sensation,
thereby undermining thermal comfort (Du et al., 2023a). Kevin and Choi’s analysis of perceived acoustics and outdoor thermal comfort
during hot summers indicated that tranquil and visually pleasing outdoor settings enhance thermal tolerance and minimize thermal
sensitivity (Lau and Choi, 2021). Jin et al.’s findings suggest that although sound does not influence thermal sensation in cooler
conditions, it contributes to thermal discomfort (Jin et al., 2020). Pellerin and Candas’ research into the combined effects of sound and
temperature on discomfort revealed that sound modifies thermal comfort in warmer climates (Nicolas Pellerin, 2003). Yang et al.’s
study on the effects of sound and thermal conditions on subjective environmental perception concluded that while sound does not
impact thermal sensation, it negatively influences thermal comfort as sound intensity increases (Yang and Moon, 2018).
Prior research has predominantly focused on the effects of individual environmental factors on thermal comfort, such as the
singular impacts of acoustic and humid conditions. In contrast, residents typically face a spectrum of environmental interactions (Lai
et al., 2020). Research by Bin et al. indicates that the aggregate physical and psychological stress encountered in a comprehensive
environmental context notably exceeds the influence exerted by any single environmental element (Yang et al., 2022). Furthermore,
the assessment of outdoor thermal comfort is governed by a blend of psychological and physiological factors (Lam et al., 2021). This
implies that an analysis confined to thermal comfort within a singular environmental aspect inadequately reflects the authentic
adaptive thermal responses of individuals in the intricate milieu of urban settings.
This study, through a questionnaire survey and the collection of meteorological data at a university in Xi’an, aimed to investigate
the changes in outdoor thermal comfort experienced by individuals in an environment influenced by the interplay of sound and
humidity. The research objectives were specifically to: (1) evaluate the influence of acoustic environment variations on outdoor

2. Experimental
2.1. Experiment site
Xi’an, positioned within the warm temperate semi-humid monsoon climate zone, is characterized by moderate rainfall and four
distinct seasons. Analysis of climate data spanning the past decade indicates an average annual temperature of 13.3 ◦C, annual pre-
cipitation fluctuating between 507.7 mm and 719.8 mm, and an average humidity level of 69.6% RH. The research was undertaken at a
university located in Xi’an (108◦922′ 765″E, 34.177′192″ N). The university’s campus, sprawling over approximately 80 ha, is replete
with verdant vegetation and a richly diverse outdoor cultural landscape. This setting presents an ideal backdrop for investigating the
dynamics of sound, humidity, and thermal comfort in campus environments. The study was conducted across four representative
campus locations: an open square, a playground, an area adjacent to a pool, and a lawn section. These sites typify the standard spatial
layouts found on the campus (Fig. 1).
2.2. Experiment design
The study will conduct experiments from 11:30 to 14:30 every day from June 1 to September 1, 2021. In case of bad weather, the
experiment will be postponed. A total of 20 volunteers, comprising 10 men and 10 women, participated in the experiment. To
minimize the effect of gender differences on the results, the volunteers were organized into two groups, each containing 5 men and 5
women. Before the experiment, the volunteers were briefed on the study’s purpose, methods, and procedures to ensure they fully
understood and agreed to participate. The two groups of volunteers alternately carried out the experiments, visiting the four selected
sites in a predetermined order. At each site, they rested for 5–10 min in a standing position, observed the environment, and completed
questionnaires.
Parallel to these activities, environmental parameters such as air temperature, relative humidity, and sound decibel levels were
measured at a standardized height of 1.2 m using a mobile weather station (Zhang et al., 2023a). The specifics regarding the mea-
surement instrument, including its model, operational range, accuracy, and appropriate environmental conditions, are enumerated in
Table 1. To ensure accuracy and mitigate the interference of ancillary sound sources in the sound decibel readings, a minimum of 20
measurements were recorded at each site, with the conclusive data being the mean of these readings (Yin et al., 2022).
2.3. Questionnaires
The questionnaire for the study consisted of three parts. The first section collected the participants’ personal information, including
gender, age, health status, and clothing type. Participants unwilling to disclose their personal details due to privacy concerns were
excluded from the test. The second section required participants to evaluate the Thermal Sensation Vote (TSV) and Thermal Comfort
Vote (TCV) of the current environment, as well as their perceptions of the acoustic and humidity environments, using the Likert 7-point
scale to reflect their satisfaction and subjective comfort levels. The final section involved the investigators documenting the envi-
ronmental physical parameters during the test, such as wind speed, sound, black globe temperature, humidity, and illumination.
In the course of the experiment, a total of 1700 questionnaires were completed, out of which 1603 were adjudged valid for in-
clusion in the study. Given the university campus setting of the test site, the participant demographic was exclusively college students,

2.4. Procedures
With the continuous development of outdoor thermal comfort research, scholars often use PET, Standard Effective Temperature
(SET), Perceived Equivalent Temperature (TEP) and Uuniversal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) to construct outdoor thermal comfort
models (Davtalab et al., 2020). Notably, PET has been increasingly adopted in recent Outdoor Thermal Comfort (OTC) assessments due
to its capacity for enabling comparative evaluations of thermal comfort across diverse environmental settings (Yin et al., 2022). In the
scope of this study, PET was specifically selected to forecast thermal comfort levels. To calculate the PET values for all participants, the
Rayman model was methodically employed throughout the research timeframe (Acero et al., 2022).

4. Discussion
4.1. The effect of humidity on thermal comfort at the same sound decibel interval
The current study delved into the dynamics of outdoor TSV within a population under the simultaneous influence of sound and
humidity. The results from this study lend support to the theory that an increase in humidity, within an environment where sound is
also a factor, elevates the outdoor TSV experienced by individuals. These findings are consistent with the empirical work conducted by
Berglund (Berglund, 1998) and align with the descriptions by Chen (Chen et al., 2011).
The results of this study intriguingly revealed that the population’s TSV displayed increased sensitivity in response to higher sound
decibels in the outdoor environment, indicating a complex interplay between auditory and thermal stimuli. Corroborating this, Yang
et al. observed that elevated environmental sound intensifies psychological stress, leading to a heightened perception of warmth (Yang
et al., 2022). Additionally, Abbasi et al. highlighted that the influence of combined environmental factors is more substantial than that
of individual factors alone (Abbasi et al., 2020). Consequently, with the increase in outdoor humidity, there is a notable rise in TSV.
Simultaneously, the amplification of the outdoor acoustic environment further escalates this increase in TSV. Therefore, it is essential
to consider both outdoor humidity and sound levels when analyzing their collective impact on thermal sensation.
In this study, we discovered that individuals in outdoor environments with lower sound levels (20 to 40 dB (A)) tended to report
less extreme thermal discomfort, reflected by a TCV of (cid:0) 3. This observation presents a contrast to the findings of Zhu et al., who
associated high humidity with increased discomfort`(Zhu et al., 2018), and Sachindra et al., who indicated a decrease in thermal
comfort with rising humidity (Sachindra and Nowosad, 2022). Our findings point to the possibility that quieter environments may
counterbalance the discomfort typically associated with high humidity, likely due to a decrease in psychological stress induced by
lower levels of acoustic stimulation.
In the context of this study, it is critical to acknowledge the limitations posed by the sparse data samples, particularly in the 20 to
40 dB (A) range and under conditions of low decibels combined with high humidity (20 to 40 dB (A), 70% RH to 85% RH). Therefore,
the conclusions drawn are more theoretical in nature and less grounded in practical application. The study brings to light the complex
dynamics between auditory and thermal sensations and their potential role in improving thermal comfort and reducing discomfort.
However, it is essential to recognize that these results are based on a limited scope of data analysis and carry a degree of speculation.
Future research endeavors should focus on a more detailed exploration of how subtle differences in sound and humidity in outdoor
settings influence human thermal comfort.
4.2. When the humidity range is the same, the sound affects the thermal comfort
The changing relationship between outdoor sound and TSV and TCV has been a topic of complexity. Scholars have worked on this
topic and obtained different results.
Several studies have elucidated the distinct relationship between sound and thermal comfort perceptions, specifically focusing on
TCV versus TSV. Notably, research by Yang, Moon et al.(Yang and Moon, 2018), as well as Juang et al. and Yang et al.(Juang et al.,
2010), revealed that while sound levels have a minimal impact on TSV, they exhibit a significant correlation with TCV. These studies
consistently found a substantial negative correlation between increased noise levels and TCV, indicating that higher sound levels lead
to a significant reduction in TCV, in contrast to their lesser influence on TSV. This underscores the nuanced impact of auditory stimuli
on different aspects of thermal perception in the outdoor environment.
Diverse studies have highlighted a significant relationship between sound and both TSV and TCV, offering a contrast to some earlier
research. Lau et al. demonstrated a notable negative correlation between TSV and sound intensity, emphasizing that TCV is also
substantially affected by the outdoor sound environment (Lau and Choi, 2021). Geng et al. identified that an agreeable acoustic
environment could lower subjective TSV and raise TCV (Geng et al., 2022). In certain circumstances, an increase in sound levels results
in a heightened TSV and a detrimental effect on TCV (Zhou et al., 2021), as observed by Zhou et al. Furthermore, research by Guan
et al. indicated that TSV values tend to rise with sound levels in neutral and warm conditions, and they documented a decline in TCV at
higher sound intensities (Guan et al., 2020). These findings collectively suggest a complex interplay between auditory stimuli and
thermal perception, extending the understanding of how environmental sounds influence thermal comfort and sensation.
In an intriguing departure from some established perspectives, research by scholars such as Fanger et al.(Fanger et al., 2007) has
shown that variations in sound levels do not correspond with changes in TSV and TCV. Specifically, their findings suggest that noise in
various environmental contexts lacks any significant correlation with shifts in TSV and TCV. Echoing this sentiment, Rahmaniar et al.
also reported that ambient sound had no notable impact on TSV and TCV (Rabiyanti et al., 2017), highlighting the potential inde-
pendence of thermal perception from auditory stimuli in certain environmental conditions.
The study conducted an experimental analysis of the correlation between sound TSV and TCV in environments where sound and
humidity intersect. Interestingly, the study found that changes in sound levels were linked to variations in TSV and TCV when humidity
levels ranged from 25% RH to 70% RH. However, no significant impact on TSV and TCV was observed in the higher humidity range of
70% RH to 85% RH. These findings are consistent with a study conducted by Raymond et al., (2017), which found that humidity,
particularly in extreme environments, primarily affects TSV and TCV. Therefore, it is essential to consider outdoor humidity levels
when studying the effects of sound on TSV and TCV, especially in conditions of elevated humidity.

4.3. Design strategy
This study elucidates that the modulation of sound levels and humidity in outdoor environments exerts a significant influence on
the TCV experienced by individuals. Consequently, it furnishes tailored design recommendations aimed at ameliorating outdoor
acoustic conditions and mitigating atmospheric moisture levels, as delineated in Fig. 9.
Fig. 9(a) illustrates the deployment of sound insulation facilities in proximate outdoor areas without compromising the educational
functionality of the campus. Wherein unsuitable for soundproofing panels, augmenting shrub density and establishing natural sound
barriers prove efficacious in attenuating the outdoor acoustic milieu (Arjunan et al., 2024).
Fig. 9(b) advocates the adoption of permeable pavement over impermeable alternatives in locales characterized by elevated hu-
midity levels (Du et al., 2023b). This strategic choice expedites surface water absorption, thereby lowering groundwater levels and
consequently ameliorating environmental humidity.
Fig. 9(c) underscores the deployment of active ventilation systems near high-humidity zones across the campus precinct (Mirzaei
and Haghighat, 2010). This initiative serves to accelerate outdoor airflow dynamics, facilitating expedited humidity dissipation and
hence, fostering a reduction in environmental moisture content.
A pivotal facet of campus planning entails prognosticating prevailing trends. As depicted in Fig. 9(d), the strategic planning of
ventilation corridors engenders a canyon effect within external building spaces. This mechanism enhances airflow dynamics and
localized ventilation, thereby engendering a diminution in overall campus humidity levels (Li et al., 2024).
4.4. Limitation and future work
The current study has some limitations. Primarily, the study was restricted to university campuses in colder climates, with college
students constituting the main study group.
Therefore, whether our conclusions are applicable to other climate regions and people of other age groups needs to be verified by
expanding the sample range. Furthermore, the study concentrated exclusively on the influence of the sound environment and humidity
on outdoor thermal comfort, excluding other factors that might also significantly affect thermal comfort, such as wind speed and solar
radiation. Future investigations should strive to integrate these additional elements to provide a more encompassing perspective of
outdoor thermal comfort. Additionally, the practicality and effectiveness of the design interventions proposed in this study should be
further validated through quantitative numerical simulations.
5. Conclusion
We collected experimental data under different humidity ranges and different sound decibel ranges on the Xi’a University campus.
This effort was specifically aimed at assessing the impact of sound and humidity environments on thermal comfort among the campus
population in open spaces. The results from this data collection provide compelling evidence of the interactive effects between hu-
midity and sound within the context of thermal comfort. The primary conclusions drawn from the study are:
(1) In the context of environments with both acoustic and humidity elements, the study revealed that a rise in humidity levels leads
to an increased sensation of heat, as indicated by the upward trend of the MTSV. Within a sound decibel range of 20 to 80 dB (A)
and humidity levels between 70% RH and 85% RH, the MPET exceeding 36 ◦C resulted in MTSV attaining and maintaining its
maximum value of 3. In contrast, under the same range of sound decibels, MTSV only reached this peak value at an MPET of
43 ◦C, but this occurred within a lower humidity range of 25% RH to 70% RH. This demonstrates the nuanced interaction
between humidity levels, sound environment, and perceived thermal sensation, particularly in varying humidity contexts.
(2) Within the context of environments incorporating both sound and humidity, the study observed a notable increase in outdoor
heat perception sensitivity as sound decibel levels escalated. The analysis across the decibel ranges of 20 to 40 dB (A), 40 to 60
dB (A), and 60 to 80 dB (A) revealed that the slopes of the MTSV and MPET fitted lines were 0.1307, 0.1482, and 0.1769,
respectively. Significantly, the slope in the 60–80 dB (A) range was much steeper compared to those in the 20–40 dB (A) and
40–60 dB (A) ranges. This pattern clearly illustrates that the sensitivity of MTSV to heat perception increases with higher sound
decibel values, underscoring the intricate relationship between auditory stimuli and thermal sensation in outdoor
environments.
(3) Within the combined influence of sound and humidity, our research highlights the imperative to consider the prevailing hu-
midity conditions when evaluating the effects of sound decibel intervals on MTSV and MTCV. The study observed that in
humidity ranges from 25% RH to 70% RH, fluctuations in sound levels significantly affected both MTSV and MTCV. However, in
more elevated humidity conditions (70% RH to 85% RH), a direct correlation between sound levels and the outcomes for MTCV
or MTSV was not evident. This underscores the critical role of outdoor humidity in the complex interplay between the acoustic
and thermal environments, particularly in assessing the joint impact on thermal comfort and sensation.
(4) Within environments influenced by both acoustic and humidity factors, the study identified that lower sound levels may be
instrumental in averting extreme thermal stress. Specifically, in lower decibel ranges, extreme thermal stress is observed at an
MPET of 48, where the MTCV and MTSV reach their peak values of (cid:0) 3 and 3, respectively. On the other hand, in higher decibel
ranges, the onset of extreme thermal stress is marked at a lower MPET of 43, with MTCV and MTSV achieving their limit values.
This phenomenon, however, appears to be specific to the given conditions and necessitates further verification through

The research highlights the pivotal role played by both the acoustic environment and humidity levels in optimizing outdoor human
thermal comfort. These findings are of significant value to architects, urban planners, and policymakers, offering them practical in-
