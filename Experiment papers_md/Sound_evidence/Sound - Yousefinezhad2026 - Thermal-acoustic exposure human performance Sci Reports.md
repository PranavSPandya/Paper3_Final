Occupants in many urban and occupational environmental settings are simultaneously exposed to
environmental traffic noise and heat stress. These combined exposures may have adverse effects
on physiological functions and cognitive performance. This study aimed to assess the effects of
concurrent exposure to traffic noise and thermal stress under controlled laboratory conditions. In
this crossover experimental study, 80 healthy participants (mean age: 25.3 ± 2.8 years) were exposed
to four exposure conditions: (1) control (comfort), (2) thermal (WBGT = 30.62 °C), (3) traffic noise (75
dB ), and (4) combined exposure (WBGT = 30.62 °C and75 dB ). Physiological parameters including
A A
heart rate, blood pressure, tympanic temperature, and mean skin temperature were recorded.
Cognitive performance was assessed using the Stroop test (for selective attention) and n-back test
(for working memory). Repeated-measures ANOVA was used for statistical analysis. Exposure to
combined heat and traffic noise produced the most pronounced physiological and cognitive alterations
among all test conditions. Core temperature rose significantly during noise (p = 0.008) and combined
exposure (p = 0.007). Diastolic blood pressure increased under thermal (p = 0.001) and combined
conditions (p < 0.001). Heart rate also elevated during combined exposure (p = 0.032). CPT reaction
time lengthened significantly across conditions (p = 0.001), with the thermal and combined situation
more than control (post hoc p < 0.05, p < 0.01). All n-back outcomes showed statistically significant
differences across exposure conditions at levels 2 and 3, and for incorrect responses and mean reaction
time at level 1 (p < 0.001). Simultaneous exposure to traffic noise and thermal stress amplifies both
physiological strain and cognitive impairment compared to single exposures. These findings highlight
the importance of integrated exposure assessment and risk mitigation strategies in occupational
environments, especially in warm and noisy settings.
Keywords Traffic noise, Themal stress, Environmental exposure, WBGT, Performance
Exposure to noise pollution, particularly traffic noise, impairs cognitive functions, leading to reduced accuracy
in brain activities, compromised cognitive coordination, and disruptions in communication and memory.
Traffic noise is recognized as an environmental stressor that degrades cognitive performance during mental
processing and elevates the risk of errors. For instance, complex cognitive tasks requiring high vigilance and
rapid reaction times—such as those critical for emergency response during driving—are significantly impaired
under traffic noise exposure1,2. This attentional impairment substantially diminishes individual performance
and, with prolonged exposure, may elevate the incidence of traffic accidents3. Exposure to environmental noise
(e.g., traffic) induces detrimental changes in precision, attention, and processing speed, concurrently heightening
psychological stress4. As an environmental stressor, traffic noise contributes to public health issues among urban
populations, including physiological and psychological disorders. Physiologically, it exerts deleterious effects on
cardiovascular activity, elevates blood pressure, increases myocardial infarction risk, and disrupts sympathetic

significant modifier of cognitive and mental performance in exposed populations6. Supporting this, Choi et al.
(2015) demonstrated elevated stress levels in subjects exposed to traffic noise through electroencephalographic
(EEG) analysis. Research indicates that increased alpha wave amplitude (µV) correlates with enhanced cognitive
performance, whereas attenuation reflects heightened psychological stress7.
Thermal stress represents another significant environmental stressor impairing cognitive performance in both
indoor and outdoor occupational settings. Heat exposure may originate from industrial processes or ambient
climatic conditions. Beyond inducing physiological disorders and heat-related illnesses, thermal stress triggers
adverse cognitive responses that degrade human performance8. As a physical hazard in occupational studies,
heat stress not only causes physiological impairments but also compromises cognitive functions—specifically
disrupting decision-making processes and the cognitive transition from simple to complex tasks8. Elevated core
body temperature appears to initiate a physiological cascade that alters mental states and impairs decision-making
capacity9. Empirical evidence indicates generalized reductions in processing speed and accuracy under elevated
ambient temperatures. Critically, thermal stress may potentiate noise-induced health effects through synergistic
interactions10. Notably, methodological limitations in single-stressor cognitive models have drawn increasing
criticism in recent years. Studies examining isolated stressors exhibit significant contradictions, methodological
shortcomings, and interpretive limitations8,11. Consequently, researchers now advocate investigating combined
environmental exposures as a more valid approach to assess physiological and cognitive health impacts12. Guan
et al. (2020) demonstrated that speech intelligibility across varying sound pressure levels (SPL) is significantly
modulated by ambient thermal conditions13. In a simultaneous noise-climate exposure study, Abbasi et al.
(2020) observed that high-intensity noise exposure (compared to low-intensity) reduced alpha wave amplitude
(µV) across all temperature regimes14. This amplitude attenuation confirms cognitive performance degradation
during concurrent exposure task execution. Chen et al. conducted controlled experiments in a climate chamber,
exposing 14 healthy young adults to combined noise and thermal stimuli. They concluded that heat stress
exacerbates noise-induced temporary threshold shift (TTS)15. Furthermore, studies revealed that thermal
comfort was significantly compromised by concurrent noise exposure during combined stressor conditions16,17.
Over recent decades, the impact of environmental factors on human comfort, health, and performance has
been extensively examined in both laboratory and field settings. Although a growing number of studies have
adopted multi-domain approaches and investigated combined environmental stressors, including interactions
between thermal conditions and noise18,19, evidence regarding their joint effects on human responses remains
heterogeneous. While some investigations have reported significant interactions between noise and thermal
stress on physiological, perceptual, or cognitive outcomes particularly under moderate-to-high exposure
levels13,14,20, Also others have not observed clear interaction effects, potentially due to differences in sound
pressure levels, temperature ranges, task demands, or controlled office-like settings21,22. This inconsistency
indicates that the physiological and cognitive consequences of concurrent thermal–acoustic exposure are not
yet fully understood and merit further systematic study. From a methodological perspective, prior research
has often focused on limited sets of outcome measures, frequently emphasizing subjective ratings of comfort,
annoyance, or performance (e.g., thermal sensation votes, noise annoyance scales), and only partially integrating
objective physiological and cognitive indicators during simultaneous exposure conditions13. This constrains the
ability to elucidate mechanistic pathways and link perceived states with measurable functional impairment.
Unlike prior work23, which primarily examined thermal comfort interactions in general indoor environments
using subjective assessments among healthy adults, the present study investigates traffic-derived noise combined
with heat stress on a broader array of objective physiological and task-based cognitive performance measures
within a controlled simulation replicating real urban traffic and climatic conditions of Sari, Iran.
Materials and methods
Study design and participants
In this within-subjects crossover experimental study with repeated measures design, 80 healthy university
students (40 males, 40 females; mean age 26.9 ± 2.8 years, range 22–28 years) from Mazandaran University of
Medical Sciences were exposed to all four experimental conditions (control comfort, thermal WBGT = 30.6 °C,
traffic noise 75 dBA, combined exposure) on separate days (maximum one scenario per day) with randomized
scenario sequence to eliminate order effects. All participants were from the same academic cohort, ensuring
homogeneity in age, education, and lifestyle factors. Inclusion criteria required: (1) self-reported absence of
systemic disease or clinical disorders, (2) normal audiometric thresholds (≤ 25 dB HL, 0.5–4 kHz), and (3) visual
acuity ≥ 20/25 (Snellen E-chart). Pre-test protocols mandated ≥ 8 h of sleep and abstinence from caffeinated
beverages (tea/coffee/chocolate) ≥ 4 h pre-assessment. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) psychiatric conditions
or psychoactive medication use, (2) tobacco/alcohol consumption within 24 h, (3) study withdrawal, or (4)
incomplete data. Voluntary participation with written informed consent was obtained following institutional
ethics board approval. In this study, incentives were provided to enhance participant motivation and ensure
diligent and accurate execution of the experiments, despite the time-intensive and sometimes repetitive nature of
the testing procedures. Consequently, all participants successfully completed the experimental sessions. Prior to
each experiment, informed consent was obtained following a comprehensive explanation of the study protocol.
Moreover, participants were allowed to choose their preferred day and time for test administration. Participants
wore standardized light summer clothing ensembles (Light office clothing Clo ≈ 0.5–0.7) to minimize inter-
individual thermal resistance variation, consistent with moderate office work attire (metabolic rate ~ 1.2 met)24.

Reproduction and anechoic chamber (traffic noise survey, produce and playback)
Prior to generating and presenting urban traffic noise (specific to Sari) to participants, field measurements of
traffic noise across Sari’s streets were required. Measurements were conducted on Saturdays (first workday) from
8:00–11:00 AM to replicate typical traffic exposure conditions and align with peak vehicle flow in congested
urban corridors (12 measurement points). Based on community noise reports, eleven high-traffic locations were
selected for 3-h recording sessions. Traffic noise was captured during summer under controlled conditions: sunny
weather, windless, dry asphalt, with vehicles maintaining approximately 30 km/h. Recording utilized a SONY

A-weighting network measured equivalent continuous sound levels (LAeq) and frequency spectra. The device
was positioned at 120 cm height and 2 m from road edges during 30-minute sampling intervals. Measurement
sites avoided reflective surfaces (vegetation, buildings) to minimize acoustic interference. Laboratory post-

Human performance
Physiological parameters
Physiological parameters: As illustrated in Fig. 1 (exposure protocol schematic), prior to activation of each
experimental scenario, participants underwent a 15 minutes’ rest period in the environmental chamber
(thermoneutral and background noise: comfort conditions) for initial acclimatization, followed by 5-minute
baseline monitoring of heart rate, core body temperature, and blood pressure. Stressors (thermal and/or traffic
noise) were then activated, and these parameters were reassessed during minutes 1–5 (initial exposure phase)
and the final 5 min of each 30-minute exposure session, consistent with the timeline shown in Fig. 1. Core
temperature was monitored tympanically using a Beurer FT78 infrared thermometer. Blood pressure (systolic/
diastolic) and heart rate were recorded oscillometrically using a Beurer BM16 digital sphygmomanometer. This
standardized protocol—baseline under thermoneutral/quiet conditions followed by scenario-specific stressor
activation—was implemented consistently across all four experimental scenarios.
Cognitive performance
Sustained attention and vigilance were evaluated using the Continuous Performance Test (CPT), while
working memory was assessed through a three-level n-back task, serving as cognitive performance measures
under different exposure conditions28,29. The CPT requires continuous monitoring of rapidly presented visual
stimuli and fast discrimination between target and non-target items, yielding omission errors, commission
errors, and reaction time as indices of attentional lapses, response inhibition failures, and processing speed,
respectively. These processes are directly relevant to real-world street and traffic behaviour, where individuals
must continuously scan the environment, detect critical events (e.g., pedestrians, vehicles, signals), and
respond quickly and accurately to avoid errors. The n-back task engages online maintenance and updating of
information under increasing cognitive load (levels 1–3), providing reaction time and accuracy measures that
reflect working memory capacity and executive control. Such functions support complex decision-making in
dynamic environments, including route selection, adaptation to changing traffic conditions, and integration of
multiple concurrent stimuli. Together, CPT and n-back therefore capture key cognitive domains that underpin
safe performance in urban traffic contexts and have been widely used in prior human studies investigating
environmental effects on attention, vigilance, and working memory. In the CPT, 150 numbers appear at specified
time intervals, with one stimulus designated as the target stimulus. Participants were instructed to respond by
pressing the corresponding key on the keyboard as quickly as possible upon presentation of the target numbers.
The measured variables in this study include omission errors, commission errors, and reaction or response
time (milliseconds). The n-back ( as a working memory task) is a cognitive performance assessment related
to executive functions. This test has three complexity levels, and all three levels—first (n1), second (n2), and
third (n3)—were utilized in this research. Level 1 procedure: Numbers 1 to 9 appear randomly on the screen
for a short duration; participants must remember the first number and wait for the second number. After the
second number appears, if it matches the previous number, they press the (correct) key as quickly as possible; if
different, they press the (incorrect) key, continuing this pattern throughout the test. Level 2 procedure follows
the same method as Level 1 but compares the first number with the third, second with fourth, and so forth. In
this test, mean reaction time and correct responses can be extracted. Level 3 procedure follows the same method
as Level 1 but compares the first number with the fourth, second with fifth, and so forth. In this test, mean
reaction time and correct responses can be extracted30–32. Cognitive performance assessments were conducted
for each participant under four scenarios: (1) thermal comfort conditions, (2) heat stress exposure, (3) traffic
noise exposure, and (4) simultaneous heat stress and traffic noise exposure. Notably, to standardize procedures
and prevent laboratory errors due to peak cognitive performance hours (approximately 8:00–12:00), all testing
occurred within this fixed daily timeframe (8:00–12:00).
Statistical analysis

assessed the normality of the dependent variables across the four exposure conditions using the Shapiro–Wilk
test, along with visual inspections of histograms and Q-Q plots. All primary physiological and cognitive outcomes
exhibited approximately normal distributions within each condition. Since all 80 participants completed each
of the four exposure scenarios, the dataset forms a fully balanced within-subjects repeated-measures design,
making it suitable for repeated-measures ANOVA. We examined the sphericity assumption for the within-
subject factor (exposure condition), and where necessary, we applied standard corrections (e.g., Greenhouse–
Geisser adjustment) to the degrees of freedom for the F-tests. Repeated-measures ANOVA was employed as the
primary inferential method for normally distributed outcomes, while the non-parametric Friedman test was

Physiological parameters (Table 2) showed significant within-condition changes (main effects): core
temperature increased during traffic noise exposure (p = 0.008), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) during thermal
exposure (p = 0.001), and DBP (p < 0.001), heart rate (p = 0.032), and core temperature (p = 0.007) during
combined exposure. Pairwise comparisons between exposure conditions showed that diastolic blood pressure
(DBP) was significantly higher in the combined heat–noise condition than in traffic noise alone at both the
beginning (p = 0.010) and end (p = 0.013) of exposure, and higher in combined than thermal exposure at
the beginning (p = 0.033). Systolic blood pressure exhibited a smaller but consistent trend, with a significant
difference for combined versus traffic noise at the end of exposure (p = 0.018) and a near-significant difference at
the beginning (p = 0.053). These results indicate that the combined condition produces greater circulatory strain
than either single stressor, particularly for DBP, even though the magnitude of between-condition differences
is modest. The absolute differences in DBP between combined and traffic-only conditions were approximately
4–6 mmHg at both time points, suggesting a small but physiologically relevant increment in vascular load under
dual exposure.

Discussion
Core temperature demonstrated statistically significant changes during both traffic noise exposure and combined
traffic noise-heat stress exposure across experimental conditions. Hemmatjou et al. similarly reported elevated
core temperature under concurrent heat and noise exposure33,34. High noise levels induce physiological stress,
triggering adrenaline secretion and peripheral vasoconstriction that elevates blood pressure. Additionally, noise
exposure alters heart rate, reduces cardiac output, and increases respiratory rate, ultimately leading to body
temperature fluctuations35.
Statistically significant correlations were identified between initial and final systolic blood pressure
measurements during combined traffic noise-heat stress exposure and corresponding initial and final systolic
blood pressure measurements during traffic noise exposure alone. In a 2015 study by Dehghan et al. examining
the effects of simultaneous noise and heat exposure on blood pressure changes in men, combined exposure
resulted in a slight increase in blood pressure - consistent with the current study’s findings36. Sepehri et al.
(2019) similarly reported significant differences in mean systolic blood pressure changes across heat levels
during exposure to annoying noise (55–75 dB), aligning with our findings of BP elevation under thermal-
acoustic stress. Like our laboratory study, theirs used a climatic chamber with healthy male students to examine
combined effects, though their focus was low air temperatures (14–22 °C) rather than our WBGT = 30.6 °C
summer simulation. Both demonstrate cardiovascular sensitivity to physical stressor interactions, supporting
the physiological plausibility of our urban exposure model37.
Significant associations were observed for diastolic blood pressure during both heat stress exposure and
combined exposure to traffic noise and heat stress. Increased heat stress elevates core body temperature, leading
to heat-related disorders such as heat cramps, heat exhaustion, and heat stroke, consequently altering blood
pressure levels38. Concurrently, noise exposure triggers short-term physiological responses via the autonomic
nervous system resulting in elevated blood pressure, heart rate, and peripheral vasoconstriction. In a similar
study by Dehghan et al. (2015) examining the combined effects of noise and heat on blood pressure in males,
concurrent exposure caused a slight increase in blood pressure, aligning with the present findings36.
Diastolic blood pressure during combined traffic noise and heat stress exposure significantly differed from
exposure to traffic noise alone. Post-exposure diastolic pressure under combined conditions showed a significant
increase compared to isolated noise exposure. This phenomenon occurs because heat exposure initially alters
intravascular fluid volume, subsequently affecting blood pressure. Subsequent sympathetic stress responses)
and endocrine activation elevate blood pressure, heart rate, stress hormones, and notably oxidative stress,
which contributes to vascular dysfunction and cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension, blood viscosity,
hyperglycemia, coagulation activation)39. Previous studies corroborate these results, demonstrating significant
blood pressure elevation following noise exposure40.
Heart rate variability exhibited significant associations during combined noise and heat stress exposure. This
may stem from synergistic effects of traffic noise and heat on stress pathways and endocrine function, elevating
both blood pressure and heart rate39. A closely related study by Wang et al. on Chinese steelworkers exposed to
noise, heat, or both revealed that physical stressors affect heart rate, with combined exposure causing the most
pronounced increase41. Conversely, Jafari et al. reported that heart rate changes during isolated or combined
noise/heat exposure were less pronounced than systolic/diastolic blood pressure alterations42.

Working memory performance (as measured by total incorrect responses across Levels 1–3) showed
significant associations with different exposure conditions. Traffic noise exposure increased error rates, indicating
impaired attentional capacity. This may stem from noise-induced stress, which reduces alpha wave amplitude
(µV) during exposure, thereby increasing mental workload and diminishing cognitive performance7. Monteiro
et al. demonstrated that noise exposure significantly elevates error rates and reduces attention levels43, while
Tzivian et al. (2017) reported similar findings for sustained attention44. Contrary to Level 1 results, error rates in
Levels 2 and 3 of the same tests decreased significantly during exposure to traffic noise, heat stress, or combined
conditions compared to the no-exposure baseline. This aligns with Vasmatzidis et al. (2002), who found no
significant link between noise/heat exposure and cognitive performance in exposed vs. control groups45. In this
regard, the pre-test training effect, which repeated task practice minimised gross estimation errors, exists, and
another factor is the Educational attainment influence, that Participants’ education levels (high school diploma
to doctoral degrees) correlated with enhanced cognitive resilience. Most studies reporting contradictory findings
involved populations with < 12 years of formal education.
Among other findings of this study, a significant association can be mentioned for the cognitive factor
of working memory in the variable of correct response percentage in levels 2 and 3 with different exposure
conditions; that is to say, the percentage of correct responses during exposure to traffic noise, heat stress, or
combined exposure to traffic noise and heat stress compared to the no-exposure condition has increased.
Therefore, contrary to the expected result, exposure to various intervention factors has no effect on the
percentage of correct responses in different levels of the mentioned test. McDonnell et al. (2021) in a study
investigating the relationship between road traffic noise and cognitive performance also found no evidence
indicating an association between noise exposure and memory with processing speed, and this may be due to
small sample size46. In this research, similar to the study by Golbabaei et al. (2014), it was not possible to stratify
individuals based on education level, which could be one of the reasons for the difference in the results of this
study compared to some other researches47. Also, this matter could be due to the pre-test training and repetition
of previous test stages, which leads to individuals having more mastery and higher skill, and as a result, the
percentage of correct responses in each stage increases compared to the no-exposure stage.
The obtained findings showed that the cognitive factor of working memory in the variable of mean response
time of all levels 1–3 had a significant association with different exposure conditions; that is to say, the mean
response time during combined exposure to traffic noise and heat stress compared to the heat stress condition
has increased. Sepehri and his colleagues also encountered such similar results in their research in 2017. They
found that the combined effect of noise and air temperature on reaction time was greater than the effect of each
of them separately37. Abbasi and colleagues also in 2018 [1397] found that the combined effects of noise and air
temperature are greater than the effect of each of them alone14.
Regarding the present study’s data concerning mean response time in levels 1–3, there is a significant decrease;
that is to say, the mean response time during exposure to traffic noise, heat stress, or combined exposure to traffic
noise and heat stress compared to the no-exposure condition has decreased. Smith reached a similar result in
this field that the combined effects of noise and heat on cognitive functions are not related to each other. Also,
in studies by Hygge and Yang, no interaction between noise and heat stress was reported21,22. Also, this matter
could be due to the pre-test training and repetition of previous test stages, which leads to individuals having
more mastery and higher skill, and as a result, the mean response time in each stage decreases compared to the
next stage.
Regarding the present study’s data, reaction time in the heat stress condition has shown a significant increase
because heat causes reduction of blood supply to the brain and leads to reduction of mental performance in
individuals48,49. These findings match the results of Gaoua’s study in 2011. He found that with increasing heat,
mental performance of individuals decreases50. Also, Habibi and colleagues found that heat through reduction
of precision during cognitive activities causes reduction of mental performance in individuals51.
The results of the present study indicate that traffic noise and heat stress have a reciprocal interaction and
can increase each other’s adverse effects. Because due to heat, blood supply to the brain is reduced and causes
reduction of mental performance in individuals48. Also, due to traffic noise, because of creating high stress
during exposure, mental workload increases and mental performance of the brain decreases7. In a study that
Chen and colleagues conducted, also such similar results were obtained and it was determined that combined
exposure to noise and heat stress causes impairment in memory15. Also, in the study by Abbasi and colleagues,
combined effects of noise and air temperature on working memory were greater than the effect of each of them
alone14. In the case that Zamanian and Smith in this regard found that the combined effects of noise and heat on
cognitive functions are not related to each other52,53.
This study was conducted in a controlled acoustic chamber that allowed precise simulation of traffic noise
(based on field measurements) with uniform distribution and specific intensity variation, enabling isolation
of thermal-acoustic effects on continuous cognitive performance. The ability to independently control heat
stress parameters alongside realistic urban noise playback represents a key methodological strength. However,
several limitations should be noted. The sample consisted of young, healthy university students with likely
prior awareness of heat and noise effects due to their medical sciences background, which may have influenced
subjective responses. This homogeneous group does not represent vulnerable populations, such as older outdoor
workers, and thus limits generalizability to occupational settings. The laboratory environment, while ecologically
valid for controlled urban simulation, cannot fully capture real-world factors like prolonged exposure, physical
workload, or individual acclimatization. Additional other affectiong variables such as personality traits, acoustic
characteristics, and thermal radiation properties may also influence outcomes. The study was not powered for
subgroup analyses (e.g., gender interactions), and future research should validate these findings in diverse, high-
risk cohorts.

Conclusion
This controlled experimental study provides robust evidence that simultaneous exposure to traffic noise and
thermal stress significantly compromises both physiological homeostasis and cognitive functioning in young
adults. While individual exposures to either traffic noise or heat stress elicited only modest physiological
alterations, the combined exposure produced synergistic effects, leading to marked elevations in diastolic blood
pressure, heart rate, and core body temperature, as well as significant delays in reaction time and diminished
working memory performance.
The application of continuous performance testing (CPT) and multi-level n-back tasks enabled a
comprehensive evaluation of sustained attention and executive cognitive capacity. The findings indicate that
co-exposure to environmental stressors impairs cognitive efficiency to a greater extent than single-stressor
scenarios, reinforcing the emerging consensus on the interactivity of physical workplace hazards.
Given the methodological rigor of this study—including a randomized crossover design, standardized
physiological measurements, and tightly controlled exposure settings—these results highlight the need
for integrated occupational exposure limits (OELs) that consider multi-stressor environments rather than
addressing each hazard in isolation. Further investigations in real-world occupational contexts, across broader
exposure durations and heterogeneous worker populations, are warranted to improve ecological validity and
inform evidence-based policy and intervention strategies.
