marked sexual dimorphism in thermo-acoustic perception, primarily within nature-dominant environments.
Males exhibited cardiovascular attenuation under slight thermal stress, with systolic pressure declining 6.08
mmHg (medium diversity) and diastolic pressure 6.61 mmHg (high diversity) versus low diversity (p <0.05),
though physiological responsivity diminished as thermal stress escalated. Females conversely demonstrated
intensified physiological reactions with increasing thermal load, including reduced heart rate under no thermal
stress conditions in anthropogenic settings (p <0.05). In nature-dominant sound environments, low soundscape
diversity attenuated male δ power while potentiating α power, consistent with diminished somnolence and
heightened relaxation; elevated female θ power, conversely, indexed enhanced positive affect. Psychologically,
males demonstrated consistently higher sensitivity, while females exhibited a biphasic pattern of initial atten-
uation followed by enhancement. In anthropogenic-dominant environments, males displayed heightened phys-
iological and psychological sensitivity to acoustic diversity, whereas females showed asynchronous patterns:
physiological responses weakened then strengthened, while psychological responses progressively intensified
with increasing thermal stress. Our findings inform sex-sensitive acoustic optimization frameworks for urban
design, advancing evidence-based healthy built environment strategies.
