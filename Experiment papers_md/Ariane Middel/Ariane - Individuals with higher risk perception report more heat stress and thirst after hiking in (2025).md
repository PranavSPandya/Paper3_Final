1.Introduction
Outdoor recreation activities such as hiking are driven by a sense of self-development, freedom, control,
or thrill, but also pose inherent risks [1]. One such risk is heat injury. Over 200 hikers are rescued every
year due to heat injury or dehydration within the Phoenix, Arizona (AZ) hiking trail system [2]. To mit-
igate heat injury risk, the City of Phoenix launched the ‘Take a Hike, Do it Right’ campaign in 2015.
The campaign educates the public about the risks and signs of exertional heat illness and ways to mitig-
ate its onset. Although much is known about the physiology of heat stress and dehydration [3, 4], little
is known about how these processes affect risk perception (RP) and the associated risk-taking behavi-
ors, especially among hikers during strenuous hikes in hot environments. The elements of self-reported
RP and risk-taking are multifaceted decision-making processes [5] influenced by the type of activity
being engaged, environmental conditions, and the individual’s fitness and adaptation level [3, 4, 6].

Understanding the relationship between physiological responses to extreme heat and RP is therefore
imperative to reduce risk behavior in hikers.
The human body is tightly controlled to maintain a narrow core body temperature range of 36 ◦C–
38 ◦C, which can easily increase to 40 ◦C or beyond during exercise or in hot environments [7]. When
the internal body temperature rises above 40 ◦C, there is an increased risk of heat injury/illness, which is
higher when exercising in hot environments in a dehydrated state [8–10]. In daily life, continued expos-
ure to heat stress leads to multiple metabolic alterations to better handle the heat, i.e. metabolic and
physiological adaptations that allow for better fluid balance, increased cardiovascular stability, improved
skeletal muscle metabolism, and increased heat tolerance [11, 12]. However, without proper hydration or
cool-seeking behaviors, heat illness may occur [8–10]. Exercise combined with heat stress leads to exer-
tional heat illness that is often characterized by elevated core body temperature, muscle cramping, heat
exhaustion, or heatstroke [13].
While the effects of heat stress and dehydration on physiological function are widely studied [7],
the interaction between these physiological states and RP and/or risk behavior during hiking exercise
is not well documented. Heat stress can impact the ability to perform both simple and complex tasks
[14, 15], and acute heat exposure in an occupational work environment can decrease RP with increased
risk-taking behaviors as a result [16]. Therefore, elevated ambient temperatures may increase the pre-
valence of unsafe behaviors [17]. Although mild to moderate dehydration has been shown to produce
decrements in aspects of cognitive function such as perceptual discrimination, visuomotor tracking, and
psychomotor skills [18, 19], other studies show inconsistent results [20, 21]. Within the scope of dehyd-
ration studies, research mainly focuses on the effects of fluid on cognitive processes or performance, but
no specific research has investigated the relationship between markers of hydration and RP in hikers.
Field studies in parks and recreation management have explored RP and risk behavior in relation to
hiking, identifying the components of risk as physical, social, and psychological, classifying RP into sub-
jective and objective categories and uncovering the prevention of risk behavior [3, 6, 22, 23]. Although
the fields of health and recreation have investigated hiker safety from a physiological and psychoso-
cial perspective respectively, there is a relative paucity in research integrating the interaction of these
two distinct domains. For example, studies looked at different areas of experienced risk in hiking, for
example identifying, physical and psychological dimensions of perceived risk in hiking without integrat-
ing physiological data to assess its relationship with RP [23]. Or they defined personal risk in outdoor
recreation, but without reporting any objective physiological outcomes related to risk measures [24, 25].
Finally, investigators have looked at the physiological impact of trails (as a result of trail difficulty), but
in that case not addressing RP [26].
Therefore, this study integrates physiological and psychosocial data to offer a more comprehensive
understanding of risk behaviors and perceptions in extreme heat conditions. Specifically, we examine the
relationship between RP, subjective measures (such as, perceived exertion, heat stress, and thirst), and
objective physiological parameters (including core body temperature, hydration status, and cardiorespir-
atory fitness). This interdisciplinary approach aims to elucidate how physiological and perceptual factors
interact to influence hiker safety under thermal stress.
Based on previous literature, the following hypotheses were tested while categorizing participants
accordingly to low vs. high RP scores:
H1: Self-reported perceived exertion, heat stress, and thirst after a hike in hot environmental condi-
tions are higher in hikers with high RP scores.
H2: After a hike in hot environmental conditions, indirect markers of heat stress, such as high core
body temperature, a high urine concentration, and a low fitness level are positively associated with
higher RP scores.
H3: Familiarity with heat risk and mitigation strategies, including the degree of preparation for the
heat, while using hydration strategies, is higher in hikers with high RP scores.
2.Methodsandmaterials
During the summer of 2021, data was collected at the Piestewa Peak Summit Trail located in the City
of Phoenix, AZ. This mountain trail is rated as extremely difficult by the City’s Parks and Recreation
Department and is very popular among city dwellers, for example to train for a hike in the Grand
Canyon National Park. During the summer of 2022, data was collected at a lower profile mountain trail
close to a university setting. This trail is rated easy and is popular amongst regular hikers. During the
first year, trained researchers systematically and randomly approached potential participants post-hike
from July to October 2021. Sampling occurred mostly on weekends and in the morning to mimic typical

summer use. During the second year, the study was advertised using social media ads, and targeted par-
ticipants who were less active compared to the previous sample. Specifically, participants who exercised
once a week or less were sought to ensure a wider range of fitness levels was represented in the cohort.
Sampling occurred in June and July of 2022. Data collection for both years was performed at the end of
the hike.
Regardless of data collection year, the data collection was divided into two parts: first, upon agree-
ing to participate in the study, participant’s heart rate was measured, and a thermal image was taken
to allow participant classification based on their body temperature. Participants then completed a 10–
15 min on-site, self-administered exit questionnaire. The second part included two additional physiolo-
gical measurements involving taking a urine sample and performing an exercise step test. Participants
could opt out of the additional element of data collection at any point.
2.1.Participants
The study included all interested participants who were at least 18 years of age and who hiked for
30 min or more. During the second year, participants who exercised once per week or less were
recruited to offset the high fitness level of the first-year cohort. A minimum of 289 participants was
needed to achieve a confidence level of 95% with a margin of error of 5%. This study was approved
by the Institutional Review Board at AZ State University (STUDY00014107), and informed consent was
included.
2.2.Procedures
Upon completing their hike, the selected participants signed the informed consent and were instruc-
ted to refrain from drinking or eating anything during Part I of the measurements (∼10 min) so as
not to interfere with the subjective measurements. All participants were assigned a subject ID to ensure
anonymity within the data set.
PartI: A headshot was taken with a thermal camera to record body temperature immediately after
each participant agreed to their involvement in the study by signing consent. Participants were then
seated to complete a 10 min online questionnaire via their mobile phone or staff-supplied tablet while
a pulse oximeter simultaneously recorded the heart rate. During this time, a researcher analyzed the size
and contents of the fluid container(s) hikers brought with them to the trail, allowing for an estimation
of the volume of fluid carried and consumed during the hike.
PartII: For those who agreed, a urine sample was collected to assess hydration status. Additionally,
a modified YMCA step test [27], was performed to determine cardiorespiratory fitness. Finally, during
each data collection day ambient environmental conditions such as ambient temperature and humidity
were registered.
2.3.Measurements
Questionnaire: A 30-question online questionnaire (supplementary file 1) was used to collect self-
reported data. The questions were categorized as follows: personal demographics, RP, scales reflecting
self-reported physiological outcomes, and familiarity with heat risk and mitigation strategies.
Personaldemographicsandhikingduration(#10questions): Body height (reported as feet and inches, con-
verted to centimeters), body mass (reported as pounds, converted to kg), age (years), sex/gender, ethni-
city, race, employment status, highest education level, income, and duration of the hike (minutes).
RP(#2questions): The combined RP score utilized in our study was adapted from two established instru-
ments described by Kalkstein & Sheridan in which participants were asked to rate how dangerous they
perceived the Phoenix heat to be [28], as well as, She etal in which participants were asked to assess
concerns related to hiking [23]. These scales have been previously employed in studies assessing RP in
similar contexts. This resulted in one question about how dangerous the Phoenix heat is perceived to be
by the participants (not at all dangerous to extremely dangerous, scale 1–5) [28], and 15 questions about
worries during hiking related to RP, such as worry about accidents, slips or falling during the hike (15
elements: strongly disagree to strongly agree, scale 1–5) [23].
Rateofperceivedexertion,heatstress,andthirstscales(#3questions): Rate of perceived exertion (Borg scale
6–20) [29], heat stress scale (very mild to excruciating unbearable, scale 0–10) [30], and thirst scale (not
thirsty at all to extremely thirsty, scale 0–100) [31].
Familiaritywithheatriskandmitigationstrategies(#5questions): Awareness of the Phoenix heat warning
system (yes/no); awareness of the City of Phoenix ‘Take a Hike, Do It Right’ campaign (yes/no) and if
yes, familiarity with this campaign and its pre-hiking safety strategies (eight elements, scale 1–5) [2].

Physiologicalmarkers:
ThermalImagingasamarkerforbodytemperature: A handheld infrared camera image (FLIR E95,

seated on a stool in front of a grey brick wall. The forehead is considered to reasonably reflect core body
temperature if adjusting for the fact that the skin temperature at that point is around 1 ◦C lower than
the actual body core temperature [32], as no further temperature adjustments were made the variable

perature of ∼26 ◦C, with emissivity set at 0.98 for human skin, to take a picture, and then brought back
outside. Participants were photographed within 30–60 s of entering the room. A heat map of the head-

ature and humidity for measurements were inserted in the program parameters, and a 3 × 3 pixel area
of the internal canthus of the eye was selected [33] to retrieve the average, minimum, and maximum
body temperature.
Hydrationstatus: Urine was collected in a 32 fl oz (∼990 ml) urine container. The hydration status was
quantified by analyzing the urine sample via urine specific gravity refractometry (Atago 4410 PAL-
10S Digital Handheld Pocket Urine S.G. Refractometer, 1–1.06 urine specific gravity range, Bellevue,
Washington). Each sample was measured twice, if the measurements differed by more than 0.005, a third
measurement was taken, and the median value was accepted.
Fluidbroughtandfluidconsumed: All fluid containers brought on the hike were inspected by a research
team member, and the volume of the container was estimated using a predetermined list of the most
frequent sizes of bottles, containers, and water bags. Then, the research team member inquired how
much fluid was available in each container before the start of the hike to estimate the total volume con-
sumed by subtracting the remaining fluid.

2.4.CalculatingRPscoresandstratifications
To calculate total heat-related RP scores, the outcomes of the following two questions were combined:
(1) ‘how dangerous you feel the Phoenix heat is for you.’ Subjects answered on a five-point Likert scale
(1–5, not at all dangerous to extremely dangerous), for five possible points. (2)‘When hiking, I worry
about…’ they responded on a five-point Likert scale (1–5, not at all worried to extremely worried) and
with 15 separate elements (removing the elements on infectious disease and contaminated foods) for 75
possible points. Combining the two questions resulted in a minimum score of 16 and a maximum score
of 80, where higher scores indicate a higher RP (supplementary file 1, questions 16 and 17).
Median RP scores were used to split participants into a high-RP (HRP) and a low-RP (LRP) group.
The thermal imaging body temperature was stratified based on group median. Self-reported rates of per-
ceived exertion, heat stress, and thirst perception were stratified using the group median. Additionally,
body temperature was split into low vs. high values based on the median. Hydration levels were categor-
ized as euhydrated (USG < 1.020) and underhydrated (USG ⩾ 1.020). Fluid carried and consumed were
stratified into low vs. high groups based on the median. Finally, participants were categorized by fitness
level based on the median-estimated VO max values (low vs. high).

3.Results
Out of 901 hikers that were approached, 340 (37.7%) agreed to participate in the study. A total of
n =319 participants were recruited in year 1 on the extremely difficult trail, and 21 hikers were
recruited in year 2 on the easy trail. As no clear differences were seen in outcomes between groups,
they were combined (p > 0.05). Height, weight, BMI, and age were normally distributed. Participants
(of which 42.9% were female) reported an average age of 40.6 ± 12.9 yr (table 1). The median hiking
duration of the participants was 90 min (IQR: 60–120 min) without a significant difference between
RP groups (p⩾ 0.71). The average ambient temperature was 31.4 ± 2.9 ◦C with 46.9 ± 12.4% relat-
ive humidity, without a significant difference between RP groups (p⩾ 0.24). A total of 73.1% (n = 249)
of the participants had no complaints during their hike. The most common complaints of the remaining
26.9% (n = 91) included being light-headed or dizzy (9.4%, n = 32), or having muscle cramps (5.1%,
n = 17) or the urge to urinate (4.4%, n = 15).

The strength of the relationship between outcomes, including the earlier reported significant difference
for heat stress and thirst between RP groups, was tested by running Spearman correlations. RP was sig-
nificantly and positively correlated with self-reported heat perception (r(331) = 0.230, p< 0.01), indic-

3.5.Heatriskandmitigationstrategies
A total of 76.5% (n = 257) of the participants were familiar with the Phoenix heat warning system,
with no difference between risk groups (low RP: 77.2%, n = 139 vs. high RP: 75.6% (n = 118), with
X2 (1) = 0.12, p= 0.733). When asking whether they were familiar with the City of Phoenix ‘Take a
Hike, Do it Right’ campaign, 42.9% (n = 146) reported to be familiar with this campaign, with the low
RP group reporting a numerically lower familiarity (38.3%, n = 69) than the high RP group (49.4%,
n = 77)(X2 (1) = 4.14, p= 0.042).
The subgroup reporting to know about the ‘Take a Hike, Do it Right’ campaign was also asked how
familiar they were with its recommendations toward hiker safety, as listed in table 4. The internal con-
sistency of the reported data in the table was high (Cronbach’s α of 0.980). Most of the hikers (n = 146)
were familiar with the advice to bring water (5.0 [4.0–5.0]), followed by taking responsibility (4.0 [4.0–
5.0]), and keeping in contact with others (4.0 [3.0–5.0]. The least known advice was to watch the
weather (3.0 [2.0–4.0]). No significant differences were found in familiarity with the campaign between
low and high RP groups (p⩾ 0.075).
Preparationfortheheat
When participants were asked if they applied specific clothing choices to prepare for or mitigate heat
stress, they reported using a hat (54.4%, n = 185), sunglasses (47.9%, n = 163), a cool shirt (45.6%,
n = 155), a cool vest (2.4%, n = 8), or clothes soaked in water (2.1%, n = 7), but the usage of these
items did not differ between RP groups (p⩾ 0.116). Only the use of a cool scarf or bandana (8.8%,
n = 30) significantly differed between low RP (5.4%, n= 10) and high RP (12.8%, n = 20), with
p= 0.017 and small to moderate effect size V = 0.13. Finally, of all participants, 14.1% (n = 48) repor-
ted they did not use any of these strategies.
Hydrationstrategies
As shown in table 5, today’s temperature (65.9%, n = 224) and planned duration of the hike (66.2%,
n = 225) were the most popular responses to determine how much fluid participants would bring, fol-
lowed by the amount of fluid participants were able to carry (40.9%, n = 139). Participants in the high
RP group (73.7%, n = 115) were more likely to consider the planned duration of the hike than low
RP (59.8%, 110), with p= 0.002, V = 0.17). Additionally, participants in the high RP group (74.4%,
n= 116) were more likely to consider the daily ambient temperature than low RP (58.7%, n = 108),
with p= 0.002, V = 0.17. Other factors such as the amount of fluid hikers were able to carry (40.9%,
n = 139), available bottle size (24.1%, n = 82), and the water/restroom facilities on the trail (16.8%,
n = 57) were of lesser importance and did not significantly differ between RP groups (p⩾ 0.055).

4.Discussion
This interdisciplinary study combined social science with performance and hydration physiology to
investigate how RP relates to physiological strain, subjective responses, and behavioral strategies in hikers
exposed to hot and dry environmental conditions. Three hypotheses were formulated. H1 predicted that
self-reported perceived exertion, heat stress, and thirst after a hike would be higher in individuals with
high RP scores. The results partially supported this hypothesis: hikers with higher RP reported greater
heat stress and thirst, but not greater perceived exertion. H2 proposed that physiological markers, such
as elevated core body temperature, high urine concentration, and low fitness level, would be associated
with lower RP. This hypothesis was not supported, as these physiological outcomes were unrelated to RP.
H3 predicted that familiarity with heat risks and mitigation strategies, including preparation for heat and
the use of hydration strategies, would be greater in hikers with higher RP. The results did not support
the first two components of H3, as familiarity with heat warnings, the ‘Take a Hike, Do it Right’ cam-
paign, and general preparation behaviors were not significantly different across RP groups. However, the
third component of H3 was supported: hikers with high RP reported giving more weight to planned hike
duration and daily ambient temperature when planning fluid intake.
4.1.RP
RP has been described in prior work in different ways [34]. Here, the RP score (48 out of 80 points) was
mainly constructed from questions about worry [23], reflecting uncertainty about particular situations
[34]. The current study revealed that physically-fit and well-informed hikers worried about accidents
when walking on a hard trail, insufficient and inadequate peers, exertional heat illness, physical exhaus-
tion, and a lack of shade while hiking on hard trails in a hot and dry environment. Aside from the
present study, only one other investigation in China [23] examined RP among hikers; conducted in a
9

Environ.Res.:Health3(2025)045006 FCWardernaaretal
non-desert environment and, found that hikers were primarily concerned with sudden weather changes,
security issues and traffic accidents. Our study contributes to a greater understanding of RP among
hikers, especially in a desert environment.
It has been suggested that when individuals are exposed to heat, their perceptions of risk related to
health and safety may decrease, thereby making them more likely to engage in risky behaviors [16]. At
the same time, it has been reported that hikers with higher RP were more likely to have engaged in cer-
tain protective behaviors [35]. While it has been reported that hikers with high-risk more frequently
report checking the weather conditions before their hike, no difference was reported for the amount of
water carried [35]. This is in contrast with our study, as we show two-thirds of participants considered
the planned duration of the hike and the ambient temperature while planning how much fluid to bring,
and in all cases, the HRP group reported these behaviors more frequently than the LRP group. Some
hikers may worry more than others, but these differences do not necessarily lead to different practical
outcomes, for example, for the amount of fluid brought or consumed. Further, RP could be shaped by
sex or gender and socioeconomic factors, and for example Huang etal have shown that RP can be shif-
ted by an intervention [36], which is interesting as it may help to safeguard hikers at risk.
4.2.Self-reportedperceivedexertion,heatstress,thirst,andRP
The HRP group reported higher self-reported scores for heat stress and thirst. For thirst, this was spe-
cifically reflected in a higher 75th percentile score compared to the low RP group. This suggests a pos-
sible relationship between perceiving higher levels of risk and physical manifestations of heat and thirst,
which is in line with the general observation that heat exposure affects an individuals’ RP [16]. In earlier
research, low perceptions of risk were associated with a faster starting pace [37], but in the current
study, no difference was seen for the rate of perceived exertion (Borg scale) between RP groups, while
rate of perceived exertion directly positively relates to work rate and pacing [38].
4.3.Physiologicalmetrics,self-reportedoutcomes,andRP
The RP score, based on perceived dangers and worries about the extreme Phoenix heat, was not signi-
ficantly associated with the post-hike collected physiological test outcomes, such as fitness level, body
temperature estimates via thermal imaging (head shot), and hydration (i.e. urine concentration (USG),
or fluid carried and consumed).
The estimated VO max in the current study, measured through conducting a submaximal stepping
2
test in a subsample (table 3), was above average to excellent (⩾41.5 ml kg −1min−1 for 40–49 year old
males) [39]. It is important to emphasize that most of the hikers were recruited at the Piestewa Peak
trail, rated extremely difficult by the City of Phoenix. It covers a 365 m (1,200-foot) elevation gain
within 1.7 km (1.05 miles), i.e. a 21.5% grade, and provides almost no shade cover. Interestingly, the
current study showed slightly higher numerical VO max values for the low RP group vs. the high RP
2
group, suggesting that fitness level may be related to RP. Although no studies directly link VO max (a
2
measure of cardiorespiratory fitness) to RP, individuals with higher hiking frequency (potentially indicat-
ive of greater fitness levels) perceived lower risks [23]. Previous research from Phoenix also showed that
less fit individuals experienced more physiological complaints during a hiking activity in the heat than
their fitter counterparts [40].
The estimation of body temperature via infrared thermal imaging was selected as a non-invasive
measurement [33]. Its accuracy in determining skin temperature [41] and body core temperature has
been debated [32]. Infrared thermal imaging has been suggested to be of some practical relevance for
screening larger athletic groups [32, 42]. In contrast to an earlier study indicating that thermal imaging
can be used to quantify body temperature changes [43], the current study showed no correlation with
any of the other physiological parameters, and no differences in temperatures were seen between HRP
and LRP groups. In addition, the measured body temperature itself was in the range (35 ◦C–36 ◦C) of
previously reported skin temperature values in hiking conditions reported by our lab [44, 45], as such
the suitability of the infrared thermal imaging vs. more precise methods to estimate body core temperat-
ure could be questioned.
The hydration state based on USG was similar to earlier reporting in hikers [40, 46, 47]. In theory,
cognitive function is associated with hydration status, and in the case of dehydration, decrements in cog-
nitive function could affect mood state [48]. At the same time, it has been shown that mood state can
increase RP [49]. However, the current study results did not show any relation between hydration out-
comes (i.e. USG, fluid brought, and fluid consumed) and RP.
Overall, it is unclear why RP did not stronger aligned with these physiological outcomes. Each of
the measurements itself had its limitations, for example the YMCA step test is a submaximal test, which

includes several assumptions, while it was performed after a hike, whereas someone normally would per-
form this standardized test in a rested state [27].
4.4.Strengthsandlimitations
This study combined questionnaire-based responses with actual physiological measurements directly
after a hike. Most of the data was collected at a difficult trail under hot conditions. The use of an exit
questionnaire resulted in a substantially larger number of participants contributing to the physiological
measurements compared to earlier studies that included pre- and post-hiking measurements [40, 46].
Limitations included the use of questions to determine RP, that although used in previous studies and
exhibiting internal reliability have not yet been subjected to any comprehensive psychometric valida-
tion. Fitness was based on standardized submaximal fitness test that took place after the hike, which
may have impacted the accuracy of the estimated fitness level. The use of non-invasive thermal ima-
ging to assess body temperature likely resulted in a less accurate outcome than using (continuous) core
body temperature sensors. A limitation of this study is that most participants were recruited from an
‘extremely difficult’ trail, which may have biased the sample toward individuals with higher fitness levels.
The small supplemental sample recruited from less active participants performing on an easier trail in
Year 2 (n = 21) was not sufficient to fully balance this, and thus the findings should be interpreted with
some caution regarding their generalizability. Finally, only those who hiked for more than 30 min were
included in the data analysis.
5.Conclusion
The collective findings suggest that RP is linked to subjective experiences of heat stress and to selected
hydration-related behaviors, but not to physiological strain or general familiarity with heat risk. These
findings underscore the complexity of how individuals perceive and respond to environmental risks in
heat-exposed recreational settings. Our results have important practical implications:
First, the fact that high RP correlates with subjective discomfort (heat and thirst) but not with
physiological strain suggests that RP may be more strongly tied to perceptual and cognitive factors than
to actual physical danger in this context. This disconnection implies that interventions focused solely on
disseminating physiological facts about hydration and heat risk may not fully influence how people per-
ceive or prepare for heat. Instead, it is suggested to investigate in future research the impact of tailored
education campaigns that also address the psychological aspects of risk, such as anxiety about accidents
or discomfort from heat.
Second, most participants considered ambient temperature and hike duration when deciding how
much water to bring, yet this did not result in differences in hydration status. This result highlights a
gap between planning and execution and suggests that even when people are aware of risk factors and
take preparatory actions, their hydration strategies may still be insufficient or not well-aligned with
actual physiological needs. This is a critical insight for park managers and public health officials: current
messaging, such as the ‘Take a Hike, Do it Right’ campaign, may raise awareness but does not necessarily
lead to behavior changes that improve physiological safety outcomes.
Based on these conclusions, our study emphasizes the need for more dynamic, real-time interven-
tions. Therefore, future research should focus on the efficacy of the use of technologies, such as mobile
alerts, that communicate weather risks or hydration reminders based on exertion that could bridge the
gap between awareness and effective action. Similarly, it is worthwhile to investigate if making hydration
stations or shaded rest stops more visible and accessible could reduce the reliance on self-assessment,
which our study shows is imperfect.
Finally, our study contributes to the understanding of how individuals navigate environmental risks
under climate stress. As heat extremes become more frequent due to climate change, understanding the
psychological and behavioral components of heat risk becomes increasingly important, not only for
recreationists but also for outdoor workers and other vulnerable populations. Future research should
explore whether similar patterns exist in different populations (e.g. less fit hikers, tourists unfamil-
iar with desert conditions, or occupational groups), and whether real-time physiological feedback can
improve safety outcomes more effectively than traditional education campaigns.
We demonstrated that RP is an essential but imperfect guide for behavior in extreme heat.
Interventions that consider both the psychological and physiological dimensions of heat risk are likely
needed to better safeguard individuals engaging in outdoor activities in hot climates.
