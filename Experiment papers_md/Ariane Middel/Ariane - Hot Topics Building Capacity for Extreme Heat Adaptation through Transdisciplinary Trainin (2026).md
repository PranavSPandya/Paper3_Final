The Inaugural Global School on Heat Adaptation
What:  The Global School on Heat Adaptation is a weeklong workshop-style educational

opportunity focused on transdisciplinary learning and training of the next generation
of heat and health researchers and practitioners to address the growing challenges of
extreme heat. The school comprised 24 international graduate students and was guided
by the in-depth participation of 13 researchers and partners. In total, 10 countries and
15 disciplines were represented. Topics ranged from active learning sessions focused
on physiological responses at the microscale to personal-level heat exposures and
behaviors, up to neighborhood-, city-, and county-level actions. Activities hosted
throughout the week integrated elements of policy, climate variability and change,
cultural contexts, and the built environment.

1. Introduction: Context and purpose
Extreme heat is a global challenge that requires local-to-personal-level adaptation strategies
and solutions to mitigate adverse impacts on human health, well-being, and productivity (Ebi
et al. 2021). Significant gaps in knowledge and practice remain, particularly in quantifying
heat exposure, understanding the subsequent physiological and behavioral responses, and
developing nonstandard methods to study human adaptation, which limit our ability to
synthesize research and thrive in a hotter climate (Vecellio and Vanos 2024). To enhance the
practice and study of these complex topics, gaps in training and education must be addressed.
Postsecondary education is still primarily delivered in disciplinary silos, with minimal stake-
holder engagement. Given the convergent nature of heat challenges and solutions, this siloed
approach does not equip students with the skills, knowledge, and experiences necessary to
holistically understand heat as a multifaceted, complex challenge encompassing physiologi-
cal, behavioral, infrastructural, and societal elements. This fragmentation across disparate
disciplines limits the creativity of individuals and organizations, impedes innovation, and
slows the translation of concrete solutions into action for end users.

To address this challenge, our team of researchers and practitioners at the Global Center for
Heat Adaptation (https://heatadaptation.asu.edu/) created and delivered the first-ever weeklong
Global School on Heat Adaptation (https://icm-mhi.org/etudiant/global-school-on-heat-adaptation/),
hosted at the Montreal Heart Institute. Both initiatives were funded by the National Science
Foundation’s Global Centers program, in partnership with the Social Sciences and Humanities
Research Council of Canada. The school ambitiously engaged graduate students, researchers,
and stakeholders in active experiential learning and cross-disciplinary exchange to foster
the creation of more use-inspired, real-world solutions. In this meeting summary, we provide
an overview of attendees and activities, offer insights into best practices, share participant
experiences and outcomes, and outline plans for future iterations of the Global School on
Heat Adaptation.

3. Activities

approaches to ensure that novel teaching and learning methods led to deeper understanding
in a safe and fun environment. A summary of the session topics and learning objectives is

cal sensors, a heat chamber, and a thermal suit; guided tours; rich discussions; collaborative
problem-solving exercises; and gamified learning (see images in Fig. 2). Each day was orga-
nized around a central theme.

Day 3 focused on the physiology of heat exposure, building on previous days’ learnings
and unique vulnerabilities to ensuing health outcomes across diverse populations. Sessions
explored how adverse heat impacts are ultimately caused by the body’s physiological response

ties for measuring heat exposure and physiological responses in daily life. The afternoon
featured experiential learning, achieved by having small groups rotate through three sta-
tions: 1) simulating hot and humid extremes in an environmental chamber; 2) measuring
physiological responses using a thermal suit; and 3) demonstrating the HeatSuite system
to quantify environmental, behavioral, and physiological responses in real-world settings
(Ravanelli et al. 2025) (see Fig. 2).

AMERICAN METEOROLOGICAL SOCIETYDay 4 was dedicated to behavioral responses to heat, discussing many of the physiological
signals and situational factors that drive or modify decisions to seek cooling (Schlader and
Vargas 2019), and the importance of studying adaptive behaviors in field-based settings to
inform policy and public health measures (Vargas et al. 2023). Students were also introduced
to several practical approaches, tools, and resources for changing behavior and motivating
adaptation to extreme heat, drawing lessons from other extreme-weather hazards (e.g., flood-
ing, wildfires). In the afternoon, attendees had free time to work, discuss, gain feedback, and
collaborate on their various research projects.

The final day focused on the public health response to extreme heat, with specific examples
from the Montreal heat response plan and the development of the extreme heat policies of
the Australian Open and Sports Medicine Australia (Tartarini et al. 2025). Two members of
Montreal Public Health’s citizen committee participated in the session, bringing their experi-
ence and expertise to the table. Participants worked in small groups to explore the essential
contribution of advocacy in developing new approaches to community resilience in the context
of extreme heat. All participants were also given the opportunity to develop their own heat
policies for sports in a group setting.

In the afternoon, a group-based workshop was held to consolidate the week’s learning.
Small groups were formed, and each group was tasked with drafting a “front page” for a grant
funding proposal, with the requirement that the proposal focuses on solutions, integrates
across multiple disciplines, and involves stakeholders and/or partners. Once each team had
drafted its front page, a representative from each team presented it to the larger group, and
discussions ensued.

4. Evaluation and outcomes
At the conclusion of the weeklong event, the University Office of Evaluation and Educational
Effectiveness (UOEEE) (https://uoeee.asu.edu/) at Arizona State University administered an
anonymous survey to all student participants. The surveys prompted participants to reflect
upon their experience and provide feedback. Twenty three of 26 students (88%) completed
the survey. Below, we present information from the survey to help us understand students’
experiences as reflected in their feedback. This information supports the next iteration of
the Global School on Heat Adaptation.

According to the UOEEE report, student survey results indicated satisfaction with the
Global School on Heat Adaptation Program in Montreal. The student participants felt that the
event positively impacted them by deepening their awareness of societal challenges posed
by extreme heat and by improving their knowledge of cutting-edge research on this topic.
The most impactful aspects of the event included exposure to diverse perspectives and col-
laboration and practical and applied learning, as well as the program design and learning.
Students frequently highlighted their positive experiences of learning from diverse global
contexts and often disparate disciplines:

The opportunity to gain diverse perspectives from colleagues and professionals on the complex
challenges of heat from the climate side to human physiology, technological applications, social
engagement and policy. The practical sessions were very relevant and informative. I also made
connections with other students from across from diverse fields related to solving the issues of
heatwaves globally.

Furthermore, 91% strongly agreed or agreed that the event deepened their awareness of
everyday societal challenges posed by extreme heat and enhanced their understanding of
cutting-edge research endeavors on extreme heat.

In summary, the inaugural Global School on Heat Adaptation brought together a diverse
group of graduate students, researchers, practitioners, and stakeholders from across the
world to engage in collaborative learning of the complex and multifaceted challenges posed
by extreme heat. Through active, multiscale learning—from physiological responses to
community-level actions—the program connected science, policy, climate change, culture,
and the built environment to advance holistic approaches to extreme heat adaptation.
Designed to train the next generation of heat and health researchers and practitioners,
this initiative provides a concrete example of breaking down silos to integrate the multiple
disciplines, sectors, and perspectives needed to identify and mobilize effective strategies
to minimize the wide-ranging impacts of extreme heat on human health, well-being, and
performance.
