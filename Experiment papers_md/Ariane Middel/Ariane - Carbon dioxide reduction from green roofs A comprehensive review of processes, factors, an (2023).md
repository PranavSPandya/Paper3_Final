47 1. Introduction
48 Rapid urban expansion has increased energy demand and greenhouse gas (GHG)
49 emissions, significantly contributing to climate change. By 2050, 8.5-9.9 billion people will
50 live in cities, accounting for 55-78% of the world's population [1, 2]. Buildings are the largest
51 consumers of energy, releasing about 40% of the world's total GHG emissions [3]. Therefore,
52 building design that reduces CO emissions and improves energy efficiency is an important
2
53 direction in the building sector to meet low-carbon goals in the face of climate change.
54 Various rooftop technologies have been proposed as potential climate mitigation and
55 adaptation solutions because roofs generally account for about 20-25% of the total urban
56 surface area [4]. Green roofs (GRs), or vegetated roofs, have been considered an effective
57 Nature-based Solution and have been adopted worldwide to improve urban sustainability and
58 resilience [5]. The significant reductions in energy consumption, CO emissions, and waste
2
59 production associated with GRs render them a cleaner environmental option. The scientific
60 literature has extensively verified that GRs sequester carbon (CO ) and improve building
2
61 energy performance – both of which contribute to reducing carbon dioxide and mitigating
62 climate change [6-8].
63 Carbon dioxide can be reduced directly and indirectly through GRs. Ambient CO is
2
64 directly absorbed by vegetation from the atmosphere and stored in biomass and substrate
65 (direct reduction), while thermal regulation reduces building energy consumption (indirect
66 reduction). However, the reduction potential of GRs to mitigate climate change is often
67 poorly quantified, and the mitigation potential is underestimated because many interacting
68 processes and mechanisms go into the quantification.
69 Previous studies have mainly focused on field experiments, simulation tools, or both to
4

70 analyze GR processes [9]. Controlled and/or long-term field experiments quantify GR
71 performance under different meteorological conditions and/or by altering vegetation and
72 substrate properties [10]. In field experiments, net ecosystemprimary productivity (NEP),
73 meteorological parameters, conductive heat fluxes, and heat load for cooling/heating can be
74 measured directly. In contrast, GR simulations can evaluate carbon-related and thermal
75 properties of GRs at different temporal and spatial scales and for various "what-if" scenarios
76 [11, 12]. Several recent studies have summarized and reviewed the environmental benefits
77 and ecosystem services of GRs [9, 13]. Various key mechanisms (e.g., carbon sequestration,
78 heat and mass transfer, and energy consumption reduction) of GRs are associated with carbon
79 dioxide reduction. Although reviews on these key mechanisms have been published
80 individually [6, 8, 14, 15], they have not been synthesized comprehensively to quantify the
81 climate change mitigation potential of GRs.
82 This study aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the related processes, factors,
83 and methods to quantify the carbon dioxide reduction of GRs. The present work advances
84 this goal by analyzing direct and indirect carbon-reduction processes (Section 2) and
85 identifying and summarizing the factors and properties of GRs that influence the related
86 physical and biophysical processes (Section 3). Finally, methods to quantify the processes
87 and evaluate the carbon-reduction benefits are presented (Section 4). By analyzing the
88 processes by which GRs sequester carbon and mitigate thermal stress, and by analyzing the
89 main factors and summarizing the methods how previous studies assessed the
90 decarbonization of GRs, this study will advance research on how to design and build GRs as
91 climate mitigation and adaption solution in urban areas.

197 2.3.2 Atmosphere-GRs heat interaction
198 Interactions of GRs with the atmosphere occur mainly through radiative heat fluxes,

288 3.1 Vegetation
289 Vegetation is the top layer and the most essential component of GRs. Vegetation
290 interacts with the atmosphere through shading, photosynthesis, respiration, and transpiration.
291 As shown in Fig. 5, vegetation parameters that significantly influence GR performance are
292 structural properties (e.g., leaf area index, plant height, coverage), spectral properties
17

293 (albedo), and stomatal resistance [55, 56].
294 3.1.1 Structural properties (LAI/ Coverage)
295 The Leaf area index (LAI) refers to the projected area of leaves over a unit of land and
296 ranges between 0.5~5.0 [56]. LAI is the most relevant vegetation parameter affecting the
297 carbon dioxide reduction of GRs [55] because it is directly related to photosynthesis, shading,
298 evapotranspiration, and convective heat transfer. In addition, the ambient CO absorption rate
2
299 increases with LAI (Table 2 Eq. 2-5). The canopy growth rate also impacts carbon dioxide
300 reduction because the canopy intercepts incoming radiation [57]. Less solar radiation reaches
301 the roof surface as LAI increases (Table 2 -Eq. 3). Yaghoobian and Srebric [58] found that
302 the daily radiative flux reaching an unplanted control surface was 32% (6.2 kWhm-2) higher
303 than that of a shaded surface from the dense canopy. They estimated that the annual building
304 cooling load decreased by 13 kWhm-2, while the heating load increased by 0.88 kWhm-2 due
305 to the GR. Another experiment showed that an increase in LAI from 2 to 5 reduces the heat
306 transmitted to the soil by 250W m-2 [59]. Lundholm, Tran [60] reported that the water loss
307 rates through ET of surfaces covered with Sedum spurium were ~16% lower than that of bare
308 soil. Similar results were found for other species (Danthonia spicate, Empetrum nigrum, and
309 Vaccinium macrocarpon). Lower cooling and higher heating load were observed with higher
310 coverage.
311 3.1.2 Spectral properties (Albedo)
312 Spectral reflectivity (albedo) is the ratio of reflected radiation to incoming solar
313 radiation. The plant albedo plays an important role in net radiation. Experiments confirmed
314 that a semi-GR (0.16) had higher albedo than an extensive GR (0.12) [43]. Zhao, Tabares-
315 Velasco [61] simulated two types of plant-substrate assemblies with high and low reflectivity.
18

316 The net radiation fluxes differed by 20%, and peak net radiation fluxes differed by 16%. In
317 addition, the selection of different plant species also leads to different albedo. Lundholm,
318 Tran [60] reported a wide albedo range (highest value of ~0.26 for Sedum acre, and lowest
319 value of ~0.16 for Rhodiola rasea), and species in the forbs and grasses group varied slightly
320 between 0.16 ~0.19.
321 3.1.3 Stomatal resistance
322 Stomatal resistance reflects the ability of plants to transpire; higher stomatal resistance
323 leads to lower transpiration rates (Table 3 -Eq. 11). The stomata on the leaves are also used
324 to uptake CO from the atmosphere. The stomatal closure depends on the water content in
2
325 the stomata cavity's interior and the leaf's exterior surface [31]. Plant species with higher
326 stomatal resistance, such as Sedum, are normally selected on extensive or semi-extensive
327 roofs because of their ability to tolerate dry conditions [42]. However, succulent plants (e.g.,
328 Sedum) produce lower ET fluxes than grass plants due to high stomatal resistance, resulting
329 in less cooling [29].

386 3.3 Building environment surrounding of GRs
387 The thermal performance of GRs is impacted by the surrounding building environment,
388 including building patterns and micro-climate.
389 3.3.1 Building and urban geometry
390 Previous studies found that building characteristics (e.g., insulation layer thickness) and
391 urban configuration (e.g., urban canyon geometry, height-to-width ratio, building density)
392 impact the thermal performance of GRs [76, 77].
393 The insulation of the building envelope varies because of physical components such as
394 the roof, walls, doors, windows, etc. As shown in Fig. 5, the building envelope affects the
395 thermal performance of GRs due to heat transfer by the slab and/or insulation layer. He, Yu
396 [78] conducted a sensitivity analysis showing that the insulation effect of GRs was mainly
397 affected by the thermal resistances of the substrate and structure layer. Many studies have
398 found that GRs are more efficient in low-insulation buildings [79].
399 Urban form, i.e., the height, density, and direction of buildings, impacts the thermal
400 performance of GRs by creating distinct microclimates [77]. Sun, Grimmond [80] found the
401 GR cooling benefit to increase linearly with size. Zonato, Martilli [76] showed that tall
402 buildings decrease the cooling effect at ground level and concluded that the impact on 2m air
403 temperature was not linear to building height.

520 The cooling benefits of GRs have been investigated at the neighborhood scale and
521 mesoscale by energy balance models (EBM) and/or computational fluid dynamics models
522 (CFD) [110]. ENVI-met is one of the most widely used CFD models [111] and has been
523 applied to calculate the thermal performance of GRs and energy savings coupled with
524 EnergyPlus [112]. Sun, Grimmond [80] modelled the heat mitigation impact of GRs in the
525 Beijing metropolitan area during heat waves with WRF-PUCM. Zonato, Martilli [76]
526 investigated the building energy consumption in summer and winter in idealized urban areas
527 using WRF. However, these methods have large uncertainties because of model
528 generalizations of processes and factors related to the carbon dioxide reduction of GRs.
529 5. Outlook and future research needs
530 The processes, factors, and quantitative methods of recent and important literature were
531 comprehensively reviewed to analyze and evaluate the carbon dioxide reduction performance
532 of GRs in Table 6. Previous studies have shown that GRs are potential carbon sinks,
533 effectively mitigate the urban heat, and improve indoor conditions and energy efficiency of
534 buildings. A gap in understanding the carbon cycling of GR ecosystems was identified. The
535 evaluation of carbon dioxide reductions of GRs is still incomplete and un-robust; thus it
536 requires interdisciplinary approaches. Future scientific research on the carbon dioxide
537 benefits of GRs should focus on three objectives:
538 (1) A better understanding of GR carbon reduction mechanisms is required to quantify
539 the carbon sequestration benefits of GRs. More studies in various climatic zones are needed
28

540 to investigate the interactions among GR elements (i.e., plants, substrate, microbiome) with
541 the environment and their dynamics (e.g., vegetation growth, water, heat transfer).
542 (2) GR systems customized for specific climate conditions will yield the greatest dioxide
543 reductions. The benefits of GRs vary by geographic region and climate. Determining the right
544 vegetation for local climate conditions is paramount to guaranteeing sustainable GRs. The
545 substrate characteristics should also be studied more in-depth to select the most efficient
546 substrate carefully.
547 (3) Research at multiple spatial and temporal scales is fundamental to understanding GR
548 ecosystems and evaluating their decarbonization impacts comprehensively. The synergistic
549 effects of GRs on carbon dioxide reductions from the ground level to the city level and from
550 seasonal to annual time scales have not been well studied, and cost-benefit analyses are
551 important to promote and implement GRs. A multi-objective evaluation of the benefits and
552 tradeoffs of GRs is needed to support stakeholder decisions better [113]. For example,
553 irrigation increases evaporative cooling at the cost of more water consumption [68].
554 Therefore, a precise long-term evaluation and/or prediction of carbon reduction of GRs for
555 multiple spatial and temporal scales is needed in future studies.
