17 Environmental, neuropsychological, and physiological factors in urban outdoor thermal
18 comfort assessments: A systematic review
19 Abstract
20 Excessive heat and thermal discomfort are growing threats for urban dwellers, severely affecting health and well-
21 being. The complex interplay of urban structures, microclimates, and the diverse psychophysiological responses of
22 individuals contribute to the complexity of Outdoor Thermal Comfort (OTC) studies. Prior literature has proposed
23 various environmental and psychophysiological (i.e., human-centered) OTC assessment models. However,
24 observational studies are necessary to understand OTC and to refine and validate these models. Yet, no guidelines
25 exist for OTC observational study planning, making it challenging to collect environmental and psychophysiological
26 OTC data following standardized practices. This paper presents a systematic review that summarizes the factors and
27 parameters found in the participant-involved OTC observational studies described in 217 papers. Their geographical
28 context, the environmental parameters studied, the meteorological instruments used, the biometric sensors,
29 neuropsychological assessments, and thermal, environmental, and sensory surveys used, as well as the observational
30 study planning strategies implemented in these papers were evaluated. Most papers used thermal surveys, while only
31 2% used sensory surveys. This review found that studies in continental and arid climates remain limited. Additionally,
32 most studies targeted 18 to 60-year-old participants, while only 1.3% (3 papers) and 2.7% (6 papers) focused on
33 children and the elderly. Only 5% of the observational studies used standardized neuropsychological assessments, all
34 conducted in the last 10 years. Furthermore, physiological sensing to access cardiovascular, neurological, and
35 dermatological functioning was only deployed in 18% of the studies. We outline knowledge gaps, identify research
36 opportunities, and suggest potential frameworks for future observational study planning.

47 1 Introduction
48 With the combined effects of global warming and increasing urbanization, urban heat has become a public health
49 concern. Each year, more than five million people worldwide die from heat (Lu et al., 2021). Heat stress is a condition
50 that occurs when the body is unable to regulate its temperature properly (Campbell et al., 2022). Heat-related illnesses,
51 including heat exhaustion, heat cramps, and heat stroke, can result from heat stress (Rock et al., 2019; Roghanchi et
52 al., 2015). For example, a heatwave in Paris in 2019 caused 1500 excess deaths, with many more inhabitants suffering
53 from severe health impacts that lasted long after the heatwave (Meerow et al., 2021; Moini et al., 2020; Rock et al.,
54 2019). Many municipalities now develop heat mitigation plans, advocating for green and blue infrastructures to
55 provide shade, enhance evapotranspiration, and reduce the Urban Heat Island (UHI) (Görgen et al., 2021; Zhao et al.,
56 2015) or surface painting to increase albedo and reduce the absorption of solar radiation by urban materials,
57 consequently lowering heat emissivity (Andrés et al., 2021; Kalkstein et al., 2022; Schneider et al., 2023), amongst
58 others.
59 The precursor of urban heat stress is thermal discomfort, which can be life-threatening when experienced by vulnerable
60 groups such as the elderly, infants, and people with preexisting diseases such as diabetes, high blood pressure, obesity,
61 and weight imbalances (Hsu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021). American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-
62 Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE-55) defines thermal comfort as “a psychological state that expresses satisfaction
63 with the thermal environment and is assessed through subjective evaluations” (Ashrae, 1992). Given the potential
64 health threats that exposure to excessive heat poses, particularly for sensitive populations, providing thermally
65 comfortable outdoor environments is crucial to promote public health, especially in dense urban areas where most of
66 the global population live and where peak temperatures are more frequently observed (Haluza et al., 2014).
67 1.1 Factors Influencing Outdoor Thermal Comfort (OTC)
68 It is well known that exposure to micrometeorological stressors (e.g., direct solar radiation, high air temperature, and
69 humidity) can lead to thermophysiological stress. However, meteorological factors only explain about 50% of an
70 individual’s OTC (Middel et al., 2016).
3

71 OTC is also driven by psychophysiological factors (where physiological body sensations are psychologically
72 perceived and interpreted) (Klemm et al., 2015; Kobas et al., 2021), also called human-centered assessments (Raj et
73 al., 2011). When the temperature of the environment becomes greater than that of the skin, the body gains heat by
74 both conduction and radiation (Yousef et al., 2022). Body temperature is then regulated and cooled by sweating and
75 increased respiration (Lipczynska et al., 2020). Previous literature stated that individuals of different genders have
76 temperature preferences that vary by up to 3°C (Darlenski et al., 2022; Bar et al., 1969; Wang et al., 2022; Bastardot
77 et al., 2019). Furthermore, underlying medical conditions such as mental illnesses, anxiety, or stress can also play a
78 significant role in how individuals perceive thermal environments (Marques et al., 2021; Picton et al., 2020). Unusual
79 sweating can be observed in people with mental health conditions, such as depression or anxiety, due to the release of
80 adrenaline during episodes of increased anxiety (Bahar et al., 2016). This mental state triggers sweating, causing
81 hyperhidrosis and resulting in the body's ineffectiveness to self-thermoregulate, making the individual prone to
82 thermal discomfort (Wohlrab et al., 2023).
83 Multisensory interactions can also influence OTC. For example, excessive noise levels can affect psychological well-
84 being and moderate OTC perception (Chen et al., 2022), whereby stress, annoyance, and distraction caused by noise,
85 combined with heat, intensify the perception of discomfort (Sepehri et al., 2019). Loud noises can also stimulate the
86 body's stress response (Bourikas et al., 2021), triggering physiological changes such as increased heart rate, blood
87 pressure, and perspiration (Guan et al., 2020), which, in turn, affects thermal comfort perception and exacerbates the
88 feeling of heat discomfort (Jin et al., 2020). Visual stimuli, such as a view from a window, also impact thermal
89 perception (Ko et al., 2020). For example, a visually appealing view, such as a natural or green landscape, can
90 positively influence thermal perception by creating a sense of psychological comfort, facilitating a perception that the
91 environment is cooler and more comfortable (Chinazzo et al., 2021), even if the actual temperature remains the same.
92 On the other hand, a visually unattractive or obstructed view leads to a perception of increased heat or discomfort
93 (Lam et al., 2020). This effect has been explained by the visual stimuli and perceived expectation of control over
94 visual inputs. Both cognitive distractions influence how individuals interpret and experience thermal conditions
95 (Salamone et al., 2020).
96 Perceived control, which refers to an individual's belief in their ability to control their environmental conditions, also
97 significantly influences OTC (Xu et al., 2021). For example, controlling clothing and moving to shaded outdoor spaces
98 can impact comfort levels, potentially leading to higher comfort even in hot conditions (Zhou et al., 2023).

106 1.2 Challenges and Advances in Human-Centered Outdoor Thermal Comfort Research
107 Data to perform OTC assessments can be collected through modeling and simulation approaches (synthetic data) or
108 observational studies (observational study data) to inform thermal comfort indices (Li et al., 2022; Matzarakis et al.,
109 2016; Pantavou et al., 2020). Various literature reviews have focused on the description of these indices and have
110 highlighted the challenges associated with incorporating subjective assessments to assess OTC (Dzyuban et al., 2022a;
111 Lai et al., 2020; Nicol et al., 2017). Prior literature has repeatedly underlined the need for observational studies to
112 collect environmental and psychophysiological data to better inform and validate these models and thermal indices
113 (Coccolo et al., 2018; Middel et al., 2016; Dzyuban et al., 2022a; Rupp et al., 2015). Recent review papers have also
114 underlined the need for a framework to select the datasets to be captured and the observational study planning
115 protocols to be implemented to perform human-centered observational studies to assess OTC (Cheung et al., 2017;
116 Elnabawi et al., 2020; Lai et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021, 2020; Nikolopoulou et al., 2001; Shooshtarian et al., 2018).
117 With this motivation, this systematic review focuses on identifying the factors and parameters used for their thermal
118 comfort assessments in prior human-centered OTC observational studies. However, this review is not an "input-data-
119 focused systematic literature review, but it analyzes the factors/parameters used into thermal comfort assessments and
120 indices.
121 To the best of the authors' knowledge, the last literature review examining OTC observational studies was conducted
122 by Johansson et al. (2014). This review described the instruments and methods used to perform 26 observational
123 studies and concluded that a standardized framework was necessary to perform OTC observational studies. In the last
124 decade, with increased recognition of the need for psychophysiological OTC assessments and advances in
125 physiological sensing technologies, several studies on human-centered OTC observational study assessments have
126 been conducted (Berto et al., 2014; Kumar et al., 2020; Mansi et al., 2021; Persiani et al., 2021; Piselli et al., 2018).
5

127 Reviewing this literature is necessary to evaluate and refine data collection and observational study practices for
128 human-centered OTC assessments, hence the focus of this systematic review.
129 2 Materials and Methods for Systematic Review
130 A literature search was developed through the Abstract and Citation database Scopus, Web of Science (WoS), and
131 Pubmed. Furthermore, Google Scholar was used to identify relevant grey literature of interest and articles referenced
132 in the reviewed articles under the framework of this work. Version 2020 of the Preferred Reporting Items for
133 Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Page et al., 2021a) was adopted for a multi-step literature search
134 and review. The first step involved the identification of relevant keywords and search queries (see Section 2.1). The
135 second step was the definition of the exclusion criteria (Section 2.2), and the third step was to perform the systematic
136 review, which resulted in 217 papers for discussion in Table B.6. This review focused on identifying the factors and
137 parameters used for their thermal comfort assessments. Another publication focusing on thermal comfort indices will
138 be prepared in the future.
139 2.1 Search Strategy & Selection Criteria

141 focused on OTC observational studies, including papers focused on environmental measures, neuropsychological
142 assessments, and physiological measures. The common keywords ("Outdoor" AND "Therm*" AND "Comfor*") were
143 combined with environmental and monitoring-related terms common to both methods: ("Environmen*" OR
144 "Weather*" OR "Climat*" OR "Microclimat*" OR "Micrometeorolog*" OR "Biometeorolog*" OR "Temperat*" OR

221 3.2 Environmental Parameters
222 Column 4 of Fig. 3 represents the environmental parameters measured. Weather and climate conditions determine
223 thermal comfort, and the importance of environmental parameters varies depending on the climatic zone (Antypov et
224 al., 2022; Chea et al., 2021; Kamchen et al., 2018). For example, in hot and arid climates, solar radiation can cause
225 excessive sweating, dehydration, and increased risk of heat exhaustion or heat stroke, making it a crucial OTC
226 parameter to capture (Gachkar et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2021; Shawesh et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2022).
227 Air temperature, which directly affects individual heat balance and thermal perception, was measured in 162 of 217
228 papers using sensors on site. Relative humidity and wind speed & direction, which also affect thermal comfort through
229 heat transfer between the body and the environment, were measured on-site in 158 and 161 papers, respectively, out
230 of the 217 papers.
231 Prior literature highlights the importance of capturing radiant heat exchanges between the human body and
232 surrounding surfaces. This data collection involves documenting the Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT), the weighted
233 average temperature of all the surfaces surrounding a person, influencing radiant heat exchange (Guo et al., 2020).
234 Measured on site in 66 of the 217 papers, MRT is influenced by the solar radiation absorbed by impervious surfaces
9

235 such as pavements or building facades (Fröhlich and Matzarakis, 2018; Wang and Akbari, 2014), provides insights
236 into the heating and cooling dynamics associated with surfaces around pedestrians in urban settings (Li et al., 2022).
237 For example, Dzyuban et al. (2022b) used the mobile mean radiant temperature cart MaRTy for detailed
238 micrometeorological observations, including air temperature measurements, relative humidity, and radiation flux
239 densities at the pedestrian level to acquire MRT.
240 The Sky View Factor (SVF) quantifies the amount of sky on a hemisphere visible from the ground (Bernard et al.,
241 2018). Low SVF indicates urban areas with dense construction where the sky is less visible due to obstructions like
242 buildings and trees. This reduced sky visibility can lead to several effects: it diminishes direct sunlight reaching the
243 ground, decreases solar heat gain during the day, decreases MRT, and limits wind movement. Low SVFs decrease
244 radiative cooling at night as the heat retained by buildings is not efficiently emitted back, resulting in increased MRT
245 over time (Inavonna et al., 2018). For example, in arid and desert climates, clear sky and intense sun contribute to
246 significant daytime heating, for which low SVF accounts for shading and reduced MRT (Hartabela et al., 2021; Middel
247 et al., 2021; Paramita et al., 2014). Hence, the importance of documenting the SVF is widely accepted in literature,
248 and it was measured on-site in 34 of the 217 papers studied. Surface temperature and luminance were measured on-
249 site in 14 & 5 papers.
250 Vapor pressure and precipitation were collected from a weather station in nine, and nine of the 217 papers
251 (respectively), both affecting the thermal environment and individuals' thermal perception. The sound level was
252 collected on-site in 5 papers. For example, a paper reported that noise levels influence thermal comfort perception in
253 warm environments (Cureau et al., 2022).
254 Table B.1 presents these environmental parameters and the number of studies that collected them. Various instruments
255 were used to capture these meteorological observations, which have been listed in Table B.2, where each provides
256 information about the measured environmental parameters, the instrument type, the instrument's least count (or

267 3.3 Thermal and Sensory Surveys
268 Column 5 of Fig. 3 represents the thermal surveys assessed in the collected papers. Thermal surveys aim to evaluate
269 how temperature, humidity, wind, and solar radiation affect the human experience of comfort (Adrian et al., 2008;

272 Vote (TSV), Thermal Comfort Vote (TCV), Mean Thermal Sensation Votes (MTSV), and Actual Thermal Sensation

274 used in 23 papers. Thermal Preference Vote (TPV) and Momentary Thermal Preference (MTP) were used in 19 papers.
275 Thermal surveys were utilized in 24 papers (11%) to assess environmental factors, namely humidity, wind, and
276 sunlight, that affect human comfort perception. (Jin et al., 2012; Vokac et al., 1976). The Humidity Sensation Vote
277 (HSV), Wind Sensation Vote (WSV), and Sun Sensation Vote (SSV) were used in 17 papers, and the Wind Speed
278 Preference Vote (WSPV), and Sunlight Preference Vote (SPV) were used in 7 papers. The thermal surveys used in
279 the gathered literature have been listed in Table B.3.

297 3.4 Neuropsychological assessments
298 Column 6 of Fig. 3 represents the standardized subjective and objective neuropsychological assessments used in the
299 reviewed papers. Of the 217 papers selected, ten only used subjective neuropsychological assessments, and one used
300 objective neuropsychological assessments.
301 Subjective neuropsychological assessments rely on individuals' self-reported perceptions and experiences to assess
302 their mental states and behaviors (Das et al., 2020; Reyes et al., 2019). Subjective and objective neuropsychological
303 assessments are discussed further in this section.
304 Four papers used the Profile of Mood States (POMS) questionnaire. POMS assesses mood states such as tension,
305 depression, anger, vigor, fatigue, and confusion influenced by thermal conditions (McNair et al., 1971). Thermal
306 discomfort influences an individual’s mood and state of mind (Kim et al., 2023; Pearlmutter et al., 2014). Mood states
307 are amongst the first biological responders to stress, including thermal distress, which is why the POMS score is
308 important for assessing OTC (Turhan et al., 2023). For example, lower POMS tension-anxiety scores were reported
309 when walking on streets covered with trees (Elsadek et al., 2019c; Yoshida et al., 2015a), concluding that the presence
310 of nature reduced tension-anxiety, improved mood, and increased the perception of comfort even though physically
311 the OTC remained the same (Elsadek et al., 2019c; Lin et al., 2019).
312 Four papers used the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) questionnaire. PANAS measures positive and
313 negative emotional states of mind that can influence the perception of OTC (Watson et al., 1988). For example, a
314 study using the PANAS questionnaire found that in summer, higher temperatures decreased Positive Affect (PA)
315 scores, indicating a decline in emotional well-being due to increased thermal discomfort (Zhang et al., 2021). Another
316 study using the PANAS questionnaire showed a greater influence of green spaces on positive emotions irrespective
317 of thermal discomfort due to summer (Niu et al., 2023).
12

318 Two papers used the Perceived Restorativeness Scale (PRS) questionnaire. PRS assesses the perceived restorative
319 quality (i.e., the ability to replenish cognitive and emotional resources (Pasanen et al., 2018; Scopelliti et al., 2019))
320 of the outdoor environment (Hartig et al., 1997). Huang et al. (2021) and Nakata et al. (2021) confirmed that restoration
321 from thermal stress was higher in environments with plants and water bodies.
322 Three papers used the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-S) questionnaire. STAI-S measures state (temporary) and
323 trait (general) anxiety, which could be inherited due to thermal discomfort (Spielberger et al., 1983). Thermal stress
324 has been reported to induce anxiety, which is evidenced by an increase in STAI-S scores, indicating higher perceived
325 anxiety levels (Mauriz et al., 2020). Elsadek et al. (2019c) showed that STAI-S questionnaire scores measuring current
326 anxiety were significantly lower after walking along roads with higher tree coverage, irrespective of thermal
327 conditions. They found an increase in anxiety levels when walking on the control road without trees.
328 One paper used the Subjective Vitality Scale (SVS) questionnaire. SVS measures the feeling of energy and vitality,
329 which is also influenced by OTC (Ryan and Frederick, 1997). Previous research has shown that physiological stress
330 due to changes in thermal condition affects the subjective vitality of participants, which was assessed using the SVS
331 questionnaire (Frederick et al., 2023; Goldbeck et al., 2019). E.g., Elsadek et al. (2019c) also used the SVS
332 questionnaire and found an increase in the subjective vitality of participants when walking along roads with green
333 spaces and a lower PET, compared to roads with no green spaces and a higher PET.
334 Two papers used the Semantic Differential (SD) questionnaire. SD evaluates the connotative meaning of objects,
335 events, and concepts present in an outdoor environment, which has been shown to influence the perception of OTC
336 (Osgood et al., 1955). Yoshida et al. (2015) found that participants reported a greater sense of relaxation and reduction
337 of thermal stress when viewing nature and sitting under trees, as indicated by a higher positive rating on the SD scale.
338 One paper used the Emotional Valence (EV) questionnaire. EV measures the positive or negative value associated
339 with a stimulus in an outdoor environment (Anderson et al., 2014). Xi et al. (2023) noticed an agreement between EV
340 and TCV surveys, stating that a good mood (measured using EV) improved the perception of thermal comfort
341 (measured using TCV).
342 One paper used the Restorative Outcome Scale (ROS) questionnaire. ROS measures the actual restorative outcome
343 experienced by the participants in the outdoor environment (Yusli et al., 2021). Elsadek et al. (2019c) reported that
344 under similar PET levels and in the small shading effect of certain trees, participants still experienced psychological
345 restoration, which improved OTC.
13

346 The collected literature using subjective neuropsychological assessments showed that the thermal conditions affected
347 the participant’s assessment scores. However, these assessments agreed that green spaces with trees improved the
348 subjective state of participants.
349 On the other hand, objective neuropsychological assessments rely on cognitive tasks to assess mental states and
350 behaviors (Grassini and Laumann, 2020; Loevinger, 1957). The selected literature includes objective
351 neuropsychological assessments; the Digit Span Backwards (DSB) test, Stroop test, Mind Map Test (MMT), and the
352 Typing Test (TT). DSB measures working memory capacity (Wechsler et al. 1939), Stroop Test assesses cognitive
353 control and attentional capacity (Stroop, 1935), MMT measures cognitive mapping and organizational skills (Buzan,
354 1993), and TT evaluates cognitive attention while measuring typing speed and accuracy (Dvorak et al., 1936). Chang
355 et al. (2023) used all four of these objective neuropsychological assessments and concluded that the thermal
356 environment consistently impacted cognitive performance, memory capacity, creativity & processing speed. At the
357 same time, observing nature improved all these cognitive resources even though thermal conditions remained the
358 same.
359 The standardized neuropsychological assessments used in the gathered papers, along with their questionnaire reference
360 of publication, the type of questionnaire, and the papers where they were used, have been included in Table 1.
361 3.5 Physiological Sensing
362 Column 7 of Fig. 3 represents the physiological sensing used in reviewed papers.
363 Physiological sensing was performed in 18% (40 out of 217) papers. Physiological sensing refers to measuring various
364 biometric parameters, such as skin temperature, blood pressure, and heart rate, which can provide insights into how
365 the body responds to OTC changes (Khan et al., 2021; Morresi et al., 2021).
366 For example, as temperature increases, the body tries to regulate internal temperature by increasing blood flow to the
367 skin, which can increase Skin Temperature (ST) (Inavonna et al., 2018).
368 Infrared (no-contact) and thermocouple (on-contact) thermometers were used to measure ST in 33 papers in the
369 collected literature. For example, in an observational study by Vanos et al. (2010), a wrist-worn thermocouple
370 measured the ST of 12 participants while cycling. The study concluded that different activities that involve physical
371 exercise change the heat emitted, causing changes in the comfort experienced by participants.
14

372 Skin Conductance (SC) measures the skin's electrical conductance, which varies with changes in sweat gland activity
373 and is used in 7 out of 217 papers. Measuring SC can help understand emotional arousal in participants, which can be
374 related to how (comfortable or anxious) they feel in a given environment (Farnsworth et al., 2019).
375 Electrodermal Activity (EDA), used in 4 out of 217 papers, measures the variation of the electrical properties of the
376 skin in response to sweat secretion (Boucsein et al., 2012).
377 Photoplethysmography (PPG), used in 5 of 217 papers, is a non-invasive optical technique used to detect volumetric
378 changes in blood circulation within the microvascular bed of tissue, which will help in signaling when thermal
379 discomfort leads to an increase in the rate of blood flow volume of skin (Kamshilin et al., 2011).
380 Changes in Blood Pressure (BP) and Heart Rate Variability (HRV) were used in 4 and 18 of 217 papers, respectively.
381 HRV can also occur in response to changes in thermal comfort and their neuropsychological state. Therefore,
382 monitoring can help identify the stress or discomfort experienced by individuals in different thermal environments
383 and provides valuable information for designing urban spaces that promote comfort and well-being (Rahma et al.,
384 2022).
385 Electroencephalogram (EEG), used in 6 of 217 papers, is a neurophysiological measurement that records electrical
386 activity from the brain to provide insights into cognitive processes and brain functioning (Light et al., 2010). The
387 details of all physiological parameters captured in the reviewed literature are presented in Table 2. The complete list
388 of instruments is listed in Table B.5. A qualitative illustration of the human-centered sensors is also included in Fig.
389 1. Fig. A.1 in the appendix shows a compilation of all the environmental parameters, thermal and sensory surveys,
390 neuropsychological assessments, and physiological measures collected in the selected papers.
391 3.6 Observational Study Planning
392 Columns 8 and 9 of Fig. 3 represent the participant count and type of study done in the reviewed papers. Of the 217
393 papers, 208 were cross-sectional studies, and 9 were longitudinal studies.
394 Cross-sectional studies generally focused on a large cohort of participants, taking 5 to 15 minutes per participant
395 within the observational study (Portnova et al., 2020; Pye et al., 2016). For example, a study by Lau et al. (2021),
396 gathered 1842 random participants who were provided with thermal and sensory surveys. They reported that
397 dissatisfaction with the environmental aesthetics increased the perception of thermal discomfort. Lehnert et al. (2021b)
398 worked with 1522 random participants to identify thermal discomfort hotspots in urban areas. They found that the
399 resident-identified hotspots differed from those based on surface temperature measurements, indicating a need for
15

400 further investigation. Similarly, Tian et al. (2022), in a study with 1772 random participants, concluded that thermal
401 perceptions in their study area were influenced by physical, individual, social, and psychological factors, and further
402 studies assessing these factors are needed. In these studies, a large number of participants were necessary to ensure a
403 fair distribution of respondents for data collection and statistically coherent results to account for variations in thermal
404 conditions within their study areas (Lehnert et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2022; Lau et al., 2021).
405 Conversely, longitudinal studies generally focused on a specific population cluster to document their OTC preferences
406 over time (Jin et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019). Zheng et al. (2023) conducted a longitudinal study with two assessments
407 on 44 elderly individuals aged 60 and above from July to August 2019 and 2021. They concluded that basal metabolic
408 rate, cardiac output, and sensitivity to environmental factors decreased with age. On the other hand, Vanos et al. (2010)
409 concentrated on a younger demographic, recruiting 12 participants aged 19 to 23 for a 30-minute observational study
410 involving exercise (running and cycling) in four different periods. They concluded that core temperature is crucial in
411 human thermoregulation, significantly influencing sweat rate and skin temperature. Longitudinal studies have also
412 been performed while focusing only on one gender, such as Li et al. (2022), who recruited 10 male participants aged
413 22 to 30 years for their study. They conducted observational studies annually from 2016 to 2017 and concluded that
414 the participants had an increased pulse rate in hot climates (32°C), while in cold climates (16°C), they exhibited lower
415 blood pressure.

419 4 Discussion
420 4.1 Environmental Urban OTC Assessment
421 Prior literature has extensively documented temperature, humidity, and wind when characterizing the environmental
422 conditions of an urban site. The importance of documenting globe temperature, MRT, and SVF has also been
423 repeatedly addressed. However, these were reported only in 88, 66, and 34 of the 217 papers, respectively, using
424 sensor and weather station data.
425 Further, many papers rely on the RayMan model to derive MRT using globe temperature readings rather than
426 measuring MRT on the field. Hence, the understanding of long and shortwave radiation in the local micrometeorology
427 and their impact on OTC, while regarded as highly important in prior literature, remains limited in most human-
16

428 centered OTC observational studies. Measuring MRT remains costly and requires expensive sensors, which are often
429 difficult to operate (Kántor et al., 2011). As described by Krüger et al. (2014), this has led researchers to exclude MRT
430 measurements from their studies. Given the high cost of integral radiation measurement, several papers in prior
431 literature have proposed to use models such as RayMan (Matzarakis et al., 2007). MRT has also been quantified using
432 low-cost environmental monitoring devices such as the PLEMS (Krüger et al., 2024), the MaRTiny IoT device
433 (Kulkarni et al., 2022), globe thermometers (Vanos et al., 2021), cylindrical radiation thermometers (CRTs)
434 (Rykaczewski et al., 2024), and the SMART sensor (Aviv et al., 2021; Houchois et al., 2019).
435 Furthermore, prior literature has highlighted environmental monitoring considerations specific to climatic and
436 geographic conditions. For example, in coastal areas, parameters such as humidity levels are more critical due to the
437 proximity to large bodies of water. The presence of water bodies increases the moisture content in the air, leading to
438 higher humidity levels, which make individuals feel hotter and more uncomfortable, even at lower temperatures
439 (Cheng et al., 2022). In regions prone to heavy rainfall or tropical storms, precipitation intensity, duration, and
440 frequency are critical environmental parameters to monitor as they impact peak surface temperature (Ngo et al., 2022).
441 In dry/arid regions, air temperature and solar radiation change significantly from day to night and must be monitored
442 along with wind speed (Bertoldi et al., 2007).
443 In addition to monitoring environmental parameters, it is important to consider the environmental sensing strategy.
444 However, the discussion on environmental data collection during OTC observational studies explicitly tailored to local
445 climatic conditions remains underexplored. There is a lack of research specifying the parameters to be measured and
446 the optimal locations for their measurement. Moreover, whether OTC measurements should be conducted using
447 stationary or mobile instruments remains unclear. For example, 91% of the observational studies used stationary field
448 setups to monitor local environmental conditions. Using stationary stations provides the advantage that participants
449 are not required to carry environmental sensors as they navigate the urban environment, and the data can be more
450 easily locally stored and sensing instruments calibrated (Geng et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). However, stationary
451 weather stations only provide data for a limited extent, which can leave undetected thermal hotspots. Enabling data
452 collection while individuals are on the move (e.g., by the individuals carrying sensing kits within backpacks or trays
453 (Gallinelli et al., 2017)) can allow for documentation of citizen environmental stress exposure across different areas
454 of a city. Given the strong environmental gradients within short distances of a city, the local monitoring of
455 environmental parameters such as solar radiation and wind speed, which can vary significantly over short distances,
17

456 becomes critical. Mobile environmental sensing platforms have been used to develop detailed urban environmental
457 monitoring maps for air temperature, solar radiation, relative humidity, wind speed and direction, noise levels, and air
458 contaminant concentrations. Mobile sensing involves the preparation of sensing kits (usually relying on rechargeable
459 batteries) such as MaRTy (Middel et al., 2019), Coolbit (Nazarian et al., 2021) that are carried by participants using
460 vests, and wristbands (Croce et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021). While the technical challenges of developing a customary
461 mobile sensing kit and an associated monitoring campaign are non-negligible, the 9% adoption rate in the gathered
462 literature reflects the early development stage in this area of research.
463 4.2 Thermal & Sensory Surveys and Neuropsychological Assessments
464 Subjective thermal comfort has been assessed using thermal surveys in 80% of papers. Sensory surveys have been
465 used to determine subjective comfort due to sensations that include fragrance, sound, or views, and they have been
466 used in only 2% of papers. Acoustic comfort was the most used sensory survey regarding thermal comfort. However,
467 given the small number of papers using sensory surveys, further OTC studies are still needed to evaluate environmental
468 sensorial dimensions.
469 Limited literature (5% of papers) has used standardized neuropsychological assessments, with subjective
470 neuropsychological assessments used in 4% of papers and objective assessments used in 1% of papers. Subjective
471 neuropsychological assessments were used to gather individuals' self-reported perceptions and experiences in response
472 to OTC. In contrast, objective assessments were used to assess the impact of OTC on cognitive performance, memory
473 capacity, creativity & processing speed. Furthermore, no consensus exists on which neuropsychological assessments
474 or thermal and sensory surveys should be used for OTC observational studies.
475 No studies have compared subjective and objective assessments in the gathered literature search. Subjective
476 assessments have been mainly used to look into the presence of green infrastructure in the reviewed literature, while
477 little focus has been placed on differences in thermal conditions. POMS has been used to test outdoor thermal comfort
478 in comfortable and uncomfortable urban spaces. On the other hand, the questionnaire STAI has been used in prior
479 literature to look into the role of thermal comfort differences in anxiety levels. However, this research remains limited,
480 and further work is required to understand the assessments that would be most pertinent for the study of thermal
481 comfort and in which cases they should be used.
18

482 4.3 Physiological Sensing for Urban OTC Assessment
483 Through the reviewed literature, the importance of cardiovascular (measured by blood pressure, HRV, ECG, PPG,
484 SpO2, RESP), neurological (measured by EEG), and dermatological (measured by ST, EDA, SC, skin humidity)
485 parameters to document psychophysiological comfort can be highlighted (Campbell et al., 2022; Candas et al., 2005;
486 Raj et al., 2011).
487 Physiological sensing focused on cardiovascular functioning was used in 25 papers (11%). This category includes
488 blood pressure, HRV, ECG, PPG, SpO2, and RESP. Due to heat stress, the body's thermoregulatory mechanisms work
489 to dissipate heat, leading to increased blood flow to the skin (Christopoulos et al., 2019). These changes in blood flow
490 were monitored by measuring skin temperature, HRV, and PPG changes (Périard et al., 2021; Zuo et al., 2021).
491 Physiological sensing focused on neurological functioning was used in 6 papers (3%). This category uses EEG to
492 measure brain activity. EEG instruments were primarily designed for laboratory settings. However, recent
493 advancements have led to the development of mobile EEG devices suitable for outdoor use (Gaoua et al., 2018). Using
494 mobile EEG devices, researchers can gain insights into the brain-cognitive impact of heat stress in urban or natural
495 settings (Tayeb et al., 2022). EEG is highly sensitive and can measure the physiological impact of OTC on cognitive
496 performance. However, interpreting EEG band signals alongside thermal, environmental, and sensory surveys and
497 neuropsychological assessments remains challenging (Cannard et al., 2021; Lang et al., 2022; Mavros et al., 2022).
498 Prior studies agree that more OTC observational studies are required to identify the specific band signals representative
499 of thermal discomfort. And we cannot reach conclusions until more OTC studies are performed using EEG.
500 Physiological sensing focused on dermatological functioning was used in 38 papers (18%). This category includes
501 ST, EDA, SC, and skin humidity. Skin temperature was reported to be highly reactive to environmental changes
502 (Dzyuban et al., 2022b; Lau et al., 2021a). Prior literature reported that delayed physiological responses have led to
503 observational biases (Havenith et al., 2001) when comparing physiological measures, such as sweat rate, side-by-side
504 with thermal and sensory surveys and neuropsychological assessments (Candas et al., 1979; Sim et al., 2018).
505 The gathered literature agrees that physiological sensing is important for OTC assessment. However, physiological
506 sensing is still limited to 18% of observational studies. In the reviewed literature, only two studies used physiological
507 sensing on the elderly, and only one used EEG. The reviewed literature also agrees that more OTC observational
508 studies are required to identify the role of gender and age on OTC, and this is yet to be explored using physiological
509 sensing.
19

510 Budgetary and technological limitations associated with implementing physiological sensors for an OTC observational
511 study should also be acknowledged. However, physiological sensors are becoming increasingly accessible and cheaper
512 with the growing demand for sensors for monitoring personal health & lifestyle by the general population (Coulby et
513 al., 2020; Henriksen et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2023). For example, including blood pressure, heart rate, and respiratory
514 measurements through smart watches, headbands, and sensors can offer insights into cardiovascular responses to
515 thermal conditions, helping identify potential risks or discomfort related to blood pressure changes (Cannard et al.,
516 2021; Ma et al., 2023; Mansi et al., 2021).
517 These technological advancements can contribute to bridging the gap in the lack of use of physiological sensing
518 technologies in observational studies focused on urban OTC assessments.
519 4.4 Observational Study Planning, Participants, And Demographics
520 Budgetary constraints must be considered when planning observational studies, especially given the advantages of
521 engaging in bioenvironmental sensing technologies and the observational study logistic preparations, which still
522 require considerable investment. Furthermore, participant recruitment challenges must also be considered. These are
523 also important constraints that will determine the number of participants involved in the observational study and the
524 study duration.
525 In the literature gathered, most papers (96%) performed cross-sectional studies, and few papers (4%) performed
526 longitudinal studies.
527 While reaching a large sample is often aimed at cross-sectional studies, longitudinal studies generally rely on small
528 sample sizes to evaluate a specific target group over a longer period. Longitudinal studies allow for more detailed
529 assessments of a specific participant demographic based on factors that include age, gender, and other relevant
530 characteristics specific to the study.
531 Within the gathered literature, only 3% of the papers focused on the elderly population, and 1% of the studies focused
532 on the younger demographic. Additionally, only 9% of the studies were done in arid climates. As evident in the
533 Sankeymatic diagram shown in Fig. 3, there is an increasing need for more studies that target vulnerable communities,
534 including children and the elderly, across diverse climate conditions.
535 4.5 Proposed Framework for OTC Observational Study Planning
536 Planning a human-centered OTC observational study is a complex, multi-stage process requiring observational study
537 planning and defining environmental, neuropsychological, and physiological data collection practices. These are to be
20

538 determined and informed by research objectives and strategies. The observational study plan must have considerations
539 for the target population (age, gender, sample size, sampling technique, health & sociocultural background), the
540 chosen site (location, climate), the planned duration of the observational study (time choice, sampling size, study type,
541 i.e., cross-sectional, longitudinal and duration), and potential challenges that might be encountered (budget constraints,
542 lack of equipment, weather). For environmental data collection, it is essential to consider the environmental parameters
543 (air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed and direction, solar radiation, vapor pressure, precipitation, sound
544 level, MRT, globe temperature, luminance, and surface temperature), and geometric parameters (such as SVF).
545 Objective assessments (such as DSB, Stroop test, MMT, TT) and subjective assessments (such as PANAS, POMS,
546 STAI-S, PRS, EV, ROS, and SVS) should be used to evaluate the role of OTC on the mental well-being of people.
547 Thermal surveys (such as TSV, TCV, HSV, TAV, TPV, and others) and sensory surveys (such as AcSV, FSV, VSV,
548 AeSV, and others) should be used to assess participant perception of OTC. Regarding the physiological data,
549 parameters related to cardiovascular (blood pressure, HRV, ECG, PPG, SpO2, RESP), neurological (EEG), and
550 dermatological (ST, EDA, SC, skin humidity) aspects prove useful for OTC assessments (see Fig. 4).
551 4.6 Limitations of this study
552 This review analyzes the factors/parameters that go into thermal comfort assessments and indices, but it is not an
553 input-data-focused systematic literature review. Another publication focusing on thermal comfort indices will be
554 prepared in future. This literature review does not consider studies written in languages other than English. This review
555 only used PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (WoS). Other article search engines could reveal more papers, that
556 have not been explored in this study. Studies that remained unretrievable towards the end of the literature search were
557 also not considered. The literature search is limited to the period from January 1, 2001 to March 21, 2024. Publications
558 after this date were not reviewed.
559 5 Conclusions
560 A systematic review was conducted to summarize the factors and parameters found in the participant-involved OTC
561 observational studies. Using Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed databases, 8725 papers were retrieved, and
562 following the PRISMA 2021 guidelines, 217 papers were narrowed down. From the observational studies developed
563 in the selected papers, 120 were located in the Global South and 97 in the Global North. Most papers (85) were based
564 on temperate, 80 in tropical, 32 in continental, and 20 in arid climate regions. The population targeted in the
565 observational studies described in the literature ranged from 18 to 60 years (112 papers), with only six papers targeting
21

566 participants over 60 years and above, three focusing on participants from 0 to 18 years old, and 96 papers did not
567 mention the participant age.
568 Participant feedback was collected using thermal, environmental, and sensory surveys or standardized
569 neuropsychological assessments. TSV and TCV were the most used thermal surveys and HSV and AcSV were the
570 most used environmental and sensory surveys, respectively. 22 unique thermal surveys and six unique sensory surveys
571 were identified and used in 80% (174 papers) and 2% (five papers), respectively.
572 Neuropsychological assessments to assess participants’ mental state were included in 5% of the selected literature,
573 highlighting the lack of consideration for the role that participants' state plays in understanding OTC. Well-being
574 influences individual experience, with high temperatures worsening mood, increasing depression/anxiety risk, causing
575 irritability, and reducing cognitive performance. This review highlighted that a combination of subjective and
576 objective assessments can be recommended. Objective assessments, such as the DSB task, directly explain how OTC
577 impacts cognition. In contrast, subjective assessments, such as using POMS, allow for assessing the impact of thermal
578 discomfort on mood.
579 From the selected literature, 18% of papers involved physiological sensing in outdoor environments. The physiological
580 sensing aimed at documenting cardiovascular (measured by blood pressure, HRV, ECG, PPG, SpO2, RESP),
581 neurological (measured by EEG), and dermatological (measured by ST, EDA, SC, skin humidity) functioning.
582 This literature has highlighted the following research gaps.
583 i) Previous observational studies centered on psychophysiological thermal comfort assessments have often
584 overlooked the documentation of MRT and SVF despite extensive literature emphasizing the influence of short
585 and long-wave radiation on OTC. Therefore, observational studies incorporating MRT environmental sensing,
586 physiological sensing, and neuropsychological assessments must be developed.
587 ii) OTC studies conducted in temperate climates, particularly focused in China and Europe, predominate in number.
588 Consequently, there is a pressing need for increased emphasis on observational studies in arid and continental
589 climates.
590 iii) EEG and HRV biometric sensors are valuable tools for acquiring supportive data in neurological comfort
591 assessments within neuropsychology. However, their potential applicability in psychophysiological OTC
592 assessments remains unexplored. Until recently, physiological sensing technologies used in medical fields, such
593 as electroencephalogram and electrodermal activity, were impractical and off-limits for OTC assessments, given
22

594 that participants had to be tested in laboratory settings. With novel mobile EEG and multisensory wrist, head,
595 and skin sensors, physiological measures can be more easily captured in complex urban environments.
596 iv) Objective assessments such as DSB, Stroop Test, MMT, and TT, used to document mood, affect, and emotions,
597 and subjective assessments such as POMS and PANAS used to document cognitive functions and attentional
598 control, are yet to be further studied to assess OTC.
599 v) Participants involved in psychophysiological OTC observational studies focused on vulnerable communities,
600 particularly children and the elderly, are yet very limited. So are studies focusing on the population suffering
601 from mental health challenges such as anxiety disorder, PTSD, etc., for whom thermal distress can be intensified
602 and require specialized studies.
603 vi) The investigation of psychophysiological OTC in urban settings has predominantly centered on stationary
604 participants. Yet, in bustling cities, citizens are typically in constant motion. Therefore, delving into mobile
605 sensing technologies, specifically through wearable environmental and psychophysiological sensors, can
606 enhance our comprehension of OTC in connection to urban mobility choices and citizen preferences and
607 behaviors.
608 vii) Observational studies examining the psychophysiological aspects of OTC during sports activities remain
609 relatively scarce, particularly in light of the growing prevalence of soft mobility modes such as biking, skating,
610 and running in urban areas. Since individuals participating in physical activities through these modes encounter
611 distinct thermal stress, it is necessary to develop focused observational studies to comprehensively evaluate their
612 thermal experiences.
613 viii) Given the scarcity of observational study endeavors documenting neuropsychological and physiological thermal
614 comfort, the correlation between subjective discomfort reports and physiological responses warrants further
615 investigation.
