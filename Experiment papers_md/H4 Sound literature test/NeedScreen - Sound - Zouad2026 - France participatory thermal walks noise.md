Introduction
Urban heat is a silent killer (UNDRR 2025). Even when it is not fatal, it still affects our
daily lives. Yet urban heat is not simply an inevitable consequence of global climate
change. It is also shaped and intensified by local planning choices concerning built form,
green space, and car traffic. These problems are particularly marked in disadvantaged
and segregated districts, where minority communities are often concentrated. It is
expected that, in the future, communities living in the most exposed districts will face
increasing health risks (Ebi et al. 2021; Ballester et al. 2023). Urban heat is therefore both
an environmental threat and a marker of urban inequalities and institutional neglect
(Klinenberg 2015).
The social sciences have increasingly focused on the challenges of a just and sustain-
able collective life in tomorrow’s hotter cities, questioning who has the voice and power
over the design and use of hotter public spaces (Steele et al. 2023). Scholars in political
economy point out that an apparatus of economic and political powers is introducing
a range of “techno-solutionist” cooling interventions such as more greenery, textile-
shaded areas, water streams and fountains, or white-painted soils and roofs (Kaika et
al. 2023). They note that such responses are often associated with green gentrification,
which refers to the integration of speculative urban projects and climate-oriented,
redesigned areas. They highlight two associated risks. One can fear that heat-proofed
urban environments may be reserved for the more affluent part of cities. One can also
regret that the democratic energies that shape cities and communities through grass-
roots movements may be set aside in favor of a top-down coalition of business, science,
and institutions in urban climate planning. If we want to reverse this scenario, we must
start the work in the very places where the heat is most accentuated and where solu-
tions may come late. Therefore, we must think and act with and for local communities.
Participatory thermal walks may help address this risk by revealing inequalities in access
to cooling resources and involving affected residents in defining local priorities. How-
ever, participation alone cannot prevent green gentrification. It must be accompanied by
broader housing and social protection policies to ensure that climate improvements do
not contribute to the displacement of vulnerable residents.
To date, there is limited knowledge and understanding of the physiological and cogni-
tive perceptions of heat among local, disadvantaged communities. Heat planning shifts
toward climatology and the domestic air-conditioning industry, both of which utilize the
concept of thermal comfort —a concept that integrates environmental and physiological
parameters (ASHRAE 55). Planners can obtain thermal comfort maps from urban cli-
mate models that account for urban morphology, wind conditions, and solar exposure.
However, by construction, these maps fail to capture the reality and meaning of heat
in its situational and relational context. Urban planners and residents can now utilize a
tool to navigate the complexities of perceived heat. Thermal walks are gaining traction
in both academic research and professional planning practice. Drawing on urban micro-
climatology, researchers and practitioners use them to investigate how specific groups
perceive heat in particular urban settings. This disciplinary transfer raises a central ques-
tion: how can planners adapt methods developed in climatology to foster participation
and collective learning?
The first part presents an overview of heat perception and heat adaptation, introduc-
ing thermal walks through a comprehensive literature review. It highlights how urban

majority of metropolitan residents.
Part 1: Exploring and experimenting (with) heat?
Heat perception and heat adaptation
Urban heat can be defined as a spatial and temporal concept. Spatially, it relates to the
urban heat island effect (UHI), a phenomenon in which urban areas are significantly
hotter than their surrounding rural areas (Oke 1982). Temporally, urban heat includes
short-term events, such as hot days and nights, as well as longer periods, including
heatwaves and seasonally warm conditions in spring and autumn (Cheval et al. 2024).
Understanding what heat means for urban dwellers is complex because it is embodied
in each of our bodies and minds. The thermal experience is multisensory and shaped by
various stimuli, which are processed through our sensory perception (Liu et al. 2025).
Heat perception encompasses synesthetic, psychological-emotional, physiological, and
behavioral dimensions (Fig. 1). There is a permanent interplay between heat perceptions
(Fig. 1, left) and our responses to heat (Fig. 1, right). Heat perception involves multiple
dimensions that together shape the human experience of thermal conditions. The synes-
thetic dimension integrates sensory inputs from touch, sight, smell, and hearing, provid-
ing texture and context to thermal experiences. The psychological-emotional dimension
encompasses the range of affective responses to heat, from discomfort to pleasure,
which are influenced by situational factors.
Thermal perception is shaped by multiple factors, such as awareness, psychoter-
rain, exposure history, and alliesthesia. Each of these factors will be examined in turn.
Heat awareness means that we can fear the risks associated with heat, such as its health
impacts. Psychoterrain designates the mental framework shaped by continuous expo-
sure to urban heat. This psychoterrain not only informs how we interpret heat, but it also
conditions our responses. Exposure history refers to our memory of former heat experi-
ences. Past exposure shapes our expectations of future heat events. Responses to heat
involve both physiological changes and emotional reactions. Bodily responses include
sweating or changes in heart rate. They can occur in real-time, but they may also exhibit
delays or lags, adding complexity to the experience of heat (Kelly 2011). Emotional

responses include positive or negative alliesthesia, which refers to the (dis)pleasure fol-

people engage with and adapt to heat in urban environments (Rony and Alamgir 2023).
Urban heat brings discomfort in everyday city life, and we adapt at the individual,
community, and city levels. Heat adaptation has a specific meaning in climatology and
covers a range of facts. Sweating is an example of unconscious, indirect, and involuntary
body adaptation. Yet each individual can seek adaptations in mobility (such as switch-
ing to the shaded sidewalk), in work habits (schedules and location), in clothing, and
so on. We consider four main elements of heat adaptation, as outlined by Nikolopou-
lou and Steemers (2003) and Rahm (2023). However, we emphasize that various factors,
including age, gender, social, psychological, and religious aspects, can either facilitate or
impede heat adaptation. Two examples illustrate this point. Adapting clothing to tem-
perature changes in public spaces is part of both physiological and social behavior, as
cultural conditions can influence these reactions, which vary by gender and context.
Adapting depends on the individual’s actions and circumstances, and not only on their
capacity. For example, people can choose a shaded place if they are able to walk, but
waiting at an unsheltered bus station limits their options for heat adaptation.
Thermal walks, a case of disciplinary transfer
The interaction between an embodied experience and the thermo-spatial environment,
mediated through walking, forms the foundation of thermal walks. Thermal walks have
been mobilized successively in four scientific contexts since the end of the 1990s. An
evolutionary perspective of thermal walks reveals not only a variety of definitions, meth-
ods, and goals, but a transfer and relative merging of this protocol from the science of
climate toward an urban participatory dimension.
Thermal walks have emerged in climatology to understand pedestrian thermal com-
fort in outdoor environments. Thermal walks have been explored in the academic lit-
erature for more than three decades. Nagara et al. (1996) combined subjective thermal
evaluations of pedestrians with real-time physico-thermal measurements along a major
artery in Japan. The authors reported that perceptions were shaped not only by immedi-
ate conditions but also by exposure history and visual cues (e.g., the presence of a river).
This insight opened a new path, and subsequent researchers (Nikolopoulou et al. 2001;
Vasilikou and Nikolopoulou 2020; Dzyuban et al. 2022) have further explored the rela-
tionship between subjective thermal perceptions and objective measurements, consider-
ing factors such as urban design, morphology, green spaces, microclimatic variations,
and human adaptation.
In a broad sense, thermal walks now consist of organizing a collective “stop-and-go”
protocol to gather physico-thermal information from individuals, as a complement
to measurements conducted at each stop. Participants respond to questionnaires and
undergo behavioral observations or body instrumentation while microclimate measure-
ments are conducted. Throughout the 2000s and 2010s, the protocol was refined and
enriched. Additional environmental and urban design factors were included as criteria
for understanding micro-local climates, such as solar radiation and the effects of build-
ing height and volume. Climate indices that assess and forecast thermal comfort under
varying environmental conditions have been developed. The modified mPET, a more
complex model, was preferred over the original Physiological Equivalent Temperature

(PET). Yet urban microclimatologists’ focus remains unchanged: to define optimal con-
ditions for human comfort based on biological responses to the physical environment.
Later, this approach gained momentum in participatory urban planning. Since 2020,
urban planners have been interested in the transfer of thermal walks into a broad series
of mobile participatory urban walks. Sometimes called sense walking, climate walking,
or weather walks, they all belong to the emerging field of walking public pedagogies. In
this transfer from climatology to urban disciplines and from academia-led fieldwork to
urban experimentation, the purpose of the walk has been partially reframed. Urban ther-
mal walks adopt both a climate and a social perspective. The aim is not only to record
the impact of physical aspects of urban design on heated bodies and minds. For urban
planners, thermal comfort extends beyond temperature, humidity, and urban morphol-
ogy and must also account for its impact on individual perception. Thermal walks now
have their place in participatory mobile methods with a vision of democratizing climate
policies. Their broad goal is twofold. First, they examine how physiological, physical,
environmental, and social factors interact. In this sense, they are part of a toolkit that
helps planners navigate the complexities and perhaps the ambiguities of urban heat as it
is perceived and experienced locally. Second, they can serve local governments that want
to put equity into climate adaptation policies. Communities may have a say in the types
of planning and urban management responses that can be provided (e.g., more public-
space shelters, more trees, but specifying where, how, and so on).
Thermal walks
Even if climatologists and planners have different approaches to defining and fram-
ing the aims of a thermal walk, the experimental protocols developed across various
experiments span both climatology and planning. A literature review was conducted on
November 21, 2024, and two databases were searched (Google Scholar and ScienceDi-
rect). The keyword for the search was “thermal walk.” The exclusion criteria were based
on language (only English) and did not limit the year range. Parallel searches were also
conducted in Scopus and Web of Science, but they did not yield a sufficient number of
relevant studies matching our focus on thermal-walk experimental protocols. Conse-
quently, ScienceDirect was retained as the main database, complemented by backward
snowballing from the reference lists of the selected papers. Google Scholar was used
as a complementary search tool. We then conducted an in-depth analysis of 9 papers,
selected based on the distinctiveness of their experimental protocols (Table 1).

city centers with historical monuments, commercial areas, waterfronts, and
riversides, in both large and small cities.
• Thermal walks operate at different spatial scales. They can focus on individual streets
or campuses, extend across an entire district, or encompass multiple districts.

have been used in winter to investigate sensitivity to cold and during hot periods

Part 2. Conducting two thermal walks at Ivry-Port
Ivry-Port site and urban context
Ivry-sur-Seine (80,000 inhabitants) is a long-lasting communist-led municipality directly
adjacent to the southern limit of Paris. The Ivry-Port district (6500 inhabitants) corre-
sponds to a post-industrial waterfront of the Seine River. It is the poorer part of this
city. The poverty rate, defined as the percentage of the population living below the 60%
income threshold, is 42% in 2024, according to Sig.ville.gouv.fr, the French geographic
information system run by the Ministry of Cities. The neighborhood features a blend
of ruined industrial warehouses and 20th-century streets, where affordable rental hous-
ing was provided for workers in 3- to 5-story brick-and-concrete buildings. In terms
of urban greening, the municipality, which was controlled by the communist party for
almost a century, followed a traditional planning vision. The main avenues were lined
with trees, but as car traffic increased and parking demand grew, some of these trees
were partially removed. Moreover, greening was traditionally considered an amenity pri-
marily for mothers and children. Several small parks with playgrounds are located near
some primary municipal schools. Football pitches were also provided for young people.
Although some green spaces remain in school grounds, many playing surfaces have been
replaced with artificial turf to accommodate more intensive use and reduce maintenance
costs. Taken together, these features make Ivry-Port emblematic of the legacy of the
Parisian “red belt”, where an inherited but shrinking network of green spaces and public
spaces is being reconfigured amid managed gentrification, with the aim of limiting the
displacement of working-class residents while accommodating new populations.
Another feature makes Ivry-Port particularly relevant for exploring perceptions of
heat. Due to its proximity to Paris, Ivry-Port is a highly sought-after location for real
estate developers. It is undergoing a massive renovation project overseen by the still-
communist municipality. Around 8000 housing units are planned on brownfield sites
over the next decade. Currently, numerous blocks of new towers (ranging from 8 to
10 stories, sometimes 20) are inserted within the existing fabric, and some new urban
greening typologies are also being introduced. The new blocks feature generous inner
gardens, reserved exclusively for new middle-class residents. Yet, some pocket parks and
one complete street are being planned for all. All in all, Ivry-Port provides a transitional
social and urban landscape. Different population groups benefit unequally from urban
greening. The majority still reside within the inherited spatial grid, with limited access
to cooling facilities. The newcomers enjoy private green areas, and they also consider the
recent provision of green public space to be somewhat connected to their new homes.
Social tensions emerge when it turns to who can access the new, rare patches of open-air
and green public spaces, and when and for what purposes.
Experimentation design
The authors have collaborated with the Department of Citizen Science of the Ivry-sur-
Seine municipality, the Civic Center of the Ivry Port district (which is in touch with the

Zouad and Baron Urban Transformations (2026) 8:27 Page 8 of 23
poorer and more disadvantaged parts of the district), and with a community collective
that represents the interests of middle-class newcomers (Urbanicc). Two thermal walks
have been conducted, engaging a total of 26 volunteers. The walks took place on October
2nd and 19th, 2024, following 8 months of preparation, which included a dozen meet-
ings with stakeholders. We explain the conditions of their prefiguration in the following
paragraphs and tables. The thermal walks were based on creating two itineraries within
the district, with different participants in each.
The Ivry-Port walks were conceived as part of an iterative experimental process
that operates through iteration and variation, two concepts central to what Ansell and
Bartenberger (2016) call “Darwinian experimentation.” Such an approach involves
repeating and progressively refining a research procedure, while allowing certain aspects
of the experiment to vary. The underlying hypothesis is that modifying the composition
and characteristics of successive experiments enables learning through trial and error.
Each thermal walk can thus become an opportunity for constructive exploration and
learning from failure (Fuenfschilling et al. 2019).
Our experience with thermal walks predates the Ivry experimentation. It began one
year earlier in the city of Valencia, Spain, under different conditions in terms of partici-
pants and urban context. The Ivry experiment was therefore reconceptualized based on
both insights from the state of the art and lessons learned from this first field experience.
This iterative process is consistent with urban climate experimentation, in which fail-
ure can contribute to methodological refinement and adaptive governance innovation
(Bulkeley and Castán Broto 2013).
The protocol resulting from this iterative process is structured into three phases
(Fig. 2). The first phase assesses the opportunity for a thermal walk, selects the study
site, and establishes local partnerships. The second anchors the experiment in the local
context through the appropriation of the site, the co-design of a pilot walk, and the
implementation of the thermal walks. The third phase evaluates the two walks and draws
methodological lessons from them. These successive steps provide a common structure

Recruitment of participants
We recruited a diverse group of participants across gender, age, origin, educational back-
ground, and social status (Table 2). The purpose was to attract people from the poor-
est areas of the district, as well as new residents enjoying better living conditions. The
hypothesis was that the former, rooted in the inherited urban grid, suffered greater heat
exposure but may have developed adaptive behavioral responses. This group was there-
fore expected to demonstrate greater heat tolerance over time, as repeated exposure fos-
ters familiarity with heat and shapes thermal perception. The latter enjoyed better living
conditions but may have been less accustomed to the thermal conditions of this deprived
urban environment. The participation of this second group could help capture different
relationships with urban green spaces and different patterns of public space use. Various
communication channels were mobilized, including posting in local social media groups
and placing informational posters in public areas. We distributed flyers in strategic
locations, including in front of the school, near key amenities and services (such as the
library and doctors’ offices), and within parks and sports facilities. When the contacts
were created, we held an information event at the civic community center, organized
a week before each walk. During this event, we introduced climate risks and circulated
heat maps and thermal comfort models. We familiarized participants with various sci-
entific instruments, including thermal cameras, thermometers, a portable weather sta-
tion, and sound level meters, enabling them to interact directly with the equipment and
explore the sites’ thermal characteristics. However, as discussed later in the article, the
purpose of providing these instruments was not to develop a crowd science approach,
but rather to foster climate citizenship. Participants were therefore encouraged to use
the instruments freely, without being required to follow strict microclimatic measure-
ment protocols.

Questionnaires
In both thermal walks, two different surveys were used. The study employed both struc-
tured, stationary paper-based interviews and go-along interviews to engage participants
during these spontaneous conversations. The questionnaires included individual infor-
mation (such as age and gender) and behavioral aspects (such as clothing and comfort
measures). They also included questions about how people psychologically responded to
their thermal perceptions (i.e., how they perceived the space and what they would like to
do to ease the discomfort, such as sitting down, seeking shade, or drinking water).
During the first thermal walk, at each stop, the questionnaire sought to understand
how the body perceives weather elements such as wind and temperature. These sub-
jective perceptions were complemented by meteorological measurements taken by the
authors. Participants were then asked to describe their multisensory experiences, detail-
ing what they saw, heard, and smelled. Individual thermal sensations were evaluated
on a four-point scale: unpleasant, annoying, acceptable, or pleasant. Participants also
reflected on their spontaneous adaptive reflexes by considering what actions they would
take to cool down in that specific situation.
Building on the first questionnaire, the second thermal walk encouraged participants
to envision climate-proof micro-planning solutions that could enhance comfort in hot-
ter future conditions. At each stop, they were invited to position themselves across tem-
poral perspectives: past, present, and future, by recalling how they felt during the last
summer heat, describing their current sensations regarding the prevailing conditions in
the space, and imagining possible improvements to make the place more thermally com-
fortable for the future.

Quantitative results
The quantitative data from the two thermal walks show fairly consistent results, justi-
fying their combined presentation. Two main findings should be highlighted. Result 1:
From a thermal perspective, the key point is that, on one hand, the study area is entirely
within the large metropolitan heat island of the Paris region, which creates a certain
overall homogeneity. On the other hand, there are localized and even highly localized
variations in temperature and humidity. During these two autumn days, conditions were
mild and above the meteorological average. While the mean temperature in October in
Ivry is typically around 13 °C, we have recorded a local temperature range of 17 to 20 °C,
with an average of 18.6 °C and a relative humidity of 74%. Since the capital and its sur-
rounding areas are situated in a river valley, wind speeds are limited, averaging around
0.15 m/s. Air circulation significantly influences thermal perception. The cooling effect
of the Seine’s hydrographic corridor, which borders the district, was nearly impercep-
tible. This is due to the morphology, density, orientation, and height of the buildings.
Larger and taller buildings from recent redevelopment phases restrict air flow, whereas
areas still made up of former industrial wastelands or undeveloped plots allow breezes to
flow more freely.
However, measurements during the thermal walks reveal distinct micro-local ther-
mal contexts. Differences are evident between points that are very close geographically.
During the first thermal walk, a temperature contrast of 2 °C was recorded between the
Fauconnières Bloc (stop 2, itinerary 1, see Fig. 3) and the Vaillant-Couturier bus stop
(stop 3), despite their proximity of only two minutes on foot. In the second walk, the dif-
ference increased to 3.7 °C. The warmer site was located along the Seine, at the base of
recently constructed high-rise towers. Although the river environment typically exerts a
cooling influence, this site recorded the highest local temperature (22.2 °C). This anom-
aly may be related to the strong presence of heat-retaining and polluting infrastructures,
including highways, industrial zones, and a nearby cement plant with heavy truck traffic
and dust emissions (stop 3, itinerary 2). In contrast, the coolest measurement (18.7 °C)
was taken in the small Prudhon pocket park (Final stop 7, itinerary 2) within the reno-
vated district, a compact, vegetated area with dense tree and shrub cover interspersed
with some open ground.
Result 2: The neighbourhood is undergoing residential redevelopment. This process
has produced contrasting urban morphologies that may generate different thermal expe-
riences. The renovated area was expected to provide better thermal perceptions. How-
ever, the responses collected along itinerary 2 did not show a consistent improvement.
Comfort scores ranged from 2.1 to 3.2 along the first itinerary (Fig. 5a). They ranged

Qualitative results
The data collected from the two questionnaires also yielded counterintuitive results, fur-
ther questioning the variety of heat perceptions and heat-adaptation reflexes among the
participants, as well as how they problematized heat within the social and spatial dimen-
sions of the transitioning district.
First result: Heat perception is never pure, fully transparent, and easily accessible to
an individual. It is filtered by other senses and by a broader valuation of place. The first
questionnaire provided insights into a dimension that participants were not fully aware
of at the beginning of the walk, namely, the synesthetic approach that connects thermal
senses with visual and acoustic information. It is well known (Zhou et al., 2022; Pellerin
and Candas 2003; Li et al. 2025) that the landscape, in its holistic dimensions, affects
thermal comfort.
In our first questionnaire, 14 participants responded on a 4-point Likert scale regard-
ing noise perception, with none selecting the ‘pleasant’ category, indicating broadly
negative noise experiences (52% unpleasant, 37% bothering, and 11% acceptable). Noise
levels measured ranged from approximately 56 dBA at Gambetta Plaza (Stop 5, first
itinerary) to highs of 73–74 dBA during the first itinerary around the Rousseau-Lénine
avenues intersection (Stop 1, itinerary 1) and the Vaillant-Couturier bus stop (Stop 3,
itinerary 1), reflecting high acoustic noise in these areas. Sound impacts heat perception:
Boulevard Vaillant-Couturier is described as dirty and oppressively hot, although no vis-
ible litter was present: “It’s full of cars, with old neglected buildings, trash strewn about,
and pavement and asphalt everywhere you look… It feels very hot in summer—and
throughout the year too.” (Participant 1, Walk 1). Participants’ accounts reflected how
noise and heat are strongly intertwined in people’s sensory experiences. “The Vaillant
Couturier bus stop area becomes very hot due to the asphalt, and the cars exacerbate

the issue. The noise adds to the discomfort, especially during peak hours. Sometimes it
calms down a bit, and a breeze passes through since the space is large, but the degraded

Visual characteristics of public space affect spatial meanings and overall well-being.
First, our research demonstrates that people tend to prioritize visual cues over other
environmental factors. This suggests that when a place appears visually normal and
acceptable, with moderate activity levels and minimal signs of degradation, occupants
generally do not report high thermal discomfort scores or feel the need for specific heat
adaptation strategies. The Gambetta area illustrates this phenomenon well. This open
space is located within the renovation zone and is surrounded by high-rise buildings.
It is intended to accommodate a future metro station. Its openness to informal leisure
activities is characteristic of the transitory use of public space. Gambetta is also part of
urban developers’ efforts to “greenify” Ivry. The site is officially designated as the “Gam-
betta Lawn” and is intended to accommodate sports activities, but residents refer to it
as “Gambetta Beach” (Fig. 6a). Barbecues and picnics are common, and social gather-
ings are frequent. The site has thus become a symbol of conviviality among Ivry-Port’s
diverse populations. The ground consists of a barren sandy expanse with sparse spon-
taneous vegetation. Constant traffic noise comes from the busy roundabout adjacent to
the site. Consequently, Gambetta ranks among Ivry’s hottest and dirtiest locations. Yet,
against all expectations, the Gambetta area shows high positive alliesthesia scores (see
Fig. 6b). Despite these nuisances, Gambetta elicits highly positive feelings and functions
as a vibrant “third space,” as its representation as a leisure beach evokes an imaginary of
pleasant heat associated with holidays, leisure, and relaxation. Heat is felt but is not per-
ceived as a problem by residents.
Second result: Thermal walks attentive to the complexities of lived space can reveal
conflicts over access to urban resources, among which access to cool spaces is becoming
increasingly important. This became particularly apparent at one of the flagship pub-
lic spaces of the Ivry-Port redevelopment project. The thermal walk led participants to
Minoterie Square (Stop 6, Itinerary 1), a former street that the municipality transformed
into a pedestrian public space. The square is surrounded by recently constructed four-
story residential buildings occupied by newcomers. The square offers a secluded and
comfortable public space. It features a line of open water, numerous trees, manicured
gardens, benches, a children’s playground, and even a free drinking water fountain. No
doubt, participants confirmed that the spot is among the coolest; it has a great visual
appeal and offers a comfortable experience, whether for those who visit it for their own
purposes or for those who spend a relaxing break outdoors. For these reasons, Minoterie
Square is an attractive and always bustling area, especially during the day, when it is full
of people, including children and youngsters who play in the playground or play football,
as well as retired individuals who enjoy listening to the radio and music (Fig. 7a). Resi-
dents socialize in the shade and on benches not only during the day. When the weather
is nice, especially when it is hot, they also enjoy the site on weekends, late in the evening,
and at night. Participants are aware that Minoterie is the site of a tense conflict between

The findings may appear counterintuitive to climatologists, yet they reveal social and

broader reflection on the aims, substance, and implications of urban experiments that
address heat adaptation and thermal comfort in disadvantaged urban contexts.
Part 3. Urbanizing heat: the learning potential of thermal walks
This part links the core knowledge established in the first part with the account of the
experiments presented in the second. It offers an ex-post review of the case study and
further elaborates on the conditions for a successful thermal walk and the learning out-
comes it generates. Yet, success relies on the aims defined by thermal walk organizers
and the implicit meanings attached to experimentation. By introducing a ternary typol-
ogy of experimentalist practices (Ansell and Bartenberger 2016), we reveal both limita-
tions and significant opportunities for integrating thermal walks into planning processes.

Targeting and engaging diverse participants in thermal walks
Climate walks benefit from a diverse group of participants, although diversity itself
requires careful consideration. Climate-oriented thermal walks are among the types of
experiments known as controlled experiments. Controlled experimentation in the nat-
ural sciences seeks to draw valid inferences about cause and effect by isolating perti-
nent factors, treating them as variables in the phenomena being explored. Controlled
experiments aim to minimize bias (either originating from the researcher or from the
survey participants). When certain cause-and-effect mechanisms are identified and
other factors are controlled, the results are considered valid and reproducible in other
experiments.
Climatologists consider that working with a heterogeneous group enriches the experi-
ment but makes survey conduct quite difficult, whereas homogeneity helps manage
mobile groups and makes the results more reliable in terms of factor controllability. Het-
erogeneity refers to recruiting people from diverse backgrounds, with varied opinions
and thought styles. Homogeneity, when applied to urban climatology, refers to individu-
als with sufficient communication skills, a readiness to learn, and the capacity to invest
time and energy (Van de Kerkhof and Wieczorek 2005). In this approach, the social
dimensions related to class, race, living conditions, and access to urban resources tend
to become blurred. This is not a problem in itself, as climatologists are merely seeking
a variety of physiological perceptions and body-mind thermal associations. Yet it is a
crucial question for urban-planning-oriented thermal walks. Homogeneity is a trap if
it refers to easy-to-recruit and easy-to-manage participants, the kind of participatory
public that responds to recruitment campaigns. In our case, it points toward the most
affluent population, which has recently occupied the renovated part of the district. Such
individuals have developed specific ways of coping with heat. They enjoy new homes
with adequate ventilation, balconies, and communal gardens within the blocks. Such
individuals are well-informed about climate risks. And, effectively, we had no difficulty
enrolling the community collective Urbanicc, which brings together such climate-con-
scious citizens. The Thermal Walk project met the collective’s intention to raise aware-
ness about environmental problems and negotiate solutions with authorities regarding
climate and greening issues. Yet we were very aware of the need to include the district’s
more disadvantaged residents.
Large and vulnerable families can be found in degraded, overcrowded flats. This pop-
ulation struggles for survival and may not respond rapidly to such an experiment, but
suffers from a lack of fresh air and access to open, public green spaces. Hence, the par-
ticipation of this other part was essential not only to address varied heat perceptions
but also for democratic reasons. In this disadvantaged district, with support from a
citizen-science-oriented municipal office, participants’ perceptions were not simply
tested. The walk served an educational aim, fostering climate awareness and empower-
ment. The group, unfamiliar with thermal questions and lacking training to articulate
their perception of heat, initially struggled to engage. Although they possessed vernacu-
lar heat knowledge, they were unaccustomed to the terminology used in the test. Their
early responses showed a conformist tendency. Gradually, they were guided to move
beyond perplexity, uncertainty, and helplessness in response to certain questionnaire
items. They came to see sensory experience as trainable and improved their percep-
tual awareness by freely handling thermal cameras and other tools. Through the walk,

vulnerable participants gained access to climate knowledge that middle-class groups
already possessed.
Learning (more) by doing (more) thermal walks, an opportunity to root climate
experiments
To examine this learning-by-doing approach, we investigated heat perceptions and
adaptation through two thermal walks conducted in the contrasting physical and
social settings of Ivry-Port. We also engaged with local community organisations and
the municipality to explore how this experimental approach could be adapted to local
conditions.
Future thermal walks are planned across different seasons, days of the week, and times
of day, as the initial two walks took place on Saturday and Wednesday afternoons for
participants’ convenience. We also plan walks tailored for children and youth to simulate
school and leisure mobility in the district, as well as walks for active adults commut-
ing to transit hubs. Additionally, we envision thermal events substituting walking with
biking, leveraging the Greater Paris public bike network. This would allow longer itin-
eraries, including visits to the district’s southern edge, where a newly inaugurated park
remains inaccessible by other means.
Such an iterative approach operates at two levels. We can progressively refine the walk
design as we develop a better understanding of what can be learned from each thermal
walk. In this perspective, the thermal walk may be considered as an experimental niche.
The public-, spatiotemporal-, and mobility-driven variations of the event will produce a
range of best (and worst) attempts. Such a modest yet repetitive approach is key to local-
izing the thermal walk within a specific urban context, where the physical and social
idiosyncrasies of Ivry-Port’s urban heat will be delineated.
Politicizing thermal walks: realizing their disruptive potential
Thermal walks may encourage people to engage more critically with heat in their every-
day environments and serve as catalysts for social, behavioral, or political change. It
depends on the type of protocol and how it balances a descriptive perspective with a
solution-oriented approach. It also varies depending on how the thermal walk is con-
ducted. In a controlled setting, the process is largely constrained by the predefined
protocol. In contrast, a generative approach to participatory thermal experimentation
makes unexpected observations and interactions part of the process, fostering delibera-
tion and creativity.
When describing the Ivry-Port thermal walk, we discovered two counterintuitive
results. One of the warmest places was “not so warm” and not so negatively perceived
(Gambetta was even labeled by deprived inhabitants as their “beach”). The place marked
by the greatest social conflict was also the coolest, yet few participants felt at ease there.
In such cases, the value of the thermal walk went beyond documenting microclimatic
conditions and assessing participants’ perceptions. The discrepancies between the two
became a starting point for collective discussion. Together with the community’s pres-
sure on local officials, the thermal walk fostered a broader process of deliberation that
developed in several directions, including bringing the issue onto the municipality’s
agenda.

The Minoterie conflict, which emerged during the thermal walk as both a heat-related
and social issue, was part of a broader local controversy. Noise disturbance, crowding,
and conflicts between different uses of the square had also been raised by residents and
local collectives (Dubois 2024). These counterintuitive findings show how the thermal
walk can move beyond documenting thermal conditions to generate new questions and
possible explanations. Rather than confirming an expected relationship between mea-
sured heat and perceived discomfort, participants proposed explanations for the dis-
crepancies they encountered. At Minoterie, the unexpected discomfort reported in one
of the coolest places was associated by participants with noise, crowding, and conflicts
between different uses. In this sense, the reasoning was abductive: an unexpected obser-
vation led to the formulation of plausible explanations rather than simply confirming
an expected correspondence between measured heat and perceived discomfort (Hanson
1958; Douven 2025).
Such experiments may also provide a means of justification for urban authorities. It is
possible that officials allowed this project to proceed because they sought to verify and
legitimize the relevance of public-space redevelopment efforts. They may have hoped
that the thermal walk would serve, through a form of participatory scientific experi-
mentation, as proof of the quality of their planning. Yet, with sites such as Fauconnières
block, Gambetta Plaza, and Minoterie square, among others, this thermal walk opened
up a series of “black boxes”.
When the thermal walk protocol is properly designed to facilitate dialogue, it can
naturally trigger a virtuous process that generates new ways of addressing the complex
issue of perceived heat. This awareness-raising dimension is also found in Barbosa and
Klok (2020), who present the thermal walk as an experiential learning tool. As previously
shown in Table 1, their approach has a potentially politicising dimension because it con-
nects individual thermal experiences with collective reflection on the built environment.
Participants not only ask themselves questions about urban heat but also pose them to
one another. Discussions arise not only about hot and cold, but also about why certain
places are perceived as problematic or not. A thermal walk conceived within a generative
experimental perspective is thus as much a political act as a scientific one. In the socially
and racially divided urban context of Ivry-Port, it can create opportunities for dialogue
across social groups and contribute to climate preparedness. In this sense, the task is
not merely to uncover the relationship between thermal experience and the major socio-
demographic transition currently underway in this Parisian suburb, which is undergoing
managed gentrification to avoid displacing vulnerable populations. The challenge of the
generative thermal walk is to help residents, collectives, and local officials move forward
together in imagining new forms of civic practice. It thus helps create “climate citizens,”
in the sense proposed by MacGregor (2006), who act beyond individual interest and take
responsibility for their collective future.

Conclusion
The article develops an analytical and critical approach to participatory thermal experi-
ments by combining a literature review with a reflexive analysis of an experimental
protocol conducted in a deprived suburban district of Paris. The first part clarifies the
epistemological foundations of the thermal walk concept, tracing its origins through a
review of the literature and its subsequent incorporation into the social sciences. Ther-
mal walks call for approaches that go beyond individual perspectives, recognizing heat
not only as an individual experience but also as a social reality that affects communi-
ties at multiple levels. The transfer of thermal walks from climatology to urban planning
reflects a broadening of their objectives, from the observation and assessment of ther-
mal conditions to the inclusion of participation, collective learning, and action research.

Zouad and Baron Urban Transformations (2026) 8:27 Page 21 of 23
Thermal walks can now be situated within a broader family of mobile and participatory
methods. The second part presents an experiment aimed at highlighting the importance
of residents’ engagement in understanding and contributing to urban heat planning. It
produced counterintuitive results that were analyzed through both climatic and critical
urban and social perspectives and discussed during and after the walks to foster debate
and shared solutions. The third part examined the extent to which thermal walks can
enhance both collective and individual awareness of the dynamic relationships among
bodies, minds, and urban spaces. The main finding is that thermal walks, when con-
ceived as iterative and generative experiments, foster collective learning. Experimenta-
tion can, in this sense, operate as a potentially disruptive process that questions not only
the sensory and psychological dimensions of heat but also the social order and existing
climate-planning practices.
The Ivry-Port experiment also demonstrates the critical capacity of the thermal walk.
Walking through and collectively interpreting these spaces revealed a gap between offi-
cial representations of public space and residents’ situated experiences. Urban redevel-
opment increasingly associates public spaces with narratives of greening, even where
these environmental qualities remain limited or ambiguous. Such representations
accompany broader processes of urban revalorization. Residents, however, do not neces-
sarily evaluate these spaces primarily through their environmental or thermal qualities.
The case of Gambetta is particularly revealing in this respect: a space that is objectively
among the hottest and least green can nevertheless generate positive thermal percep-
tions because of its social uses, conviviality, and association with leisure. Thermal walks,
therefore, make it possible to critically examine not only whether an urban space is hot
or cool, but also who qualifies it as such, according to which criteria, and with what
implications for urban transformation.
Finally, the Ivry-Port experiment shows how thermal walks can evolve from one-off
experiments into tools for public policy. Iterative thermal walks can help build practi-
cal expertise, generate local knowledge, and support institutional learning. In Ivry-Port,
the experience gained through these experiments can inform the intermunicipal climate
plan, particularly its efforts to address heat vulnerability and adaptation in deprived
neighborhoods. The value of thermal walks, therefore, extends beyond the knowledge
produced during a single event. Over time, they can help bring residents’ experiences
into dialogue with experimental practices and local climate policy.
Under these conditions, thermal walks connect the production of knowledge with col-
lective action. They contribute to the development of climate citizens while providing
a means of questioning established categories of thermal comfort, environmental qual-
ity, and climate adaptation. Their contribution to urban experimentation lies precisely in
this dual capacity: to reveal discrepancies between planned and lived thermal environ-
ments and to turn these situated observations into resources for collective and policy
action.
