showed that walking in forests, in environments with lower road traffic noise and longer relative quiet time
without road traffic noise was associated with increased feelings of restoration, subjective stress recovery, and
perceived restorativeness, compared to walking in urban built environments with higher road traffic noise as well
as such with less relative quiet time. These associations were not observed for salivary cortisol and for attention,
though cortisol levels decreased in all conditions, likely due to walking itself. Findings from quantitative and
qualitative data on soundscape-induced positive and negative affect showed discrepancies, indicating a need for
developing and using affective measures that include low activation affect. Our results suggest that even when
noise exposure is relatively low, it can still impede subjective restoration and subjective stress recovery. This
highlights the need for noise mapping and mitigation strategies to reduce noise exposure, specifically in public
greenspaces. The results further underscore the importance of noise-free periods, without road traffic noise.
When planning, designing, and/or preserving urban environments to support the well-being of inhabitants, road
traffic noise as an impediment to subjective restoration and subjective stress recovery should be addressed.
