Abstract
Urban residents are exposed to multiple environmental stimuli. Past studies have examined the
influence of heat stress on outdoor thermal comfort, but the cross-modal effects of various sensory
stimuli on outdoor thermal comfort remain unclear. This study used chi-square tests and logistic
regression to examine the cross-modal effects of perceived environmental quality (acoustic, visual
and air quality) and psychological status (irritability, perceived tiredness) on outdoor thermal
comfort. We conducted thermal walk experiments in Guangzhou, China, in September and
October 2022. This panel study consisted of mobile environmental sensors and thermal comfort
surveys by fourteen university students on three walking routes, representing urban renewal sites,
new urban areas, and urban blue-green spaces. The ‘very bright’ (OR=15.76, p<0.001) and ‘glaring’
groups (OR=4.09, p<0.001) were likelier to feel hotter than the ‘dark’ and ‘not glaring’ groups.
People who found the noise level annoying were likelier to feel uncomfortable (OR=1.61,
p=0.004). Moreover, people perceiving air quality as ‘very good’ were less likely to find the
thermal environment unacceptable (OR=0.27, p=0.035). The irritated (OR=4.18, p<0.001) and
tired groups (OR=1.81, p=0.002) were likelier to find the thermal environment intolerable. Our
findings highlight the need to consider multisensory comfort and psychological status to inform
sustainable urban design.
Keywords: multisensory interaction, perceived environmental quality, outdoor thermal comfort,
acoustic comfort, visual comfort, perceived air quality, urban environment

1 Introduction
In assessing urban overheating risks, increasing heatwaves associated with climate change and
urban heat islands increase the risk of heat exposure for urban populations (Nazarian et al., 2022;
Tong et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). According to most Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP)
scenarios (SSP2.4-8.5), East Asia will likely experience an increase of > 2 °C by the end of the
21st century (You et al., 2022), which will result in serious health concerns (Zhang et al., 2023).
On average, globally, urban heat island warming will probably be equivalent to about half the
warming caused by climate change by the year 2050 (Huang et al., 2019). For instance, in a city
that experiences 2 °C of warming from climate change, that would mean an extra degree of
warming. Due to past warming trends, globally there were 7 heat-related excess deaths per 100,000
residents in 2000–2019 (Zhao et al., 2021). In China, the attributable deaths to heatwaves have
increased fourfold from 3679 in the 1980s to 15,500 in the 2010s (Chen et al., 2022c). The
proportion of attributable deaths to heatwaves for people with cardiovascular disease increased
from 42.7% in the 1990s to 59.4% in the 2010s in China (Chen et al., 2022c). Moreover, the death
toll caused by heatwaves in 2090 will increase under Representative Concentration Pathway
(RCP)2.6 (20,303 deaths), RCP4.5 (35,025 deaths) and RCP8.5 (72,260 deaths) (Chen et al.,
2022b). Approximately half of all heatwave-related deaths will occur in east and central China
under any scenario, encompassing developed urban areas in China (Chen et al., 2022b). This issue
is particularly urgent for vulnerable populations such as the elderly (Cai et al., 2022) and outdoor
workers (Ebi et al., 2021).
In China, urban warming accounted for about a third (0.49 °C) towards the overall warming trend
in annual mean temperature from 1961–2013 (1.44 °C) (Sun et al., 2016). With a significant
5

contribution of urban heating in cities towards overall worldwide warming (Hamdi et al., 2020),
healthy city design has been proposed to alleviate the increasing heat stress in cities (Frumkin et
al., 2020). Sustainable urban design at the neighbourhood and building scales is crucial to enhance
the outdoor thermal environment, thus keeping the city cooler and improving the comfort of urban
residents (Jay et al., 2021).
People in the outdoor environment are influenced by multisensory stimulants, which interact with
each other. Previous studies have focused on the unimodal effects of visual factors on outdoor
visual comfort (Achsani & Wonorahardjo, 2020; Compagnon & Goyette-Pernot, 2004), noise on
acoustic comfort (Kang et al., 2016) and air pollution levels on air quality perception (Pantavou et
al., 2017; Pantavou et al., 2018). Other studies have indicated a cross-modal effect of visual
comfort (Nitidara et al., 2022), acoustic comfort (Lau & Choi, 2021; Ma et al., 2021) and perceived
air quality (Cureau et al., 2022) on thermal comfort. Yet, the combined impact of multi-domain
comfort (visual, acoustic and air quality) on thermal comfort is not well understood. To investigate
the effects of personalised characteristics of heat exposure, it is necessary to use portable sensing
devices, multi-domain mobile transects (Kousis et al., 2022) and surveys to consider the interaction
between perceived environmental quality factors (visual, acoustic and air quality) and thermal
factors. Personal heat and environmental exposure could differ substantially from those at the
population level (Tuholske et al., 2021), which requires the use of portable sensors in different
urban locations (Bailey et al., 2020). In particular, personal environmental exposure differs
substantially due to differences in urban morphology (Dzyuban et al., 2022; Pigliautile & Pisello,
2020; Schnell et al., 2021), human factors (e.g., behavioural patterns, physiological and
psychological responses) (Nazarian & Lee, 2021) and personal activities (Chaix, 2018). Apart
from the interaction between multi-domain comfort, Schreuder et al. (2016) highlighted the lack
6

of empirical evidence on the interaction between multisensory comfort and emotional responses.
This study contributes to the field in the following ways:
• We develop survey questions to comprehensively assess multi-domain comfort in outdoor
settings, which can be applied in cities in various climates.
• We analyse multi-domain comfort and psychological status using mobile transects, which
provide a better understanding of the impact of multisensory interaction and psychological
factors on outdoor thermal comfort in transient settings.
Fig. 1 shows the theoretical framework of this study (Gordon & Leon, 2005; Kuras et al., 2017;
Liu et al., 2023a; Ma et al., 2021). This framework highlights the role of personal environmental
exposure and outdoor multisensory interactions as contributing factors to outdoor thermal comfort.
Moreover, we acknowledge thermal comfort as an early warning signal to prevent more serious
heat-related illnesses from developing, which has implications for public health.
In short, pedestrians are exposed to multiple stimulants in outdoor environments, but the
interaction between various outdoor environmental factors and its effect on thermal perceptions
remain unclear. Besides multisensory interaction, the effects of psychological status on outdoor
thermal comfort are not clearly understood. To address these issues, this study aimed to examine
the cross-modal effects of perceived environmental quality (visual comfort, acoustic comfort, and
air quality) and psychological status on outdoor thermal comfort. Specifically, we answer the
following research questions.
1) What is the relationship between perceived environmental quality (visual comfort, acoustic
comfort, air quality) and outdoor thermal comfort?
7

2)  What is the relationship between psychological status and outdoor thermal comfort?
3)  To what extent do visual comfort, acoustic comfort, perceived air quality and psychological
status predict people’s perception of outdoor thermal conditions?
To answer these questions, we conducted mobile transect measurements, including a mobile
meteorological station on a backpack and a concurrent outdoor environmental quality survey. This
human-centric approach quantifies the effects of personalized characteristics of environmental
exposure on human health at a finer spatial scale (Nazarian & Lee, 2021). Three locations in
Guangzhou, China, were selected to represent different landscape characteristics: Yongqingfang,
a typical old urban block; Knowledge City, a new urban development site; Haizhu Wetland Park,
a typical urban greenspace.

2 Materials and methods
2.1 The study area and regional climate
Guangzhou is located on the southeast coast of China (23°7’ N 113°15’ E) and experiences a
humid subtropical climate (Köppen Cfa) (Fig. 2). From 1981 to 2010, the mean maximum
temperature and average relative humidity in September in Guangzhou is 32.1 °C and 72%,
respectively (China Meteorological Administration, 2012). The mean September wind speed in
Guangzhou is 1.5 m/s (China Meteorological Administration, 2012). Moreover, the mean total
daily global radiation is 166.5 W/m2 for Guangzhou between 2000 and 2010 (World Radiation
Data Centre, 2019). Summer in Guangzhou is featured by high temperature and humidity
associated with the region’s monsoons.
Fig. 2 Location of the study area. (a) Map of China, (b) Location of Guangzhou, (c) Haizhu
Wetland Park, (d) Knowledge City, (e) Yongqingfang.

Guangzhou is one of the pilot cities of the World Bank’s sustainable urban cooling project in China.
In this project, we selected three sites for our pilot study: Yongqingfang, Knowledge City, and
Haizhu Wetland Park (Fig. 2). We classified our sites according to the local climate zone (LCZ),
which is an urban surface classification system considering urban built forms and land cover types
(Stewart & Oke, 2012). Yongqingfang (LCZ 3 – compact low-rise) is an urban renewal site located
about 8 km from the city centre, with historical buildings renovated in 2016. It has a dense mix of
low-rise buildings (1-3 stories) and narrow streets with limited urban vegetation. Knowledge City
(LCZ 4 – open high-rise) is a newly developed urban area built in 2010, which is about 30 km
away from the city centre. It features an open arrangement of tall buildings (mean building height
of 40 m), wide streets and scattered vegetation. Haizhu Wetland Park (LCZ A – dense trees) is an
urban wetland (1,100 hectares) about 6 km from the city centre, with abundant trees on mostly
pervious land cover, a delta city lake and a river wetland. These study locations represent old urban
blocks, new urban development sites and typical urban greenspace.
2.2 Experimental setup
2.2.1 Thermal walk experiments and survey campaigns
The thermal walk survey campaign was conducted in Knowledge City (25/9/2021), Haizhu
Wetland Park (27/9/2021), and Yongqingfang (clear day: 29/9/2021 and cloudy day: 20/10/2021).
We conducted the thermal walk on each experiment day at the following times: 09:30-11:00,
13:30-15:00, and 16:30-18:00, representing the diurnal cycle of the thermal environment. Three
walking routes were designed for Yongqingfang, Knowledge City and Haizhu Wetland Park (Fig.
3). The route at Yongqingfang passed through the Cantonese Opera Museum, a small river, arcade
streets and alleys, with 16 survey points. The route at Knowledge City consisted of a commercial
10

district, a path by a river, a street sidewalk and a science park, with 14 survey points. The route at
Haizhu Wetland Park passed through an exposed square, pathways near a lake, forest mosaic
composite wetlands, pavilions, and lawn areas, with 10 survey points. Each walking route was
about 1 km long and took about 60 minutes.
To study people’s perceptions under different environmental conditions and obtain more
comprehensive results, we chose survey points with significant changes in the surrounding
landscape, such as corners of streets and exits of alleys. Therefore, more survey points were
selected in the old urban block Yongqingfang and the new development block Knowledge City
owing to high landscape heterogeneity. In contrast, fewer survey points were chosen in the Haizhu
Wetland Park due to relatively low landscape heterogeneity.

We acknowledge heat storage from direct sunlight and physical activity during the experiments
may affect participants’ thermal sensations (Xu et al., 2021). To account for the impact of heat
storage on thermal sensation, we scheduled rest periods between the three experiments conducted
in a day. Participants rested in air-conditioned mall shops in Knowledge City and Yongqingfang,
as well as in outdoor shops with shade and parasols in Haizhu Wetland Park. Additionally, we
arranged for survey campaigns to be conducted on alternate days, allowing participants a full day
of rest after each survey campaign.
A baseline survey was given to each participant to understand their demographic background, such
as gender, age, height, weight, place of origin, and length of stay in Guangzhou. We asked the
participants to wear the same summer clothing (a light-coloured short-sleeved shirt, long pants, a
cap, and a pair of socks and shoes) to keep the clothing insulation value (clo) at 0.51. The clothing
insulation value was derived from ISO 9920 (2007) (Table S1).

At each survey point, the participants were asked to fill out the survey questions regarding their
perceived environmental quality, including the thermal, visual, acoustic, and air quality
environments (ISO 10551, 2019), as well as their psychological status (irritability and perceived
tiredness) (Lam et al., 2021b; Liu et al., 2017). At each stop, we asked the participants to stand for
14

over three minutes before responding to the survey, in order to account for the time-lag effect of
meteorological parameters on thermal sensation (Lau et al., 2019). Our supplementary material
includes the survey questions for the baseline survey and thermal walk experiment.
The survey questions on the thermal environment were designed according to ISO 10551 (2019),
including thermal sensation vote (TSV), thermal preference, thermal comfort vote (TCV), thermal
acceptance vote (TAV) and thermal tolerance vote (TTV). We extended the ASHRAE 7-point to
11-point scale to account for our participants’ exposure to the hot and humid outdoor environment
in Guangzhou. The 11-point scale was introduced in the thermal sensation index model (TSENS)
(Gagge et al., 1986; Soligo et al., 1998) and later adopted in studies in various climate zones (Chen
et al., 2018; Khatun et al., 2017; Yin et al., 2021). In particular, we added +4 (very hot) and +5
(extremely hot) to capture people’s thermal sensations in hot and humid conditions in Guangzhou,
which was shown to be valid in a previous Guangzhou study (Fang et al., 2019b). We removed -4
(very cold) and -5 (extremely cold) from our analysis as these conditions were not encountered in
this study, resulting in a 9-point scale (-3: cold to +5: extremely hot) like Fang et al. (2019b). As
Guangzhou experiences a hot and humid summer, we added a five-point scale of humidity
sensation vote (HSV) and wind sensation vote (WSV), which have been used in previous studies
(Heng & Chow, 2019; Lam et al., 2021a).
The questions for assessing the visual environment were designed based on ISO 10551 (2019).
We included a question on whether the participants were under shade to understand the impact of
shade on visual perception. Apart from the sun sensation vote (SSV), we also included a question
on the glare sensation vote (GSV), which was modified from Iwata and Tokura (1998). The
questions for assessing the acoustic environment were designed based on ISO 10551 (2019). We
15

included a question on noise annoyance vote (NAV) for the noise level. Moreover, we included a
question on the sound types (Jeon & Jo, 2020) to capture sounds perceived as pleasant or
unpleasant by people (Chen et al., 2022a). The questions for assessing outdoor air quality were
divided into two parts. The first part is about olfactory comfort (i.e., the smelliness level) perceived
by people (ISO 10551, 2019), whereas the second part concerns the perceived air dustiness vote
(ADV) and air quality vote (AQV) (Pantavou et al., 2017). Moreover, we include questions on
respondents’ irritability and perceived tiredness to indicate their psychological status during the
thermal walk experiments. Overall, these survey questions present a comprehensive approach to
understand people’s perception of outdoor environmental quality.

We chose the Physiological Equivalent Temperature (PET) as our thermal indices as it has been
used in our previous study (Deng et al., 2023) and is also widely adopted in outdoor studies
(Potchter et al., 2018, 2022). PET is defined as ‘the air temperature at which, in a typical indoor
setting, the heat budget of the human body is balanced with the same core and skin temperature as
under  the  complex  outdoor  conditions  to  be  assessed’  (Höppe,  1999,  p.71).  For  PET,  the
standardized person is featured by a metabolic rate of 80 W on top of basic metabolism, and a heat
resistance of clothing of 0.9 clo (Höppe, 1999). The PET used the Munich Energy-balance Model
for Individuals (MEMI) to present the human heat budget (Matzarakis & Mayer, 1996). We used
Rayman Pro 2.1 to calculate the PET (Matzarakis et al., 2010). The input for calculating the PET
18

was T , RH, v and T , together with a clothing insulation of 0.51 clo and metabolic rate of 125 W

2.3 Statistical analysis
We conducted a chi-square test of association to determine whether a significant association exists
between perceived outdoor environmental quality indicators (i.e., visual, acoustic, and air quality
environments) and thermal comfort indicators (TSV, TCV, TAV, and TTV). A chi-square test of
association was also conducted to determine if there was an association between psychological
status (i.e., irritability and perceived tiredness) and outdoor thermal comfort (research question 2).
We chose Cramer’s V (ϕ ) as the effect size statistics as our chi-square test tables are greater than
c
2 by 2. The ϕ indicates the association between two variables, ranging from 0 (no association) to
c
+1 (perfect association). In particular, the value of ϕ indicates a small (0.1), medium (0.3) or large
c
effect (0.5). Then, we used a Bonferroni-corrected z-test to indicate which pairs of columns for a
given row in different paired groups (perceived outdoor environmental quality
indicators/psychological status and outdoor thermal comfort) have statistically significantly
different proportions.
We used binary logistic regression (BLR) and ordinal logistic regression analyses (OLR) to
investigate which indicators of outdoor environmental quality (perceived visual comfort, acoustic
comfort, and air quality) and psychological status significantly predict our participants’ outdoor
thermal comfort. The BLR examined which indicators of perceived outdoor environmental quality
and psychological status significantly predict people’s thermal sensation and acceptance. The
dependent variable is a binary variable (TSV: -1 to +1 - slightly cool to slightly warm vs +2 to +5:
warm to extremely hot, and TAV: acceptable and unacceptable). We omitted TSV responses
19

between -3 (cold) and -2 (cool), because we wanted to compare TSV between the thermal neutral
range (TSV: -1 to +1) and people who felt warm and above (TSV: +2 to +5). The independent
variables are the perceived outdoor environmental quality indicators (e.g., SSV, GSV, NAV,
Smelliness, ADV and AQV) and psychological status (irritability and perceived tiredness). We
adopted BLR for TSV instead of OLR since the TSV model violates the proportional odds
assumption. The proportional odds assumption assumes that the odds ratio is the same regardless
of where the outcome categories are dichotomized (Kleinbaum & Klein, 2002). Violating this
assumption could cause invalid results, so we used a separate binary logistic regression (BLR)
model instead (Bender & Grouven, 1998). Moreover, reducing TSV into a smaller number of
categories has been adopted in previous research that used logistic regression (e.g., TSV: -1 to +1
and TSV > +1) (Albadra et al., 2017; Lam et al., 2021b). Furthermore, we conducted OLR analyses
to determine whether our participants’ TCV and TTV could be predicted from perceived outdoor
environmental quality indicators and psychological status. The ordinal dependent variable is TCV
and TTV (with three or more levels), whereas outdoor environmental quality indicators and
psychological status are our dependent variables. The OLR model has been used in previous
research assessing outdoor thermal environments (Ali & Patnaik, 2018; Lai et al., 2018). We
considered an alpha value of 0.05 as significant, and SPSS version 20 was used for all data analyses.

3.6 Environmental and psychological indicators’ impact on thermal comfort
We tested the multicollinearity and proportional odds assumption for our logistic regression model.
All these models’ tolerance values were above 0.1, indicating no multicollinearity. We found a
significant Chi-square statistic (p > 0.05) in the test of parallel lines in SPSS (e.g., p = 0.064 for
TCV and p = 0.628 for TTV). Therefore, our ordinal logistic regression (OLR) model also satisfies
the proportional odds assumption. Since the TSV and TAV models violate the proportional odds
assumption, we employed a binary logistic regression (BLR) model instead. As the independent
variables are all categorical, the odds ratio (OR) value means how many times more likely the
category is to result in respondents feeling hotter (or less comfortable, less acceptable, less
tolerable) than the reference category. An OR value greater than 1 means that the respondent is
more likely to feel hotter, uncomfortable, unacceptable, and intolerable with the thermal
environment.
We used binary logistic regression (BLR) to assess the effects of the indicators of outdoor
environmental quality and psychological status on TSV and TAV. The BLR model for TSV was
statistically significant, χ2(29, n = 2199) = 1149.6, p < 0.001. The model explained 54.3%
(Nagelkerke R2) of the variance in TSV and correctly classified 80.1 % of cases. The BLR model
for TAV was also statistically significant, χ2(29, n = 2352) = 871.2, p < 0.001. The model explained
50.3% (Nagelkerke R2) of the variance in TAV and correctly classified 87.6 % of cases.
Ordinal logistic regression (OLR) was used to assess the effects of the same independent variables
on TCV and TTV. Overall, the OLR model was statistically significant for TCV, χ2 (29, n = 2352)
=1707.6, p < 0.001. The model explained 56.2% (Nagelkerke R2) of the variance in TCV and
correctly classified 60.6 % of cases. The OLR model was also statistically significant for TTV, χ2
34

(29, n = 2352) =1521.9, p < 0.001. The model explained 53.1% (Nagelkerke R2) of the variance
in TTV and correctly classified 65.5 % of cases.

SSV, GSV, irritability, and tiredness were significant predictors of all thermal comfort indicators
(TSV, TCV, TAV and TTV) (Table A1 to Table A4). Gender was a significant predictor of TSV
and TCV, but not TAV and TTV. In particular, females (OR = 9.69, p < 0.001, OR = 1.42,

4 Discussion
Our results indicate a cross-modal influence of visual, acoustic and air quality perception on
thermal perception in the urban environment. We found that a higher sun sensation and glare
sensation affect people’s thermal perception. A more annoying noise perception can lead to higher
thermal discomfort. While perceived odour has limited influence on thermal perception, a better
air quality perception leads to better thermal comfort. Psychological status (e.g., higher irritability
and perceived tiredness) is also associated with higher levels of thermal discomfort. Females are
more likely to feel hotter and more uncomfortable than males because females have a higher
sensitivity to changes in environmental conditions (Chen et al., 2021) and are less tolerant to heat
(Tung et al., 2014). Our findings highlight the need to consider different indicators of outdoor
environmental quality and psychological status to assess individual climate change adaptation to
increasing urban heat.
4.1 Association between visual comfort and thermal comfort
We found that higher sun sensation led to worse thermal comfort perception (Fig. 6a-d, Fig. S1-
S2). Similarly, a Chinese study shows that an increase in visual perceptions increases thermal
sensation (Liu et al., 2023a). However, past studies from Italy (Cureau et al., 2022) and Indonesia
(Nitidara et al., 2022) indicate that people feel more comfortable when they perceive the
environment to be brighter. The discrepancy between past findings and our results could be related
to the differences in the study site, in which people might prefer brighter visual sensation in parks
compared with urban squares. The relationship between visual and thermal comfort also depends
on the level of heat stress (Geng et al., 2022; Lam et al., 2020). For example, the thermal sensation
vote is positively correlated with the sun sensation vote, and thermal perception is significantly
39

associated with sunlight preference (Lam et al., 2020). The sunlight exposure produces thermal
and visual sensations at the same time. Sunlight, which is associated with warmth and heat, could
activate the human’s hot emotional system (Xu & Labroo, 2014). The heat of sunlight is also
received by the skin (Nitidara et al., 2022). We found that the effect size of the relationship between
sun sensation vote and thermal sensation vote is greater for bright sunlight conditions than overcast
skies (Fig. S1). This finding is supported by a study in Beijing, China (Liu et al., 2023b), which
indicate that high solar radiation is associated with a high level of illuminance and causes people
to experience strong local heating. Past studies suggest that people could feel hotter at a higher
solar radiation intensity at a similar thermal index range (Lam & Hang, 2017). Light also affects
human melatonin levels. Melatonin could affect thermoregulation (Saarela & Reiter, 1994) by
changing the heat loss induction through skin temperature (Egan et al., 2005) and core body
temperature (Cajochen et al., 2005), which affects thermal sensation (Wang et al., 2007). In brief,
both visual and non-visual pathways of light could affect thermal comfort.
Apart from sun sensation, glare perception also affects people’s thermal comfort perception (Fig.
6e-h, Fig. S3-S4). This result is consistent with a study in central Italy which indicates that glare
effects could affect pedestrian comfort (Rosso et al., 2016). Glare refers to the excessive brightness
level within the field of vision (Quek et al., 2021). The source of glare in the urban environment
could be a reflection of building façade materials (Achsani & Wonorahardjo, 2020). Excessive
direct sunlight in outdoor environments would need to be addressed to enhance outdoor thermal
and visual comfort (Pan & Du, 2021). Therefore, our findings emphasize that building design
would need to avoid glare in street canyons to improve pedestrian comfort level.
40

4.2 Association between acoustic comfort and thermal comfort
When people are more annoyed with the noise in the environment, it can make people feel more
irritated and hotter than those who are not annoyed with the acoustic environment (Fig. 7). Our
findings are in good agreement with a Hong Kong study by Lau and Choi (2021), who state a
perceptually quiet environment could lead to a higher thermal tolerance. Similarly, the level of
thermal comfort reduces with a high noise level (Guan et al., 2020a; Yin et al., 2022b) and greater
dissatisfaction with the acoustic quality (Lau & Choi, 2021). Increasing sound pressure levels can
also affect the sense of irritation rating and subsequently thermal discomfort (Chen et al., 2023;
Guan et al., 2020b).
We found that the association between NAV and TSV was only significant over higher PET range
(Fig. S5). This finding is supported by a Chinese study by Guan et al. (2020a), who demonstrate a
more noticeable impact of noise on thermal comfort in a hotter environment (e.g., > 30 °C). In
warm conditions, a high noise level increases the heart rate, which increases thermal discomfort
(Guan et al., 2020a). Yet, the skin temperature and fingertip blood perfusion do not differ under
different sound environments (Guan et al., 2020b). These findings suggest that thermos-
physiology affects the relationship between the acoustic environment and thermal comfort in
various ways and warrants further study.
4.3 Association between perceived air quality and thermal comfort
We found that perceived odour has little association with TCV, TAV and TTV (Fig. 8). However,
this result contrasts with past Chinese studies that demonstrate a possible effect of perceived odour
on thermal comfort (Jia et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023a). Odour can be associated with heat or cold
41

properties relating to traditional conscious knowledge, which affects people’s heat sensation
(Wnuk et al., 2017). Moreover, odour can activate the trigeminal receptors in the human nose,
resulting in the sensation of temperature changes (Gingras-Lessard & Frasnelli, 2016). In
particular, Chang et al. (2023) suggest that increasing the fragrance comfort vote can alleviate
thermal discomfort during summer. The discrepancy between our findings and past studies might
be due to the differences in odour sources, such as green spaces on a university campus in Chang
et al. (2023) and outdoor urban environment in this study. The questions on olfactory comfort also
differ between Chang et al. (2023) (fragrance comfort vote) and our study (smelliness vote), which
could explain the differences in results.
A better air quality vote and air dustiness vote are more likely to be associated with better thermal
comfort and tolerance levels in this study (Fig. 9). Similar findings have been reported in a Hong
Kong study by Ma et al. (2021) and a Dutch study by Peng et al. (2019), which indicate links
between perceived air quality and pedestrian comfort. An increased PM2.5 concentration increased
thermal sensation and reduced thermal comfort (Yin et al., 2022a). The amount of pollutant
concentration would be related to the wind velocity in the urban street canyon, which also affects
thermal comfort indices (Li et al., 2022). Perceived air quality affects environmental perception
(Peng et al., 2021b) and the acceptability and need satisfaction of outdoor activity, subsequently
affecting pedestrians’ outdoor comfort (Peng et al., 2021a). In short, perceived outdoor air quality
would need to be considered when assessing outdoor thermal comfort.
4.4 Association between psychological status and outdoor thermal comfort
Higher irritability level is more likely to be associated with hotter sensations and worse thermal
comfort levels (Fig. 10a-d). This finding agrees with a Dutch study by Peng et al. (2021a), who
42

demonstrated that emotional state could affect thermal comfort in urban public space. Similarly,
psychological cognition, like the sense of pleasure, communication, and satisfaction, positively
affects thermal comfort (Liu et al., 2023a). Positive emotion can also decrease as thermal sensation
increases (Niu et al., 2023). Increasing negative emotion can increase thermal sensation vote,
which varies with different ranges of PET (Zhang et al., 2021). Furthermore, higher ambient
temperature can increase negative emotions and tiredness (Noelke et al., 2016). A Chinese indoor
study has also found that people tend to feel warmer in a boring state compared with a neutral and
joyful state when they are sitting or standing, as blood pressure and heart rate increase during a
boring state (Wang & Liu, 2020). However, they found that the participants’ emotional state had
no significant effect on thermal comfort during exercise. Although our results differ slightly from
those of Wang and Liu (2020), the difference is possibly due to less extreme positive or negative
emotions experienced in the indoor study.
A higher perceived tiredness level is associated with worse thermal comfort perceptions (Fig. 10e-
h). This finding confirms another study in Guangzhou, China, which shows perceived tiredness as
a predictor of outdoor thermal comfort during exercise (Lam et al., 2021b). Perceived tiredness is
related to the perception of effort in exercise tolerance in the psychobiological model (Smirmaul
et al., 2013). When people feel tired, it could reduce their ability to cope with heat stress, thus
causing them to feel hotter and more uncomfortable in a hot environment. In short, psychological
adaptation (such as emotional status and perceived tiredness) plays a major role in how people
cope with heat stress.

4.5 Implications for urban planning and public health policies
Most of the current urban planning practice focuses on one aspect of the outdoor environmental
quality. However, our results suggest that a multi-domain analysis considering thermal, visual,
acoustic and air quality could provide a deeper insight into the impact of urban design on human
comfort. What stands out in this work is that multiple environmental quality variables would affect
pedestrian comfort, which leads to some practical advice on incorporating multi-domain comfort
assessment in sustainable urban planning.
First, urban planners would need to consider the effects of urban vegetation on different outdoor
environmental quality factors. Our findings indicate that the shortcomings in one environmental
factor (e.g., thermal) can be offset by improving other factors (e.g., visual), which is in agreement
with Nitidara et al. (2022). Urban vegetation not only mitigates urban heat (Kondo et al., 2021;
Shooshtarian et al., 2018) and promotes walkability (Xiong & He, 2024), but it also increases a
location’s visual and aesthetic appeal and lowers people’s thermal sensation (Lau & Choi, 2021).
Moreover, pedestrian comfort levels are influenced by perceived air quality (Ma et al., 2021; Peng
et al., 2019), which can be related to the amount of urban vegetation. The air quality in parks is
perceived to be better than the surrounding built environment (Rosso et al., 2022), which is
supported by a higher particulate matter concentration in roads than urban green space (Wang et
al., 2022). The dust retention effects of plants and the negative air ions released by vegetation can
remove particulate matter and improve air freshness (Jiang et al., 2021). However, excessive
vegetation in high-density urban environments could reduce ventilation and increase the
concentration of air pollutants (Fang et al., 2021). The effect of vegetation on air quality depends
on the species and air pollution level (Janhäll, 2015). Furthermore, street trees could affect both
44

the thermal environment and air quality depending on the planting density and leaf area index (Li
et al., 2023). Briefly, our findings suggest that urban greenery management needs to consider the
trade-off between various outdoor environmental quality factors, including thermal comfort, visual
comfort and air quality.
Second, urban planners should promote natural sound types in cities to improve acoustic comfort,
which subsequently affects outdoor thermal comfort (Du et al., 2023). However, the effects of
acoustic perception on thermal comfort differ for various sound types (Chen et al., 2022a).
Acoustic comfort can be related to sound pressure level and spectrum (manifested in different
sound types) (Cureau et al., 2022; Du et al., 2023). For example, traffic noise can lower thermal
comfort in summer (Jin et al., 2020a; Nitidara et al., 2022). In contrast, certain natural sound types
(e.g., birdsong, water, background music) are perceived to be favourable by humans and even
positively influence thermal comfort (Chen et al., 2022a; Geng et al., 2022; Guan et al., 2020b).
Water sounds from fountains can also mask the road traffic noise (Axelsson et al., 2014) and
increase people’s comfort level. As birdsong and music have more middle- and high-frequency
components and a higher variety variability in sound, people appear to have a higher acceptance
of these sounds (Jin et al., 2020b). Moreover, positive sound sources improve the overall ratings
on comfort, irritation and satisfaction (Chen et al., 2023), as well as emotional state (Ito et al.,
2023). Depending on the sound type, people can be more tolerant of a higher sound pressure if
they find it pleasant. As such, urban planner should introduce more natural sound in the urban
landscape to improve acoustic comfort, which can reduce thermal discomfort.
Third, urban planners would need to recognise the influence of the audio-visual context of a
location on the emotional state of outdoor users. In particular, the congruency between the audio
45

and visual components of a location would enhance the pleasantness of natural sounds (Hong et
al., 2020). Interaction with nature-based solutions also benefits mental health (Sharifi et al., 2021).
Urban planners should promote natural elements in the urban landscape, such as natural sound and
openness, to foster psychological restoration and improve mental well-being (Jeon et al., 2023;
Yin et al., 2023). Images and sound in the natural environment can also induce physiological
comfort and subjective restorativeness (Li et al., 2021). Furthermore, the audio-visual interaction
relating to urban vegetation can positively affect people’s emotional state, reducing anxiety and
tiredness (Elsadek et al., 2019). Moreover, more positive emotion (e.g., cheerful, relaxed) is
reported in the waterscape than in the urban built environment (Yan et al., 2023), which is
associated with more negative emotion (e.g., anxiety, tiredness) (Zhang et al., 2019). The lower
anxiety and perceived stress level in peri-urban forests is associated with a lower thermal sensation
vote (Gillerot et al., 2024), further supporting our findings. In brief, urban planners should consider
multisensory interaction, environmental perception and users’ psychology status in managing
street greenery and waterbody, which enhance the overall pedestrian comfort.
Apart from informing urban planning practices, our findings have implications for designing
personalized early warning systems for vulnerable urban populations. There has been a shift of
focus from a population-level heat-health warning system to a personalized heat-health exposure
assessment (Nazarian & Lee, 2021) and warning system (Kuras et al., 2017; Morris et al., 2021).
Past heat-health studies highlight the heterogeneous characteristics of the urban population
(Forceville et al., 2024; Harlan et al., 2013; Tuholske et al., 2021). Moreover, people’s age,
education level and income affect their heat adaptation (He et al., 2022). Hence, it is necessary to
provide targeted warning information to suit individual needs (Jay et al., 2021). The World Health
Organization sees thermal comfort as a link to protect vulnerable groups from extreme
46

temperatures (Ormandy & Ezratty, 2012). Thermal comfort can be seen as an early warning signal
to prevent the development of more serious heat-related illnesses (Lam et al., 2021a). This study
underscores the importance of considering the multisensory influence on thermal comfort, thus
providing a holistic evaluation of people’s adaptation to heat stress and informing adaptation
measures to produce health co-benefits (Sharifi et al., 2021). Smartphone apps like the ClimApp
include weather variables and user input to predict individual thermal stress (Eggeling et al., 2022;
Folkerts et al., 2021). Our study emphasises the need to incorporate other environmental variables
(e.g., illuminance, noise and air quality) (Matzarakis, 2020) in such smartphone apps. Developing
the smartphone app and personalized warning system would require input from citizen-based
monitoring projects on different environmental variables, such as air temperature (Lam et al.,
2021c), light intensity (Cabrera et al., 2021), noise (Longo et al., 2020), and air quality (Mahajan
et al., 2022). Feedback from end-users would also improve the app design and functions (Neset et
al., 2021). The multisensory interaction in the outdoor environment is key to improving people’s
thermal adaptation in light of climate change. Thus, the present findings indicate that a
comprehensive early warning system requires not only heat exposure but also exposure to other
environmental quality variables, which would increase the resilience of the urban population to
urban heat.
4.6 Strengths and limitations of this study
The study contributes to the field in two ways. First, this study has highlighted the importance of
the cross-modal effect of visual comfort, acoustic comfort and perceived air quality on outdoor
thermal comfort. Our method of thermal walk and multi-domain comfort surveys could be applied
to similar studies in other climate zones. The value of our work lies in informing appropriate urban
47

design interventions to reduce undesirable exposure to environmental variables. The shortcomings
in one environmental factor (e.g., noise) can be counteracted by improving other factors (e.g.,
visual). Therefore, climate-resilient urban design that considers multisensory interaction is critical
to improving the resilience of urban populations to future climate change. Second, our findings
emphasise the need for a paradigm shift from considering only heat stress to incorporating
multisensory comfort and psychological status in early warning systems. These heat-health
warning systems would need to consider other forms of environmental exposure, such as
undesirable levels of light and glare, noise and air pollution. Our data suggest that self-report
thermal discomfort (considering multisensory interaction and psychological status) can be an early
warning signal to prevent the development of more serious heat-related illnesses. In particular,
these personalised heat exposure data provide higher resolution information for developing an
effective early warning system tailored towards different individuals.
This study has several limitations. First, the participants of this study are university students, who
are mainly young and healthy adults. The environmental perception of other vulnerable groups,
such as the elderly, would likely differ (Baquero Larriva & Higueras, 2020). Therefore, it would
be worthwhile to include other populations in future studies to compare the impact of age groups
on multisensory interaction and comfort perception. Second, due to the limitation in recruiting
participants, we had one participant from the Gansu Province. Future studies could ensure that all
participants come from the same geographical region. Third, this study mainly concerns the
subjective perception of environmental quality in outdoor environments. We acknowledge that
physiological parameters could affect people’s comfort during exercise (Lam et al., 2021b). As
this study focuses on multisensory interaction, it is beyond the scope of this study to examine the
impact of physiological variables on outdoor thermal comfort. Fourth, we mainly consider
48

irritability and perceived tiredness to represent participants’ emotional status. We recognise that
other psychological adaptations and emotions can affect people’s comfort perception and are worth
investigating further.
5 Conclusions
This study examined the effects of visual comfort, acoustic comfort, perceived air quality and
psychological status on outdoor thermal comfort. We used mobile transect measurements and
thermal walks in three study sites in Guangzhou, China, as a case study. The following conclusions
are drawn.
1) Sun sensation vote and glare sensation vote were significant predictors of thermal comfort
indicators. The ‘very bright’ (OR = 15.76–48.28) and ‘glaring and very glaring’ groups
(OR = 4.09–6.81) were more likely to feel hotter, more uncomfortable, unacceptable and
intolerable than the ‘dark’ and ‘not glaring’ groups.
2) The noise annoyance vote was a significant predictor of thermal comfort indicators, except
for the thermal acceptance vote. People who found the noise level ‘annoying and very
annoying’ (OR = 1.51–1.81) were likelier to feel hotter, more uncomfortable and
intolerable than those who were not annoyed.
3) The air quality vote was a significant predictor of outdoor thermal comfort. People who
perceived the air quality as ‘very good’ were likelier to feel hotter (OR = 3.92), acceptable
(OR = 0.27) and tolerable (OR = 0.29) than the ‘bad and very bad’ group. However, odour
perception (smelliness) and perceived air quality indicators did not significantly predict
TCV.
49

4) Our results demonstrate the impact of psychological status on adapting to the thermal
environment. The ‘irritated and very irritated’ (OR = 2.30–8.09) and ‘slightly tired’ groups
(OR = 1.29–1.61) were more likely to feel hotter, uncomfortable, unacceptable and
intolerable than the ‘not irritated’ and ‘not tired’ groups.
In terms of future research directions, studies can conduct physiological measurements like heart
rate and skin temperature (Nazarian et al., 2021) and compare the physiological data with
subjective comfort perception under different urban environments. Moreover, future studies can
incorporate other aspects like electroencephalogram (Ma et al., 2023) on emotional and brainwave
responses. It is also worthwhile to analyse the impact of emotion on comfort perception (Gillerot
et al., 2024) using the Profile of Mood States (POMS) (McNair et al., 1992) or Positive and
Negative Affective Schedule (PANAS) (Watson et al., 1988), which provides more comprehensive
information on people’s emotional status. Overall, the significance of this study lies in informing
climate-resilient urban design and heat-health warning systems that incorporate multisensory
interaction and people’s psychological status.
