## Summary & Abstract
Exposure to road traffic noise, fine particulate matter (aerodynamic diameter $\le 2.5\ \mu\text{m}$; $\text{PM}_{2.5}$), and nitrogen oxides ($\text{NO}_x$) has been associated with transient changes in blood pressure, but whether an interaction exists remains unclear. This panel study investigated whether noise, $\text{PM}_{2.5}$, and $\text{NO}_x$ exposure were independently associated with changes in 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure.

We recruited 33 males and 33 females aged 18–32 years as study subjects. Personal noise exposure and ambulatory blood pressure were monitored simultaneously. Data on $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ and $\text{NO}_x$ from air-quality monitors within 6 km of participants' addresses were used to estimate individual exposures. Linear mixed-effects regression models (LMM) were used to estimate single and combined effects on ambulatory blood pressure.

Exposure to both noise and $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ was significantly associated with increased systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) over 24 h; $\text{NO}_x$ exposure was only significantly related to elevated DBP. Twenty-four-hour ambulatory blood pressure increased with current noise exposure of 5 A-weighted decibels (dBA) (SBP 1.44 [95% CI: 1.16, 1.71] mmHg and DBP 1.40 [1.18, 1.61] mmHg) and $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ exposure of $10\ \mu\text{g/m}^3$ (SBP 0.81 [0.19, 1.43] mmHg and DBP 0.63 [0.17, 1.10] mmHg), as well as current $\text{NO}_x$ exposure of 10 ppb (DBP 0.54 [0.12, 0.97] mmHg) after simultaneous adjustment. These findings suggest that exposure to noise and air pollutants may independently increase ambulatory blood pressure and cardiovascular risk.
