# Apparent wind and the interpretation of wearable sound measurements during walking and cycling in Brussels

Mobile sensing can resolve changes in environmental exposure that fixed stations miss, but movement also changes the conditions under which a wearable microphone records. During cycling, airflow at the instrument may contribute to recorded sound or coincide with changes in the surrounding soundscape. Both mechanisms complicate comparisons between routes and modes when exposure is represented by a single A-weighted level. Previous field studies have combined pedestrian comfort ratings across thermal and acoustic domains, and mobile noise studies have demonstrated the importance of microphone placement and wind protection [1–3]. We examine how simultaneous measurements of apparent wind and acoustic spectra inform interpretation of route contrasts and comfort associations in a repeated-participant walking and cycling campaign.

Eleven adults completed matched eight-minute bouts of walking and cycling on a green park corridor and an impervious street corridor in Brussels during summer 2025. The four bouts per participant provided 44 participant–route–mode observations. Acoustic and micrometeorological measurements were synchronized at ten-second resolution. Acoustic descriptors included A-weighted sound level, octave-band energy proportions, spectral centroid and automated sound-source proportions. The environmental measurements included apparent wind and inputs to the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI). Participants rated comfort after each bout. The two measurement scales address different questions. The ten-second series describe changes in recorded conditions during movement, while the post-bout ratings provide one report of experience for each route and mode.

We estimated acoustic and environmental associations separately for each route–mode combination with participant random intercepts and first-order temporal autocorrelation. The acoustic analysis screened 23 descriptors, including broadband level and measures of spectral distribution and source composition. False discovery rate was controlled across estimable tests within each route–mode and outcome family. The post-bout sound–comfort analysis used 22 walking bouts and examined whether the association between A-weighted level and thermal comfort persisted after accounting for route. As an exploratory sensitivity analysis, we estimated separate apparent-wind slopes for each participant and phase with GPS travel speed included as a covariate. These complementary analyses address whether acoustic measurements track airflow during movement and whether the sound–comfort association is distinguishable from the contrast between the two corridors.

The urban corridor was 4.2 dB(A) louder than the green corridor during walking and 9.2 dB(A) louder during cycling. The corresponding walking and cycling UTCI contrasts were 7.3 °C and 2.0 °C, respectively. Thus, the mode with the larger acoustic contrast had the smaller thermal contrast. In the ten-second analysis, 22 acoustic–environmental associations passed the false-discovery threshold, all during cycling. Seventeen involved apparent wind speed. On both cycling routes, higher apparent wind coincided with higher A-weighted sound levels and lower spectral centroid. Greater low-frequency energy share also accompanied higher wind, in the 20–200 Hz band on the green route and in the 31.5 Hz octave on the urban route. The consistency of the broadband and centroid directions across the two cycling routes provides a specific pattern for evaluating the recordings. No physiological or questionnaire association passed correction in the expanded acoustic screen. In the speed-adjusted sensitivity analysis, the median participant slope for A-weighted level was +2.18 dB per m/s of apparent wind on the green cycling route and +2.40 dB per m/s on the urban cycling route. The slope was positive for nine and ten of the eleven participants, respectively. These descriptive estimates summarize participant-level consistency.

In the walking bouts, higher A-weighted sound levels were associated with lower thermal comfort in the initial mixed model (nominal p = 0.0037). The association weakened when route was included (p = 0.47). Each corridor combined multiple changes in exposure, so the unadjusted association offers limited evidence about sound independently of route. The wind associations likewise allow more than one explanation. The campaign used a windscreen, and the recordings alone cannot distinguish airflow-induced microphone noise from audible wind or changes in traffic and other environmental sources. The results therefore identify wind-sensitive measurements under these field conditions and motivate a direct validation against an independent acoustic reference.

The practical implication for active-mobility monitoring is to report apparent wind and spectral descriptors alongside broadband level, especially when comparing cycling with walking. A corridor comparison based on dB(A) alone can obscure whether higher recorded levels coincide with a redistribution of spectral energy under stronger airflow. The Brussels campaign illustrates how synchronized measurements can flag that ambiguity before acoustic exposure is related to comfort. Future campaigns can test the measurement mechanism using a stationary reference, paired microphone configurations and repeated runs across a range of wind and travel speeds.
